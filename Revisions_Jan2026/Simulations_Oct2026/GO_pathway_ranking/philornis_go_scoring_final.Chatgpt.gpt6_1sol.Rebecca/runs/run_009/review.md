# A priori physiological relevance review

Scoring used only the supplied GO terms, the ecological/physiological literature below, and general biological knowledge. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or epigenetic results were used. Such papers appeared incidentally among search results and were excluded as evidence. Terms were judged individually without a target score distribution.

## Host damage and candidate responses

Early Philornis downsi larvae occupy nestling nostrils and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Blood and tissue consumption produces anemia, wounds, nasal damage, impaired growth, and mortality. These observations directly motivate blood-cell replacement and strongly predict iron regulation, oxygen-stress compensation, hemostasis, epithelial repair, vascular repair, and maintenance of tissue barriers. The latter mechanisms are biological predictions rather than demonstrations that every associated pathway responds to this fly. Nasal damage can also alter adult song, making sensory/behavioral consequences plausible, while organ-specific embryonic patterning has little specific relevance.

Resistance and tolerance were distinguished: resistance lowers parasite burden, whereas tolerance limits the fitness cost at a given burden. Adult female medium ground finches experimentally increased fly-binding antibodies following exposure; stronger maternal responses were associated with fewer parasites, but did not rescue brood survival. Males and nestlings did not show that induced response in the same experiment. Thus adaptive immunity is directly grounded; its lymphocyte components are strong predictions. Innate recognition, inflammatory regulation, phagocytosis, and cytokine signaling are credible defenses to feeding injury and possible wound contamination, but are not all demonstrated anti-Philornis mechanisms. Virus-specific processes receive little weight because this is a tissue-feeding ectoparasite, not a demonstrated viral challenge.

Mockingbird nestlings can increase begging and receive more parental food, providing a documented behavioral tolerance mechanism. This compensation varies with environmental resources and is weaker in dry years. Comparable compensation is not universal in Darwin's finches; green warbler-finch females reduced provisioning under heavier infestation. Nutrient sensing, glucose and energy homeostasis, catabolism, and growth regulation are therefore relevant supporting physiology, without assuming a particular metabolic pathway has been experimentally established. Nocturnal disturbance and changes in maternal brooding also provide plausible links to circadian regulation and thermal stress.

Behavioral resistance includes occasional larval removal/preening, avoidance, and potentially application of plant repellents. Finch feather-anointing with Psidium galapageium is observed, and laboratory assays support repellency, but protection of wild birds from P. downsi remains a hypothesis. Taking insecticide-treated cotton into nests can reduce infestation experimentally; this demonstrates a usable nesting behavior, not natural synthesis of insecticide. Learning, sensory detection, and motor pathways are supporting or indirect processes rather than equally direct defenses.

Generic transcription, RNA processing, protein trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, and cell-cycle machinery were not elevated merely because they are necessary for living cells. Some have plausible roles in repair or compensation; specialized gametogenesis, chromosome mechanics, and unrelated embryonic structures generally have no specific link. The input entry GO:0160240 supplies no interpretable term name; a focused public lookup did not resolve it, so it received 1 rather than an invented function.
