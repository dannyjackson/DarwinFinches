# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

The ratings use published parasite ecology, experimental host physiology and behavior, and general biological knowledge. No host genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results were used. Each supplied term was considered independently; scores concern potential association, not demonstrated adaptive evolution, and no score distribution was targeted.

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood removal and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nostrils and other tissues; later larvae largely inhabit nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Blood loss, anemia, reduced hemoglobin, nasal damage, poor growth, and nestling mortality are established consequences. Increased immature red blood cells with greater infestation supports compensatory erythrocyte production. Blood-cell replacement therefore receives especially high relevance. Iron handling, oxygen-stress responses, coagulation, vascular repair, epithelial maintenance, and wound repair are strong mechanistic predictions, although their molecular details have not generally been demonstrated in these birds. Tissue and mucosal disruption also provides a plausible secondary-infection route; antibacterial defenses are less direct than defenses against the fly itself.

- Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's Small Ground Finch ...*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host–parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/oryx/article/video-analysis-of-hostparasite-interactions-in-nests-of-darwins-finches/BF956C9751763DAFAEE36345498A4A01

**Immune resistance.** Experimental fly removal demonstrated higher fly-binding antibody levels in exposed medium-ground-finch mothers; corresponding responses were not demonstrated in fathers or nestlings. The antibody–parasite association was suggestive rather than proof of effective protection, and maternal responses did not rescue reproduction in that experiment. Adaptive immunity and its B-cell machinery are consequently strongly relevant. Innate recognition, inflammation, cytokine communication, immune-cell recruitment, and their regulation are plausible resistance or damage-control mechanisms, but specific pathways should not be described as proven fly defenses. An absent response in a particular age, species, or experiment does not make that mechanism irrelevant a priori.

- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

**Tolerance and energy compensation.** In experimental comparisons, parasitized Galápagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging and parents increased provisioning; reproductive performance was maintained despite parasite exposure. Medium ground finches lacked comparable compensation. Across years, mockingbird tolerance declined with scarce rainfall/resources, linking tolerance to energetic capacity. Green warbler-finches did not compensate through increased total provisioning; highly infested nests had reduced nestling mass and lower female provisioning. Nutrient availability, glucose and lipid use, energetic homeostasis, growth, and allocation between repair and reproduction therefore have meaningful relevance, with generic metabolic processes generally rated below directly demonstrated organismal mechanisms.

- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/
- *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth under Philornis downsi parasitism in the Galapagos Green Warbler-Finch* (2023): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583/

**Behavioral resistance and exposure.** Recorded defenses include occasional larva removal or consumption, preening, scratching, and avoidance movements. Their efficacy varies and is not established by observation alone. Changes in brooding and posture can reduce contact, while nest placement, breeding timing, nest sanitation, and potential use of repellent materials are proposed exposure-reduction mechanisms. Provision of insecticide-treated cotton demonstrates nest-material collection as an assisted control route, rather than natural insecticide production. Sensory, motor, circadian, learning, and hormonal processes can support such behavior but most individual neural-development or synaptic terms have only indirect relevance.

- Pike et al. (2021), *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests in the Galapagos Islands*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982214003509
- McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi, an Introduced Parasite of Galápagos Birds*: https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103

## Interpretation of ratings

5 marks direct overlap with documented host responses, especially immune response, erythrocyte replacement, injury response, and energetic compensation. 4 marks core machinery or strong predictions such as B-cell function, inflammatory defense, hemostasis, epithelial repair, and hypoxia responses. 3 marks plausible supporting physiology, including nutrient processing, oxidative-stress control, repair signaling, and selected behavioral support. 2 marks weak or nonspecific links, including much general transport, cellular organization, and signaling. 1 marks processes without a specific credible link, especially unrelated embryonic patterning, gametogenesis, specialized neural development, and many generic nucleic-acid maintenance processes. Related processes can differ because their specificity and causal proximity differ. Positive versus negative regulation does not by itself establish beneficial versus harmful effects.

Avian thrombocytes provide the relevant functional analogy for platelet-labeled hemostasis terms; these scores do not assert that birds have mammalian platelets. Tissue damage does not establish every molecular repair pathway, and anemia does not establish a measured hypoxia or oxidative-stress response. These distinctions separate strong predictions from direct evidence.
