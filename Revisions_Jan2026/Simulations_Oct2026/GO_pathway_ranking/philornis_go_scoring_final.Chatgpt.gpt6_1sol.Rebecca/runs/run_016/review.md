# Literature basis and scoring rationale

The scores assess potential physiological association with the fly, not evidence of selection, proven adaptation, or whether increasing a process would benefit a bird. I used the supplied term list, published non-genomic host–parasite literature, and general biological knowledge. Gene-expression, transcriptomic, and genomic results were excluded, including an urban-finch expression study encountered during searching. No other local files were read.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood and tissue damage.** Early larvae occupy nostrils and other tissues; later larvae hide in nest material and feed externally on nestling blood. These injuries motivate hemostasis, epithelial protection and repair, extracellular-matrix repair, vascular repair, and sensory responses to biting. Most of these specific cellular mechanisms are strong predictions rather than experimentally demonstrated defenses. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089
- **Anemia and compensatory blood production.** Parasite intensity was associated with lower hemoglobin and more immature red blood cells in small ground finch nestlings. Erythrocyte differentiation therefore directly fits a measured host response. Hematopoietic progenitors, iron homeostasis, oxygen limitation, circulatory compensation, and energy provision support strong or plausible predictions; their exact molecular pathways were not established by this study. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- **Immune resistance and its limits.** Experimental parasite manipulation increased fly-binding antibodies in mother finches, but not fathers or nestlings; apparent maternal defense did not prevent reproductive failure. The paper proposes inflammation and ingested defensive host products as possible anti-feeding mechanisms. Accordingly adaptive immunity is directly supported; B-cell mechanisms and innate/inflammatory pathways are strong predictions, while specific cytokines, complement, and pathogen-recognition pathways should not be mistaken for fly-specific observations. Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651 ; accessible article: https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/118.pdf
- **Behavioral resistance.** Preening, probing nestlings' nares, and larva removal have been observed but are limited and do not establish effective protection. Sensory processing, movement, and learning can support such behavior, whereas generic synaptic or neuronal-development processes have only indirect links. Pike et al. (2021), *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests*: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10905-021-09789-7 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- **Tolerance through food compensation.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with increased parental feeding and tolerance; comparable compensation was not found in medium ground finches. This supports energy and nutrient homeostasis, metabolic support of growth, and behavioral food solicitation as potential tolerance mechanisms without assuming they already operate effectively in finches. Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin’s finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- **Environmental dependence.** A four-season mockingbird experiment found that tolerance declined in low-rainfall years, with parental provisioning implicated. This strengthens resource-allocation and nutritional-support hypotheses, while leaving individual metabolic pathways as predictions. McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2019.0049 ; https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/Bush66.pdf
- **Endocrine caution.** An experiment found no parasite-treatment difference in baseline or stress-induced corticosterone in breeding female finches over the sampling interval. Stress and steroid signaling remain possible contextual mechanisms, not demonstrated general explanations. Knutie et al. (2013), *Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin’s finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/

## Application to terms

Direct immune and erythrocyte responses receive 5. Core immune regulation, wound responses, coagulation and hypoxia responses generally receive 4 as strong physiological predictions. Supporting tissue repair, energy metabolism, redox balance, nutrient handling and selected signaling receive 3. Generic intracellular transport, housekeeping, ion handling and most neural machinery receive 2 when the connection requires several unsupported steps. Unrelated reproduction, specific embryonic anatomy, and much basic informational machinery receive 1. Positive and negative regulation are judged for association with a mechanism, not assumed adaptive direction. Mammalian platelet terminology is treated as a prediction about avian thrombocyte/hemostatic function, not evidence that birds have mammalian platelets. Virus-specific terms do not establish fly-mediated viral infection.

The input supplies GO:0160240 without a descriptive name. Searches did not resolve its meaning; its score of 1 denotes no identifiable specific link from the supplied annotation, not a verified biological absence of relevance. The ID is retained exactly.
