# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi host-response mechanisms

The scoring is based on non-omic studies of the parasite, host physiology and behavior, plus general biological knowledge. No transcriptomic, gene-expression, genomic-selection results or other raters' files were used. Each of the 1,008 supplied GO IDs is retained in the original order. Scores express physiological relevance, not evidence that the process is effective protection or actually under selection.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood loss and tissue damage.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, particularly at night. Feeding causes substantial blood loss, anemia, wounds and nasal deformation, with reduced growth and survival. These findings make erythrocyte replacement, iron availability, oxygen compensation, clotting and epithelial repair especially strong prior candidates. Erythrocyte differentiation is particularly close to the reported compensatory increase in immature red cells; clotting and repair are mechanistic predictions rather than demonstrations of effective resistance. Sources: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x ; Koop et al. (2011), experimental parasite reduction and nestling fitness, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3092749/ .

- **Immune resistance, with important limitations.** Experimentally parasitized medium ground finch mothers had higher fly-binding antibody levels; higher maternal antibodies tended to accompany fewer parasites, but did not rescue reproductive success. Males and nestlings did not show the same induced response. A finch/mockingbird comparison also found no significant antibody-mediated response in its sampled hosts. Antibody-related adaptive immunity and B-cell mechanisms therefore have strong relevance, but neither universal responsiveness nor effectiveness is assumed. Innate immunity, inflammation and cytokine signaling are reasonable predictions from biting injury; specific cytokines or signaling pathways are supporting predictions rather than documented Philornis-specific responses. Sources: Koop et al. (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ ; Knutie et al. (2016), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ .

- **Resource-dependent tolerance.** Galapagos mockingbirds can compensate through greater nestling begging and parental provisioning, maintaining reproductive success despite parasites. This tolerance varies among years and declines under limited rainfall/resources. Finch species often fail to increase provisioning; green warbler-finch females even reduced provisioning at high infestation in one study. Energy homeostasis, nutrient utilization and growth support are plausible tolerance mechanisms; generic metabolism is less specific than direct blood replacement. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963843/ ; *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth ... Green Warbler-Finch* (2023), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583/ . The latter is a behavioral/growth study, not an expression study.

- **Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Preening, occasional larval removal/consumption and nest sanitation are candidate defenses, but observations do not establish widespread effective behavioral resistance in finches. Mothers may reduce brooding or stand more to avoid larvae, potentially compromising nestling thermoregulation. Rubbing guayabillo leaves on feathers has been proposed as self-medication; its role against the fly remains a proposal. Sensory, learning, circadian and motor processes can support these behaviors, but broad neuronal signaling or neural development terms are not themselves evidence of antiparasite behavior. Synthesis: McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies ...*, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 ; maternal/nestling behavioral measurements: Koop et al. (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

## Scoring interpretation

5 denotes a term directly capturing an observed mechanism (e.g., immune response, response to wounding, compensatory erythrocyte differentiation). 4 denotes a core component or strong prediction (e.g., B-cell activity, hematopoiesis, wound healing, hypoxia response and coagulation). 3 denotes supporting physiology, including energy metabolism, inflammatory regulation, tissue remodeling and cellular stress. 2 denotes a weak/indirect link, including general transcription, translation, trafficking, signaling and many homeostatic mechanisms. 1 denotes no specific link, including many embryonic patterning, reproductive-cell, organ-development and virus-specific processes. Negative regulation was evaluated as modulation of a relevant process, not automatically scored as protective or irrelevant. Avian thrombocytes perform hemostatic functions; platelet-named processes were assessed for their physiological analogues, without assuming birds have mammalian platelets. Specific infection pathways were not promoted merely because the fly is a parasite.
