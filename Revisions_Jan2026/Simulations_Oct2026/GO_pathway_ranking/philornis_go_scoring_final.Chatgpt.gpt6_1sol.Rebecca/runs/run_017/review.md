# Independent physiological review for run 017

The fly's larval stages, rather than free-living adults, injure hosts. Early larvae occupy nestling nares and tissues; later larvae hide in nest material and feed externally, especially at night. Tissue destruction, open feeding wounds, blood loss and anemia impair nestling growth and survival. Naris deformation can persist and alter adult song. These observations motivate strong predictions for hemostasis, epithelial/barrier repair, extracellular matrix remodeling, erythropoiesis, iron handling and responses to reduced oxygen delivery. Wound repair and hypoxia responses are predictions from the injury, not demonstrated molecular responses.

Resistance evidence includes experimentally induced P. downsi-binding antibodies in medium ground-finch mothers. The association between stronger antibody responses and fewer larvae was suggestive, and antibodies did not rescue offspring survival in that experiment. Antibody responses were not consistently detected in males or nestlings. Thus adaptive immunity and erythrocyte replacement receive the strongest ratings; B-cell processes and innate/inflammatory support are strong predictions, while specific cytokine pathways have less direct evidence. Inflammation and ingested immune effectors have been proposed as mechanisms against blood-feeding larvae, rather than established as effective responses in every host.

Tolerance is distinct from parasite removal. Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging and received more provisioning; resource compensation explained their better performance relative to finches. Mockingbird tolerance weakened in dry years. Energy balance, nutrient responses and resource allocation are consequently relevant, but generic metabolism does not automatically indicate parasite defense.

Observed/proposed behaviors include preening, allopreening, nest sanitation, agitation/avoidance and altered brooding. Reduced brooding can also compromise thermoregulation. Finches incorporate supplied insecticide-treated cotton into nests. Leaf rubbing with Psidium galapageium is observed; leaf extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larvae, but the natural behavior's antiparasitic effectiveness remains a hypothesis. These findings support behavioral capacity without making every neural-development or synaptic term specifically relevant.

Scores assess a potential host response, not proven adaptation, selection, or increased fitness. Generic cellular machinery, embryonic patterning, reproduction and organ-specific neural construction receive low scores without a specific connection. Antiviral pathways and radiation/UV responses are weak or nonspecific links. No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression study of Galapagos birds informed these ratings.
