# Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds: mechanisms used for GO scoring

## What the larvae do to hosts
- First and early second instar larvae live inside the host, causing myiasis in the nares and other tissues. This produces tissue damage and permanent enlargement or deformation of the naris and beak. Older larvae live in the nest base and feed on blood at night, causing 18-55% blood loss (anaemia) and high nestling mortality (up to 76%). (Fessl et al. 2006 Parasitology; Dudaniec et al. 2006 Austral Ecology)
- Parasitized nestlings have lower haemoglobin and slower growth, and many die before fledging. (Dudaniec et al. 2006; McNew et al. 2019)
- Naris deformation goes on to change adult song and mating signals. (Kleindorfer et al. 2019)
- Nest microclimate (temperature, humidity) affects how fast larvae develop. Comparisons of host blood quality showed little difference between species. (Frontiers Ecol Evol 2024)

## Host defenses
- **Resistance (adaptive humoral):** Darwin's finches produce *P. downsi*-specific IgY antibodies, and higher antibody levels are linked to fewer parasites. The response is weak in nestlings and appears mostly in adults (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE). In one study, neither finches nor mockingbirds showed a significant antibody response (Knutie et al. 2016).
- **Inflammation and innate immunity at wound sites:** these are proposed general responses to larval feeding. They connect to wound healing, haemostasis and skin repair.
- **Tolerance:** this means regaining blood and energy. Mockingbirds tolerate the flies because parasitized nestlings beg more and parents feed them more. Tolerance falls in dry years when food is scarce (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Finches showed no compensating rise in glucose (Knutie et al. 2016). Urban nestlings that ate more protein had fewer parasites, lost less blood and survived better (Knutie et al., urban study; only its abstract-level ecological findings were used). Proposed tolerance mechanisms include erythropoiesis, haemoglobin and iron homeostasis, and energy and glucose homeostasis.
- **Stress and endocrine:** fly treatment did not change corticosterone in breeding female finches (Knutie et al. 2013 GCE).
- **Behavioral:** finches rub *Psidium galapageium* leaves into their feathers, and the leaves repel adult flies and reduce larval growth (Cimadom et al. 2016 Sci Rep). Other behavioral defenses are nestling behavior, begging and parental provisioning.

## Scoring logic
- **5:** humoral or adaptive immunity (IgY and B cells) and erythropoiesis (recovering haemoglobin).
- **4:** general immunity and inflammation, haemostasis and wound healing, pro-inflammatory cytokines, T cells, and iron homeostasis.
- **3:** downstream signaling (NF-kB, TLR, JAK-STAT, TNF), hypoxia, angiogenesis, skin and ECM repair, energy, glucose and starvation responses, oxidative stress, pain, and bone remodeling.
- **2:** antiviral immunity, vascular and bone development, insulin and metabolism, learning and memory (behavior), xenobiotic response, and general stress.
- **1:** everything else (housekeeping, cell cycle, RNA and DNA processing, neural and organ development, and so on).

Transcriptomic and genomic results from Galápagos birds were not used.
