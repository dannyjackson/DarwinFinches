# Review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_027)

## What the larvae do
- First-instar larvae live in nestlings' nares. Older instars hide in nest material and come out at night to feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and sometimes of brooding females. This causes blood loss (lower hemoglobin, i.e. anemia), lower mass in older nestlings, high nestling mortality, and enlarged nares and deformed beaks. The beak damage later changes song and may affect mate choice and hybridization.
- Larvae make open wounds, so infection and tissue damage are likely downstream effects.

## Documented or proposed host defenses
- **Resistance, adaptive or humoral:** female finches exposed to P. downsi produce a parasite-specific IgY antibody response. Experiments link higher antibody titers to fewer parasites (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). Nestlings had weak or undetectable antibody responses.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly. Their nestlings lose hemoglobin but fledge normally. Proposed routes are making up lost blood (erythropoiesis, iron balance) and compensation through begging and provisioning (Knutie et al. 2016). Parasitized mockingbirds had lower plasma glucose, which ties energy and glucose metabolism to the costs of parasitism.
- **Behavioral:** changes in begging and provisioning, less brooding (parents stand on the nest rim), and possibly preening. Begging intensity does not honestly signal need in parasitized finch nests.
- **Stress and energetics:** corticosterone (glucocorticoid) responses in breeding finches have been tested experimentally. Food and nutrition affect how well hosts cope.
- **Plausible supporting processes:** inflammation and cytokine signaling at bite sites, innate immunity, hemostasis and coagulation (larvae feed on blood), wound healing and re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, oxidative stress from blood feeding and heme, and responses to bacteria entering wounds.
