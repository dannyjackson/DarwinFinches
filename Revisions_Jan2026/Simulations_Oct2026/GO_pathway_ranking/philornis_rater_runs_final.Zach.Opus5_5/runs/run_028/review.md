# Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds: a brief review for a priori GO scoring

## What the larvae do
- Free-living adult flies lay eggs in nests. The larvae feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and sometimes of brooding females. Early instars feed inside the nares, which deforms the beak and naris; later instars feed externally at night. The effects are blood loss, lower hemoglobin and anemia, open lesions and tissue damage, slower growth, and high nestling mortality. Sources: Fessl et al. 2006, Parasitology (https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0031182006001089/type/journal_article); IUCN GISD (https://iucngisd.org/gisd/species.php?sc=1400); naris deformation and nestling behaviour (https://redi.cedia.edu.ec/document/365568).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (adaptive / humoral immunity):** Adult and nestling medium ground finches produce IgY antibodies specific to P. downsi. Antibody levels respond to experimental parasite manipulation and have been linked to fewer parasites. Sources: Koop et al. 2013, Ecology & Evolution (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3930052/); SICB abstract (https://sicb.org/?p=25430). Innate and inflammatory responses at feeding sites, and blood coagulation against hematophagous feeding, are general host defenses and strong a priori predictions.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate P. downsi through more begging and more parental provisioning, which compensates for energy and blood loss. Finches show no such compensation, and neither species mounted a significant antibody response in that study (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology: https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70770688-559c-41e2-ba8b-ead6e9e97f7d/full). Food and resource availability affects tolerance (Dryad data: https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.n11h95s), and urban finches with access to human food survive parasitism better (https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10496501). Physiological tolerance mechanisms predicted from this work are blood replacement (hematopoiesis, erythropoiesis, iron homeostasis), tissue repair and wound healing, and energy and glucose homeostasis.
- **Behavioral:** Finches self-fumigate by incorporating permethrin-treated cotton into nests (Knutie et al. 2014: https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/120.pdf). Nestling behaviour predicts naris damage.
- **Stress physiology:** Corticosterone in breeding finches has been tested experimentally against fly manipulation (https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65df89250f3e94b9e5d97be8). Oxidative stress from blood feeding and inflammation is a plausible secondary effect.

## Scoring rationale
- **5:** antibody-mediated adaptive immunity (immune response, adaptive immune response, B cell activation and differentiation).
- **4:** core immune, inflammatory and cytokine processes; T-cell help; complement; hematopoiesis and erythropoiesis; coagulation; iron homeostasis; wound response and healing.
- **3:** supporting innate signalling (TLR, NF-kB, TNF, JAK-STAT), phagocytosis, chemotaxis, skin and ECM repair, angiogenesis, hypoxia, oxidative stress, glucose and energy homeostasis, IGF signalling.
- **2:** weak links (growth and nutrient signalling, bone and beak, hormone signalling, learning, xenobiotic response, antibacterial defense of wounds).
- **1:** everything else, including antiviral terms.
