# Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds: host mechanisms used for scoring

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Females lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae live in the nestlings' nares. Second- and third-instar larvae live in the nest base and come out at night to feed externally on nestlings' blood and tissue, and sometimes on brooding females. This causes wounds, blood loss (anaemia, lower hemoglobin), slower growth, enlarged or deformed nares (which can also affect song), and high nestling mortality.
- Sources: Fessl et al. 2006 Parasitology (https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0031182006001089/type/journal_article); O'Connor et al. 2010 video analysis (https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF956C9751763DAFAEE36345498A4A01/S0030605310000086a.pdf/video_analysis_of_hostparasite_interactions_in_nests_of_darwins_finches.pdf); summary page (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avian_vampire_fly); Kleindorfer group naris and behaviour work (https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fevo.2024.1398353/full).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Adaptive humoral immunity (resistance).** Female medium ground finches at parasitized nests produced P. downsi-binding antibodies (IgY). Higher titres tended to go with fewer parasites, but the response did not save broods. Source: Koop et al. 2013 Ecol. Evol. (https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/a9e7cf71cae2cfd1aba4fccd3a329bce/PMC3930052.pdf); Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE (https://doaj.org/article/c0c1dd23b9f64ef2bc80d5833593b012).
2. **Tolerance through blood replacement and energy compensation.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings had lower hemoglobin but higher plasma glucose. They begged more and their parents fed them more, and their fledging success held up. Tolerance depended on rainfall and food supply. Source: Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology (https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/126.pdf); McNew et al. 2019 Proc. R. Soc. B (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884).
3. **Diet and condition.** Urban finches eating human food survived parasitism much better, which suggests that resources (protein, energy) support tolerance and resistance. Source: https://phys.org/news/2024-01-galapagos-urban-finches-fare-vampire.pdf. Only the survival and diet findings were used; the gene-expression results were deliberately ignored.
4. **Behavioral defenses.** Brooding, preening and nest sanitation were observed and changed only modestly. Finches also self-fumigate by bringing permethrin-treated cotton into their nests. Sources: Koop et al. 2013; Knutie et al. 2014 (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/darwins-finches-stamp-out-deadly-parasite-with-help-from-cotton-balls).

## Physiological predictions used in scoring
- **5:** adaptive immunity and B-cell responses, wound response and healing, and erythropoiesis (to replace lost blood).
- **4:** inflammation, cytokines, T cells, innate immunity, coagulation and platelets (hemostasis at feeding wounds), hemopoiesis and iron homeostasis.
- **3:** downstream or supporting processes: NF-kB, JAK-STAT, chemotaxis, phagocytosis, oxidative stress and ROS, hypoxia (from anaemia), angiogenesis, skin and keratinocyte biology, extracellular matrix and collagen (tissue repair), glucose homeostasis and gluconeogenesis, response to starvation and nutrients, steroid (glucocorticoid) signaling, and pain.
- **2:** weak or indirect links: antibacterial and TLR responses (secondary infection of wounds), vascular endothelial processes, bone and beak development (naris deformation), insulin and lipid metabolism, growth factors, ER stress, and generic hormone signaling.
- **1:** everything else, such as housekeeping, neural development, the cell cycle, RNA processing, antiviral responses and gametogenesis.
