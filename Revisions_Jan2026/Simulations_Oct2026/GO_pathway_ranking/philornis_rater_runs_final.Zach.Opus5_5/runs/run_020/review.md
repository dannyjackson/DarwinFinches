# Literature review: Philornis downsi and host responses (run_020)

## What the larvae do
- First-instar larvae live in the nares and ear canals of nestlings. Second and third instars live in the nest base and feed at night on blood and tissue. This causes lesions, internal bleeding, swelling, enlarged or deformed nares that can persist into adulthood, and in severe cases loss of the upper beak.
- Effects on nestlings include lower hemoglobin and hematocrit (roughly -0.8 g/dL per added larva and about 28% overall), lower body mass, shorter feathers and slower growth. In-nest mortality is high, averaging about 55% and often near 100%. Death follows blood loss.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance through the humoral immune response:** P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies were measured by ELISA. They were higher in adult females at parasitized nests, and higher antibody levels correlated with fewer parasites. No response was detected in nestlings at about 5 days old.
- **Inflammation:** The proposed mechanism is that antibodies speed up inflammation at feeding sites, which keeps larvae from reaching blood vessels. Inflamed tissue may also expose larvae to host defensive peptides or lytic molecules. Itching may prompt defensive behavior.
- **Tolerance:** Proposed routes are compensating for blood loss (making red blood cells, hemostasis), repairing wounds and tissue, and compensating for lost energy. In mockingbirds, more begging and more parental provisioning appear to offset the damage, while finches showed no change in begging, provisioning or glucose. In a related nest-fly system, blood recovery and parental compensation were tested and not supported as tolerance mechanisms, and tissue repair was suggested as a future test.
- **Behavior:** Females brood less, probe the nest base and preen nestlings, sometimes eating larvae. Nestlings climb on top of siblings and preen. Finches rub leaves of Psidium galapageium into their plumage, which repels adult flies and reduces larval weight gain. No corticosterone stress response was found in females.
- A transcriptomic study of Galápagos finches came up during the search. Per the rules, none of its results were used.
