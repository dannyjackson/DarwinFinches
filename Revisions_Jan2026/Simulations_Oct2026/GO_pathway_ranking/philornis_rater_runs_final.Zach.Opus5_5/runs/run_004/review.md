# Philornis downsi and host responses: summary of literature review (run_004)

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae enter the nares and sometimes the body of nestlings. Later instars live in nest material and feed at night on the blood and tissue of nestlings and brooding females (Fessl et al. 2006, Parasitology).
- The effects are blood loss and anemia (lower haemoglobin), open wounds and tissue damage, slower growth (mass, tarsus, feathers), and high nestling mortality, often more than 50%. Nestlings that survive can have enlarged nares and deformed beaks, which later alter song and mating signals (Dudaniec et al.; Koop et al.; Kleindorfer et al. 2019).

## Documented or proposed host defenses
- **Resistance (adaptive/humoral):** Nestlings and adult females produce P. downsi-specific antibodies (IgY). In medium ground finches, higher antibody titres were linked to fewer parasites (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013, experimental demonstration). This points to B-cell activation, the adaptive response and possibly complement.
- **Innate and inflammatory responses** at feeding sites, plus wound repair of skin and epithelium (general myiasis pathology; Philornis histopathology in other hosts).
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite in wet years. Tolerance falls in dry years and increases with parental provisioning (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Proposed tolerance mechanisms are compensating for blood loss (erythropoiesis, iron homeostasis, haemostasis/coagulation), keeping growth and energy balance going under resource limits, and damage control (inflammation regulation, oxidative stress, wound healing). Urban finches that have human food show higher tolerance (urban rescue study).
- **Behavioral:** Nestlings beg more and parents may adjust provisioning (Kleindorfer and colleagues). Nest sanitation, preening and standing on nestmates gave no effective resistance. Finches use self-fumigation with permethrin-treated cotton when it is provided (Knutie et al. 2014). Females may adjust brooding.
- **Stress physiology:** Corticosterone responses in breeding finches have been tested experimentally, giving a weak, indirect link to hormone signalling.

## Scoring logic
- 5: immune response, adaptive immune response, B-cell activation/BCR signalling, inflammatory and defense response.
- 4: general immune regulation, innate immunity, complement, B-cell differentiation/proliferation, T-cell activation, inflammation regulation, wounding/wound healing, coagulation/platelets, haematopoiesis/erythropoiesis, iron homeostasis.
- 3: cytokine and NF-kB signalling, phagocytosis/chemotaxis, epidermis/collagen/ECM repair, TGF-beta, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, hypoxia, pain.
- 2: bacterial/viral PRR signalling, growth-factor signalling, nutrient/energy and insulin pathways, hormone/stress responses, learning/memory (behavior), cardiovascular regulation.
- 1: everything else, such as housekeeping, development, neural, meiosis and DNA repair.
