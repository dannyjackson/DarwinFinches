# Philornis downsi and its Galápagos hosts: literature review for GO scoring (run_030)

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae live in the nestlings' nares. Later instars hide in the nest base and feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and of brooding females, at night (Fessl et al. 2006).
- Harm to the host: blood loss and anaemia (lower haemoglobin), skin and tissue wounds, enlarged or deformed nares, lower growth and body condition, and very high nestling mortality, in some years up to 100% (Fessl et al. 2006; Dudaniec et al.; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009).
- Saliva from a blood-feeding larva probably contains anticoagulants and immunomodulators, so the host's haemostasis and wound response are directly challenged. This is my general-knowledge inference, not a finding from the papers above.

## Documented or proposed host defences
1. **Adaptive immune resistance (5).** Adult and nestling finches make P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies (Huber et al. 2010, medium ground finch). Experiments link antibody titres to parasite exposure and to fewer or smaller larvae (Koop et al. 2013; Knutie et al. 2016). This supports B-cell and antibody processes, with T-cell help as a core component (4).
2. **Tolerance through blood replacement (5).** Haemoglobin loss is the main damage. Erythropoiesis and haematopoiesis are the tolerance mechanism, and iron homeostasis is a core component (4). Hypoxia responses and blood-pressure or heart-rate compensation are downstream (3 or 2).
3. **Haemostasis (4).** Blood coagulation and thrombocyte activation or aggregation oppose blood feeding.
4. **Innate immunity, inflammation and wound repair (4).** Larval wounds are proposed to trigger inflammation, which could deter feeding. Cytokine, NF-κB, TLR, JAK-STAT, complement, phagocytosis, chemotaxis and respiratory-burst processes are supporting processes (3 or 4). Skin and epidermis repair and keratinocyte processes score 3.
5. **Tolerance through resources and parental behaviour (3).** Compensatory parental provisioning has been shown in Galápagos mockingbirds, which tolerate P. downsi (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology). Food and nutrient limitation affects outcomes in warbler finches. Nutrient, starvation and energy-homeostasis terms score 3, and metabolic hormone terms score 2.
6. **Stress physiology (3).** Corticosterone responses of breeding finches have been tested experimentally, so hormone and glucocorticoid-receptor signalling terms score 3.
7. **Behavioural defences (2).** These include rubbing feathers with Psidium galapageium leaves (Cimadom et al. 2016), female in-nest attendance and brooding (Cimadom et al. 2021), nestling begging and behaviour (linked to naris deformation), and nest-material choice. They have no direct GO correlate, so learning, memory and cognition score 2, and nociception scores 3 because larvae bite the host.

Terms with no specific link score 1. These include development, the cell cycle, DNA, RNA and protein housekeeping, neuronal and synaptic processes, and antiviral or interferon pathways (avian pox is a separate pathogen).

I did not use any genomic or transcriptomic results from Galápagos birds. McNew et al., the urban-finch study (NSF PAR record below), includes gene-expression data. From that paper I used only the field and physiological observations: haemoglobin, survival and the inflammation hypothesis.
