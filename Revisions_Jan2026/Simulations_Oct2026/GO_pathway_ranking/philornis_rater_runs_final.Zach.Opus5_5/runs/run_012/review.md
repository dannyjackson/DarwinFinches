# Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds: brief review used for scoring

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies lay eggs in host nests. First-instar larvae live in the nestlings' nares and ear canals. Later instars live in the nest material and feed on nestlings' blood and tissue at night. This causes blood loss (lower haemoglobin), open wounds and tissue damage (including enlarged or deformed nares and beaks), slower growth and very high nestling mortality. Adult brooding females can also be bitten.
- Sources: Fessl et al. 2006, Parasitology (https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0031182006001089/type/journal_article); Dudaniec et al. 2006, on haemoglobin effects (https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/); O'Connor et al. 2010, video of host–parasite interactions (https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/BF956C9751763DAFAEE36345498A4A01/core-reader); IUCN GISD (https://iucngisd.org/gisd/species.php?sc=1400).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Resistance through acquired immunity.** Parasitized female medium ground finches produce P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies. Higher antibody titres are linked to fewer parasites and better fledging success. This is the B-cell, humoral and adaptive-immunity axis (Huber et al. 2010, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3930052/; Koop et al. 2013, https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/ece3.651). Proposed: maternal antibodies passed to nestlings, and innate or inflammatory responses at feeding wounds.
2. **Tolerance.** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasites: they suffer no loss of fledging success. This is thought to come from extra parental provisioning and nestling begging, and from making up for blood loss (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology, https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70770688-559c-41e2-ba8b-ead6e9e97f7d/full). Food supplementation raises tolerance in finches (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884). Candidate physiological mechanisms are erythropoiesis, haemostasis and coagulation, wound healing and tissue repair, and the energy and nutrient metabolism behind growth under blood loss.
3. **Behavioral defenses.** Finches put permethrin-treated cotton into their nests ("self-fumigation", Knutie et al. 2014, https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/120.pdf). They rub leaves of Psidium galapageium, a repellent plant, into their feathers (Cimadom et al. 2016, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5056383/). Female in-nest attendance is linked to parasite load (https://redi.cedia.edu.ec/document/247411). Nestlings also show defensive behavior against the larvae. These suggest a weak link to learning and cognition.
4. **Stress physiology.** Fly-induced changes in adult corticosterone were tested experimentally. The effects were limited, but this makes hormone or glucocorticoid signalling a plausible supporting process (https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65df89250f3e94b9e5d97be8).

## Scoring logic
- **5:** adaptive and humoral immune response, the documented antibody mechanism.
- **4:** B-cell activation and differentiation, the B-cell receptor pathway, innate, defense and inflammatory responses, complement, haemostasis and coagulation, platelet processes, erythropoiesis, and wound healing.
- **3:** cytokine and NF-kB/JAK-STAT signalling, T-cell processes, phagocytosis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, angiogenesis, skin and keratinocyte repair, ECM and collagen repair, and TGF-beta.
- **2:** general stress, oxidative and redox processes, nutrient and energy metabolism and starvation, hormone signalling, bone and beak development, learning and memory, pain, and xenobiotic response (insecticide).
- **1:** all other terms.
