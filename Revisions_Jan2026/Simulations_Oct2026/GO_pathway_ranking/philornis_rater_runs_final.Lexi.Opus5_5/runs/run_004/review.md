# Run 004: Literature review on the host response to Philornis downsi

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae enter the nestlings' nares and can enlarge or deform the naris and beak. Later instars live in the nest material and feed at night on the nestlings' blood and tissue, which wounds the skin.
- The effects are blood loss (lower hemoglobin and hematocrit), slower growth, less energy reserve and high nestling mortality. Nestlings often die before they fledge.
- Adult females that brood the nestlings are also bitten.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (adaptive humoral immunity).** Adult and nestling finches produce *P. downsi*-specific IgY antibodies. Experimentally parasitized nests had higher antibody levels, and higher titers were linked to fewer parasites. Antibodies may also pass from mother to nestling.
- **Inflammatory and innate responses.** These are expected at larval feeding sites and are proposed in the wider *Philornis* myiasis literature.
- **Tolerance.** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly. Parents bring more food, so nestlings stay in condition despite the parasite load. Proposed tolerance mechanisms are:
  - replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis and iron),
  - hemostasis and wound repair,
  - coping with nutrition and energy shortfalls,
  - coping with low oxygen supply caused by anemia.
- **Physiological stress.** Adult female corticosterone was tested experimentally and showed little effect, so this link is weak.
- **Behavioral defenses.** These include:
  - building fumigated (permethrin-treated) cotton into nests, an experimental tool that birds took up,
  - rubbing Psidium galapageium leaves on the feathers, which works as a repellent,
  - more begging, more provisioning, and nest-attending behavior.
