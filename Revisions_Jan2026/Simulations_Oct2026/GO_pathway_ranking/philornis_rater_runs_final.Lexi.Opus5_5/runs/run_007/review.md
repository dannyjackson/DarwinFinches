# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_007)

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests; larvae feed on nestlings (and brooding females) for blood and tissue. Early instars live in the nares and burrow into tissue. Later instars feed externally on blood at night.
- Documented effects: blood loss with lower haemoglobin (anaemia), lower nestling mass and growth, deformed and enlarged nares (beak damage that lasts into adulthood), skin wounds, and high nestling mortality (often >50% in parasitised nests). Females brooding the nest are also bitten.

## Host defences documented or proposed
- **Resistance (immune):** Nestlings and adults produce P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies. Higher antibody titres go with fewer parasites, which points to adaptive and humoral (B-cell) immunity. Innate and inflammatory responses at bite and wound sites are proposed.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite, and in some species and years parents feed nestlings more. Physiological tolerance is predicted to work through replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis, iron and haem balance), stopping bleeding (coagulation and thrombocytes), wound and skin repair (angiogenesis, extracellular matrix and collagen, keratinocytes), and limiting immunopathology (control of inflammation). Coping with anaemia-related hypoxia and with energy and nutrient stress is a supporting process.
- **Stress physiology:** Corticosterone responses of parasitised breeding finches have been tested experimentally, so glucocorticoid (steroid hormone) signalling is relevant.
- **Behaviour:** Finches self-fumigate by rubbing leaves of Psidium galapagense on their feathers, which repels P. downsi. Learning and chemical (xenobiotic) handling are possible weak links. Parental provisioning and nest attendance are behavioural forms of tolerance.
