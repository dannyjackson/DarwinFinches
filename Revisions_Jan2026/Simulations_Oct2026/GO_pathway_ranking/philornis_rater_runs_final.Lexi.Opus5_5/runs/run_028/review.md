# Philornis downsi and Galapagos land birds: mechanisms used for scoring (run_028)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Larvae are semi-hematophagous. They feed on nestling blood and tissue, mostly at night, and hide in the base of the nest during the day. Females oviposit in nests, sometimes as early as mid-incubation.
- Blood loss and anemia: nestlings lose an estimated 18-55% of their blood overnight. Hemoglobin is about 28% lower in parasitized finch nestlings, and about 40% lower in sham-treated mockingbird nestlings.
- Tissue damage: larvae cause wounds, damage to the nasal cavity, and enlarged or deformed nares (beak malformation).
- Growth and condition: parasitized nestlings have lower body mass and shorter primary feathers. Survival and fledging success are much lower: in-nest mortality ranges from 16% to 100% across hosts, and nestlings in more recent years have died younger.
- P. downsi affects 12 of the 17 Darwin's finch species and has contributed to population declines (for example in the small tree finch and green warbler-finch).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- Resistance through the immune response: finches can mount an antibody (IgY) response to P. downsi (Koop et al., experimental study). Inflammation, wound repair and clotting at feeding sites are strong a priori predictions. In nestling comparisons, the antibody responses were weak or undetectable.
- Tolerance:
  - Mockingbirds appear to tolerate parasitism. Parents at parasitized nests provision more, and this tracks nestling begging.
  - Parasitized mockingbird nestlings have higher plasma glucose, which points to energy compensation.
  - Making up for blood loss (erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis) and maintaining growth under nutritional stress are further predicted tolerance routes.
- Behavior:
  - Parents preen nestling nares, remove dead nestlings, and enter the nest more often.
  - Females at heavily parasitized nests brood about 42% less and stand in the nest about 74% more.
  - Nestlings stand on each other, apparently to avoid larvae.
  - Heavily parasitized nestlings beg less, and parents feed the strongest beggars.
  - Finches take up insecticide-treated cotton for nest "self-fumigation", but this is a human-designed intervention, not a natural trait.

## Scoring logic
- **5:** wound healing and response to wounding, blood coagulation, inflammatory response, immune and adaptive immune response, B cell activation (antibody), erythrocyte differentiation and hemopoiesis, defense response.
- **4:** regulation of inflammation and immunity, complement, platelet activation and aggregation, B and T cell signaling and differentiation, innate immunity, iron homeostasis, hematopoietic progenitors.
- **3:** cytokines and chemokines, leukocyte migration and phagocytosis, hypoxia (anemia), angiogenesis, skin, keratinocyte and ECM repair, TGF-beta, nutrient, starvation and energy homeostasis (tolerance), pain perception (nestling detection of larvae), oxidative stress, general stress response.
- **2:** antibacterial and PRR signaling (secondary wound infection), NF-kB and JAK-STAT, cardiovascular compensation, endothelial and epithelial repair details, glucose and insulin metabolism, thermogenesis (reduced brooding), learning, memory and neuropeptides (behavior), hormone and xenobiotic responses.
- **1:** all other terms (development, housekeeping, neural, DNA and RNA processing, and so on). This includes antiviral terms and the unnamed GO:0160240.
