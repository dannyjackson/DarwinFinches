# Review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* in Galápagos land birds (run_008)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests; early-instar larvae enter nestling nares, later instars feed externally at night on blood and tissue. This causes open wounds, blood loss, reduced hemoglobin (anemia), slower growth, enlarged/deformed nares and beaks (later changing song), and high nestling mortality across Darwin's finches and other passerines.
  - Darwin Initiative factsheet: https://www.darwininitiative.org.uk/documents/DAR15005/20871/15-005%20Philornis%20factsheet%202009.pdf
  - Galápagos Conservation Trust species page: https://galapagosconservation.org.uk/?p=8221
  - Hemoglobin effects (Flinders): https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/
  - Beak/naris damage: https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/parasites-are-destroying-the-beaks-of-darwins-famous-finches

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Resistance via antibodies.** Finches make *P. downsi*-specific IgY antibodies (adaptive, B-cell mediated). Experiments that manipulated parasites showed the response is induced by the parasite.
   - Huber et al. 2010, PLoS ONE: https://doaj.org/article/c0c1dd23b9f64ef2bc80d5833593b012
   - Koop et al. 2013, experimental immune response: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3930052/
   - Fitness consequences of the immune response: https://sicb.org/?p=25430
2. **Tolerance.** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite, apparently by parents bringing more food so nestlings make up for blood loss. Proposed physiological routes to tolerance are replacing lost blood (making new red cells, using iron), stopping bleeding, healing wounds, and limiting damage from inflammation.
   - Mockingbird tolerance: https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70770688-559c-41e2-ba8b-ead6e9e97f7d/full
   - Green Warbler-Finch provisioning and growth: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10188583/
   - Begging does not honestly signal need: https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/darwins-finch-begging-intensity-does-not-honestly-signal-need-in-/
   - Urban living (food) rescues finches: https://research.usfq.edu.ec/en/publications/urban-living-can-rescue-darwins-finches-from-the-lethal-effects-o/
3. **Behavioral defenses.** These include female brooding, nestlings moving or reacting to larvae, and self-fumigation (adults rubbing permethrin-treated cotton or *Psidium* leaves into the nest).
   - Video analysis of host-parasite interactions: https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/video-analysis-of-host-parasite-interactions-in-nests-of-darwins-/
   - Self-fumigation: https://theconversation.com/no-fly-zone-darwins-finches-self-fumigate-with-a-little-help-26399
   - Host-parasite coevolution (Tebbich group): https://becogbio.univie.ac.at/people/pis-postdoctoral-researchers/sabine-tebbich/host-parasite-co-evolution/
