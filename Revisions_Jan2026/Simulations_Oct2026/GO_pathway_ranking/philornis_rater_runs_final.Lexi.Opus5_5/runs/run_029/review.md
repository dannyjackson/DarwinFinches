# Run 029: literature review summary for Philornis downsi host response

## What the larvae do to the host
- Adult flies lay eggs in nest material and in nestling nares. First-instar larvae can live inside the nares. Later instars stay in the nest and come out mostly at night. They chew through the skin and eat blood and other fluids, so they are semi-hematophagous.
- Costs documented in nestlings:
  - skin lesions
  - naris and beak deformation that can last into adulthood
  - blood loss, measured as lower haemoglobin and haematocrit
  - slower growth and shorter primary feathers
  - high mortality before fledging (over 80% in heavily parasitized nests)

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (adaptive immunity).** Medium ground finches mount a P. downsi-specific IgY antibody response. The response correlates with fewer and smaller flies. This is the clearest documented host mechanism.
- **Tolerance.** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate parasitism in wet years but not in dry years. Tolerance increases with parental provisioning. Parasitized nestlings beg more. Haemoglobin loss happened in all years, so tolerance relies on compensating for resources (nutrition, growth, replacing blood) rather than on reducing damage.
- **Behaviour.**
  - Nestlings avoid larvae and preen them off.
  - Finches carry insecticide-treated cotton into their nests.
  - Some finches rub their feathers with Psidium leaves, a possible form of self-medication.
  - Changes in provisioning and begging are also reported.
  - None of these reliably improves fledging success.
- **Proposed and inferred mechanisms:**
  - hemostasis against larval anticoagulants (coagulation and platelets)
  - inflammation and wound repair in skin
  - erythropoiesis and iron balance to recover from anaemia
  - hypoxia and oxidative-stress responses
  - nutrient and energy homeostasis supporting growth under parasitism
  - nociception driving behavioural defence
  - craniofacial and bone development affected by naris damage
