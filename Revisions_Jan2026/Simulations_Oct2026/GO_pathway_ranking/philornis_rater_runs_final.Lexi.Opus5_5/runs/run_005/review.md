# Literature review: host responses of Galápagos land birds to *Philornis downsi*

## What the larvae do to their hosts
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. The larvae feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and sometimes of brooding females. Early instars live in the nares and can deform the nares and beak. Later instars feed on blood externally.
- The main effects are blood loss (lower hemoglobin), smaller body size and condition, lower blood glucose in heavily parasitized broods, and high nestling mortality (often close to 100%, rising with parasite intensity).
- No effects on the condition or survival of adults have been shown. In female medium ground finches, hematocrit and corticosterone did not differ between parasitized and fumigated nests.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Resistance through the adaptive and humoral immune system.** Breeding females produce antibodies (IgY) that bind *P. downsi* proteins, and antibody levels are higher in nests with fewer parasites. Nestlings show little measurable antibody response. This is the main reason B-cell, antibody and adaptive-immunity terms get the top scores. Innate, inflammatory and cytokine responses at feeding wounds are a strong a priori prediction.
2. **Tolerance through recovery from blood loss and tissue damage.** Hemoglobin level is the key health measure. Urban finch nestlings kept higher hemoglobin and fledged despite heavy infestations, and the authors propose this reflects recovery from blood loss. That predicts a role for erythropoiesis and hematopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hemostasis and coagulation, and wound healing and repair (re-epithelialization, fibroblasts, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix).
3. **Tolerance through nutrition and behavior.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings beg more and their parents feed them more, which compensates for the damage. Higher dietary protein (δ15N) is linked to fewer parasites. This points to processes for energy, glucose and nutrient homeostasis, response to starvation, and growth signaling (IGF/insulin).
4. **Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** These are rare or speculative: a nestling removing a larva by preening, a female picking at a nestling's nares, rubbing feathers with insect-repellent guava leaves, and nest height. Heavily parasitized females brood less, which affects nest temperature. These are only weakly linked to the GO terms (xenobiotic response, thermogenesis, pain perception).
5. **Stress physiology.** Corticosterone was measured in females, but parasite treatment had no effect on it, so terms for hormone and stress responses get only weak scores.

## Scoring approach
- **5:** antibody and B-cell, adaptive immune response, immune response, response to wounding and wound healing, erythrocyte differentiation.
- **4:** immune regulation, inflammation, complement, cytokine signaling, T-cell activation, hematopoiesis, iron homeostasis, coagulation and platelets, hypoxia.
- **3:** specific cytokines (TNF, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17), NF-κB, TLR and JAK-STAT signaling, leukocyte chemotaxis and adhesion, phagocytosis, skin and keratinocyte processes, angiogenesis, fibroblast and collagen processes, glucose, energy and starvation responses, response to bacteria (secondary wound infection), blood pressure and heart rate.
- **2:** oxidative stress, hormone and insulin signaling, thermogenesis, xenobiotic response, pain, TGF-β, EGF and VEGF signaling, bone and beak-related development, apoptosis, ER stress, lipid storage, antiviral and interferon terms.
- **1:** housekeeping processes (DNA, RNA, translation, cell cycle, neurodevelopment, synaptic processes and similar).

I did not use the transcriptomic study by Knutie et al. (bioRxiv 2023.03.06.531275) or any gene-expression results.
