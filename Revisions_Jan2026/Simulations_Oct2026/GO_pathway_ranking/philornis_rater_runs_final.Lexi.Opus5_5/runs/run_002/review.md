# Review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_002)

## What the larvae do
- Free-living larvae live in the nest base and feed at night on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and also of brooding females. First instars enter the nares, which can leave nestlings with an enlarged naris and a deformed beak. Infestation causes skin and tissue lesions and blood loss.
- Recorded effects on hosts: lower hemoglobin (anemia), lower body mass and slower growth (mass, tarsus and feathers), and high nestling mortality in Darwin's finches. Damage to the beak and naris can later change song and foraging.

## Host defenses that have been documented or proposed
- **Resistance (humoral and adaptive immunity):** Medium ground finches produce antibodies that bind P. downsi. Females with higher antibody titres had fewer parasites in their nests (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). Food supplementation and urban living have been linked to lower parasite loads. Inflammatory and innate responses at feeding sites, including cytokines, complement and leukocyte recruitment, are the expected route to that resistance.
- **Tolerance (limiting damage):** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite. They lose hemoglobin and mass but keep fledging success (Knutie et al. 2016). Their tolerance changes from year to year and depends on rainfall and parental provisioning (McNew et al. 2019). Physiological mechanisms predicted from this: making new red cells, iron homeostasis, coagulation and hemostasis, wound healing and epidermal repair, a response to hypoxia, and coping with energy or nutrient stress.
- **Behavioral defenses:** Parasitized nestlings beg more, and some parents feed them more to compensate (Knutie et al. 2016; studies of parental care in tree finches and the warbler-finch). Finches also self-fumigate by bringing permethrin-treated cotton into their nests. Behavior and the nervous system therefore have only an indirect, weak link to the GO terms.
- Parasitism has also been tied to lower gut bacterial richness in nestlings (Solomon et al. 2023).
