# run_009: P. downsi host-response literature review (summary)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Philornis downsi is a muscid fly. Adults lay eggs in the nests of Galápagos land birds. First-instar larvae live inside the nestlings' nares (nostrils) and later instars feed externally on nestling blood and tissue at night. This causes blood loss, lower haemoglobin (anaemia), tissue damage, enlarged or deformed nares, slower growth and high nestling mortality. Brooding females can also be bitten.
- Secondary bacterial infection of wounds is possible, and so is pathogen transmission (for example avian pox). This is proposed, not established.

## Host defences documented or proposed
- **Resistance (immune):** finches make a P. downsi-specific IgY antibody response, so the adaptive or humoral response involves B cells. Antibody titres go down when nests are fumigated, and higher titres are linked to fewer or smaller larvae. Innate and inflammatory responses at feeding sites are a strong prediction.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate parasitism better than finches. They keep nestlings growing and surviving through more parental provisioning and through nestling compensation, which may include replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis, iron) and repairing tissue (wound healing, re-epithelialisation, angiogenesis, haemostasis). How well hosts tolerate the fly changes from year to year with food availability.
- **Stress / physiology:** corticosterone was tested experimentally in breeding finches. Oxidative stress and energy balance are plausible costs.
- **Behaviour:** begging intensity is not an honest signal of need in parasitised nests. Nest sanitation and self-fumigation (finches using permethrin-treated cotton) have been reported. Female brooding and provisioning behaviour may also matter.
