# Run 015 – Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi*

## What the larvae do
- Larvae feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, so they are hematophagous and break the skin. First instars live in the nares and ear canals and may eat beak keratin. Second and third instars hide in the nest base and pierce the skin to feed at night. In-nest mortality is high, up to 100%, and rises with parasite intensity.
- Parasitized nestlings have lower hemoglobin (anemia) and smaller body size. Survivors can have enlarged or deformed nares.
- In tree finches, deformed nares go with altered song: a lower maximum frequency and more deviation from typical song. This blurs species-specific mating signals.
- Larvae have been found in nests with eggs and have fed on adults in the lab. Adults show no clear costs.
- Corticosterone in breeding finches has been tested experimentally as a possible stress response.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (acquired immunity):** Brooding female medium ground finches make *P. downsi*-specific antibodies (IgY), and higher antibody levels go with lower parasite intensity. The response appears not to improve fledging success. Nestlings show no detectable antibody response.
- **Inferred resistance components:** inflammation and wound responses at feeding sites, innate immunity, and hemostasis (coagulation and platelets) against blood feeding.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite. Parasitized nestlings beg more and parents feed them more, which offsets the damage. Finches do not do this, and their plasma glucose does not change when parasitized. The physiological basis of tolerance is predicted to involve replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis), energy and glucose homeostasis, responses to nutrient limitation and hypoxia, and wound repair (ECM, collagen, keratinocyte/epidermis, angiogenesis).
- **Behavioral:** Parasitized females spend more time standing upright while brooding. Preening larvae off is rare. Rubbing *Psidium* (guava) leaves onto feathers has been proposed as a repellent. Nest density and breeding timing affect exposure. The cotton/permethrin "self-fumigation" studies are a human intervention, not an evolved host trait.

## Scoring rationale
- **5:** immune response, adaptive immune response (the documented antibody response).
- **4:** B-cell and antibody components, complement, innate and inflammatory responses, wound responses, coagulation and platelets, erythropoiesis, hemopoiesis, iron homeostasis.
- **3:** T-cell and cytokine signaling, phagocytosis, leukocyte chemotaxis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, ECM/collagen/keratinocyte repair, angiogenesis, pain perception, hypoxia, glucose and energy homeostasis, response to nutrient levels, glucocorticoid (steroid nuclear receptor) signaling.
- **2:** weaker links, such as LPS and bacterial defense from wound infection, vascular processes, oxidative stress, thermoregulation and circadian rhythm (brooding, nocturnal larval feeding), hormone signaling, learning and hearing (song), and palate development (beak and naris).
- **1:** everything else.
