# Independent physiological review for GO scoring

Only published ecological, parasitological, behavioral and physiological evidence and general biological knowledge informed scoring. No host genomic, transcriptomic, expression or selection results were used. The scores describe potential association with a host response, not demonstrated adaptation, protection or selection.

## Larval attack and physiological consequences

Early *Philornis downsi* larvae occupy nares and other tissues; later larvae reside in nest material and feed externally on nestlings, primarily at night. Brooding females may also be bitten. Tissue destruction and blood loss cause wounds, anemia, impaired growth and nestling mortality. Surviving birds may have persistent naris and beak deformities. Immature red blood cell numbers increase with parasite intensity, supporting compensatory erythropoiesis. Blood-cell replacement and tissue repair therefore connect closely to the documented damage. Coagulation, iron homeostasis, hypoxia responses, vascular repair and barrier maintenance are strong predictions or supporting processes; individual molecular pathways have not thereby been experimentally demonstrated.

- Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), “The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival.” https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089
- Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), “Effects of the introduced ectoparasite Philornis downsi on haemoglobin level and nestling survival ...” https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- Galligan & Kleindorfer (2009), “Naris and beak malformation caused by the parasitic fly, Philornis downsi ...” https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-8312.2009.01309.x

## Immune resistance

Experimentally parasitized medium ground finch mothers increased fly-binding antibodies. Stronger responses tended to accompany fewer parasites, but improved nestling survival was not demonstrated. Fathers and nestlings did not show comparable responses. Thus adaptive/humoral immunity is documented, while lymphocyte machinery, innate immunity, inflammatory regulation and cytokine signaling are core components or predictions of varying specificity. An antibody response does not establish effective protection, and immunity findings vary with host age and study. Specific antiviral processes have weaker links because the fly is not a virus.

- Koop et al. (2013), “Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin’s finches and introduced nest flies.” https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

## Tolerance and behavior

Parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with increased parental provisioning and apparent compensation for parasite damage. Finches did not consistently show equivalent compensation. Mockingbird tolerance declined in dry years, indicating dependence on resource availability. Energy balance, nutrient acquisition and growth can therefore support tolerance, without making every metabolic pathway a documented defense.

- Knutie et al. (2016), “Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin’s finches.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- McNew et al. (2019), “Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites.” https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/

Observed resistance behaviors include nestling and maternal preening or consumption of larvae, maternal probing of nares and nest material, and nestlings standing on siblings. Efficacy is variable, and very young nestlings have limited motor ability. Birds can incorporate experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton into nests; this exploits nest building and is not evidence of endogenous insecticide production. General neural processes only indirectly support these specific behaviors.

- O’Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), “Video analysis of host–parasite interactions in nests of Darwin’s finches.” https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086
- Knutie et al. (2014), “Darwin’s finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/

A manipulation of parasite abundance did not support maternal corticosterone elevation as the cause of reproductive failure. Stress physiology remains plausible but should not automatically receive a high score as an established defense.

- Knutie et al. (2013), “Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin’s finches.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/

## Application to terms

Every term was reviewed individually. Direct response categories receive 5; core components and strong physiological predictions receive 4; plausible downstream/supporting processes receive 3; weak links through general cell maintenance, signaling, neural function or resource allocation receive 2; processes with no specific connection receive 1. Regulation in either direction is not presumed beneficial. Specialized reproduction, embryonic patterning and many RNA-processing processes have no specific relationship; broad housekeeping processes generally have only weak connections. No target score distribution was imposed.

The input labels and IDs were preserved. GO:0160240 lacked a descriptive label in the input; its GO record identifies RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, scored as housekeeping: https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo/term/GO%3A0160240
