# Independent a priori physiological review

The scores use the requested five-point scale and distinguish observed host responses from predicted mechanisms and generic cellular requirements. No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression findings were used. Every supplied term was judged individually; high scores do not imply that the process is an effective defense in every species or age class.

## Host injury and predicted compensation

Early Philornis downsi larvae invade the nares and other nestling tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, mainly at night. Their blood feeding causes substantial blood loss and anemia, while tissue consumption produces skin lesions and lasting naris/beak deformity. Experimental parasite reductions improve fledging and can improve growth. These observations strongly motivate hematopoiesis/erythrocyte differentiation, iron homeostasis, hemostasis, hypoxia responses and epithelial repair. The latter mechanisms are physiological predictions, rather than demonstrations that a particular molecular pathway mediates tolerance. General metabolism, redox protection, growth and cell survival are supporting processes with less specific links. Development of unrelated organs, meiosis and housekeeping RNA/DNA processes have little specific relationship.

## Immune resistance

Koop et al. experimentally found parasite-induced fly-binding antibodies in adult female medium ground finches. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but did not rescue nestling survival. Males and nestlings did not show the corresponding induced defense. Thus adaptive immunity is directly relevant, with B-cell and T-cell processes as components. Innate sensing, inflammation, cytokines, phagocytosis and complement are plausible supporting defenses, but should not all receive the score reserved for directly demonstrated mechanisms. Virus-specific pathways have no special connection to this extracellular fly; antibacterial defenses have an indirect potential connection through damaged barriers, without assuming documented bacterial transmission.

- Koop et al. (2013), experimental immune/behavioral study: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

## Tolerance and behavior

Parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging; begging correlated with parental provisioning, consistent with nutritional compensation and tolerance despite parasite densities comparable to finches. Medium ground finches in that experiment did not show compensatory increases in provisioning, begging or glucose. Energy homeostasis and nutrient responses therefore have strong relevance; generic metabolic enzymes or neural development are less specific. This result does not demonstrate a particular insulin or mitochondrial signaling mechanism.

- Knutie et al. (2016), experimental comparison of mockingbirds and finches: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/

Preening, scratching, larval removal and parental pecking at nares have been observed, although effectiveness and frequency vary. Flycatcher nest cameras recorded removal behaviors and adult predation on flies. These observations motivate sensory and motor support, while generic synapse assembly is a weak link. Avoidance through nest placement/timing and movement away from larvae is discussed in the literature, with limited evidence for adaptive efficacy. Finch feather anointing with Psidium leaves is documented and the plant has repellent activity, but field protection against P. downsi remains uncertain; insecticide-treated nesting material is an assisted control intervention, not evidence that endogenous host detoxification is a natural fly defense.
