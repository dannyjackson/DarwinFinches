# Literature basis for independent a priori scores

I reviewed non-genomic published accounts of Philornis downsi host damage, experimental parasite removal, immune assays, and host behavior. I used no genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression findings, and no other rater information. Scores concern potential physiological relevance, not whether any term has actually experienced selection.

## Mechanisms

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Adult flies are free-living. Early larvae occupy nestling nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed on blood and tissue, largely at night. Myiasis, skin wounds, anemia, impaired growth, and substantial nestling mortality are central effects. Naris damage can persist into adulthood. These findings make erythrocyte replacement and iron handling, hemostasis, tissue repair, epithelial integrity, and compensation for reduced oxygen delivery strong candidates. Wounding and erythropoiesis directly match the phenotype; individual coagulation, angiogenic, inflammatory, or metabolic pathways remain mechanistic predictions unless specifically demonstrated. Platelet GO terminology was interpreted through the analogous avian thrombocyte hemostatic function.

**Immune resistance.** Experimental manipulation demonstrated increased fly-binding antibodies in parasitized medium ground finch mothers. A tendency for stronger antibody responses to accompany fewer parasites suggested resistance, but did not demonstrate improved reproductive success. Males and young nestlings did not show corresponding induced responses in that experiment. Another finch/mockingbird comparison found no significant antibody-mediated response. Thus immune and adaptive immune processes have a direct literature connection, B-cell machinery is a core component, and innate recognition, inflammatory mediators, complement, and phagocytosis are biologically plausible predictions rather than established effective defenses. Virus-specific processes have little particular connection to this extracellular fly; bacterial defenses have a supporting connection through breached tissue, without assuming documented bacterial transmission.

**Behavioral resistance and tolerance.** Nest sanitation, parental or nestling preening, occasional larval removal, agitation, and attempted avoidance have been observed, but are inconsistent and limited in very young nestlings. Fumigated cotton can recruit nest-building behavior into parasite control; it does not establish natural insecticide detoxification as an evolved response. Mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with parental food delivery, and could tolerate burdens that reduced finch reproductive success. Across years, mockingbird tolerance declined with drought and limited resources. Food acquisition, energy balance, nutrient availability, and growth compensation therefore have specific relevance. Broad neuronal, synaptic, or developmental terms are much less informative than a named defensive behavior and were not promoted solely because behavior needs neurons.

**Stress and secondary consequences.** An experimental study did not find the proposed corticosterone-mediated response in breeding female finches; nestling stress was unresolved. Hormonal stress, redox balance, and cellular survival are supporting possibilities, not proven fly-specific mechanisms. Development of unrelated organs, reproduction-specific cellular processes, meiosis, and generic information-processing machinery generally provide no specific or only weak links. Oxygen, energy, epithelial, and repair pathways were judged on their physiological roles rather than assumed molecular results.

Directional regulation was assessed for its relevance to balancing the response, not assumed beneficial. No target score distribution was used.
