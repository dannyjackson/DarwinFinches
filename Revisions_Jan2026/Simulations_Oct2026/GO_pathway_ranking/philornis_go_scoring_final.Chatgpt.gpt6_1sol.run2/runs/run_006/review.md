# A priori review: host responses to Philornis downsi

## Scope and scoring approach

I reviewed ecological, behavioral, parasitological, and immunological literature using web searches before assigning the scores. No transcriptomic, gene-expression, or genomic results from Galápagos birds informed these judgments; a search result for a paper involving gene expression was excluded. Every supplied term was considered individually. Scores describe physiological relevance, not evidence that a particular gene is selected, and no score distribution was targeted.

I reserved 5 for terms directly encompassing reported responses, including the experimentally demonstrated immune response, responses to injury, and compensatory energy homeostasis. Scores of 4 cover core immune components and strong predictions from blood loss or tissue injury, including erythropoiesis, hemostasis, epithelial repair, and hypoxia responses. Scores of 3 indicate plausible metabolic, cellular, neural, or tissue-level support. Generic machinery or remote links receive 2; processes without a specific mechanistic connection receive 1. A mechanism need not be effective or universally present to be relevant. In particular, maternal immune responses cannot be assumed to occur in nestlings.

## Mechanisms identified

**Feeding injury and physiological tolerance.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; older larvae largely hide in nest material and feed externally, especially at night. Blood and tissue loss cause anemia, reduced hemoglobin, wounds, impaired growth, and mortality. Naris and beak malformations can persist beyond the nestling stage. Strong a priori responses therefore include replacing erythrocytes, maintaining iron availability and circulation, limiting bleeding, repairing epithelial and connective tissue, and responding to reduced oxygen delivery. Specific molecular components of these responses are predictions from the pathology rather than mechanisms all directly measured in these birds.

**Resistance through immunity.** Experimental parasite removal demonstrated increased fly-binding antibodies in parasitized medium ground finch mothers. Higher antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but the response did not rescue reproductive success in that experiment. Males and young nestlings did not show the same response. Antibody production and adaptive immunity are therefore directly relevant; B-cell functions and T-cell assistance are core supporting mechanisms. The literature proposes inflammatory skin responses, itch, and host defensive molecules as ways to interfere with blood feeding, but these particular effector pathways should not be treated as experimentally established in finch nestlings. Innate recognition, cytokines, complement, and phagocytosis are plausible defenses or injury responses. Antibacterial processes have an additional, indirect connection through damaged tissue; the fly does not establish a specific antiviral mechanism.

**Tolerance through food and energy compensation.** In a comparative experiment, parasitized Galápagos mockingbirds increased begging and parental provisioning, maintaining reproductive success despite parasite loads. Medium ground finches did not show this compensation. Subsequent work showed that mockingbird tolerance varied among years and weakened under drought, implicating food availability. Nutrient sensing, energy reserves, carbohydrate and lipid use, growth maintenance, and thermal balance are relevant supporting processes, but ubiquitous metabolism alone is not direct evidence of an antiparasite mechanism.

**Behavioral resistance and its constraints.** Preening, scratching, nest sanitation, larval removal, agitation, and repositioning have been observed or proposed, with limited or inconsistent evidence of effective protection. Very young nestlings have limited motor capacity. Altered brooding can have thermoregulatory costs. Galápagos flycatcher observations also document defensive interactions with adult flies and larvae. Sensory detection, movement, learning, and timing can support these behaviors, whereas an arbitrary neuronal development or synaptic subtype term has only a weak or absent specific connection. Finch feather treatment with guayabillo leaves is a proposed self-medication mechanism: extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth experimentally, but protection from the behavior in wild birds remains unproven.
