# Independent a priori physiological review

## Scope and scoring
I reviewed ecological, behavioral, parasitological and physiological literature using web search, then assessed all supplied terms individually using that literature and general biological knowledge. No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression results were used. An urban-finches gene-expression paper appeared incidentally in search results and was excluded in full. No other local input or rater output was consulted. Scores describe plausible association with a host response, not evidence that a process actually evolved or successfully protects birds. Broad housekeeping functions receive weak scores even when indispensable to living cells. Positive and negative regulation can both matter; direction alone does not establish protection. No target score distribution was used.

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood loss and compensation.** Larvae consume blood and host tissues, with early stages occupying nestling nares and later stages feeding from the nest, often at night. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer and Fessl (2006) found lower hemoglobin with greater parasite intensity, more immature erythrocytes with greater parasitism and lower hemoglobin, and survival costs. This directly supports erythrocyte production and strongly predicts hematopoietic progenitor activity, iron homeostasis and hypoxia responses; metabolic and cardiovascular adjustments are supporting inferences. Source: *Effects of the introduced ectoparasite Philornis downsi on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's Small Ground Finch*, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x .

**Tissue injury and repair.** Feeding damages nares and soft tissue; deformities may persist beyond fledging and impair later function. Wound responses, hemostasis, epithelial renewal, keratinocyte activity, extracellular matrix/collagen repair and vascular repair therefore have strong a priori links. These detailed repair pathways are predictions from documented damage, not measured molecular findings. Sources: O'Connor, Robertson and Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086 ; Kleindorfer et al. (2022), *Functional traits and foraging behaviour: Avian vampire fly larvae change the beak and fitness of their Darwin's finch hosts*, https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.14061 .

**Immune resistance and its limitations.** Koop et al. (2013) experimentally detected parasite-binding antibody induction in mothers at parasitized nests. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but did not prevent complete nestling loss in the parasitized nests studied. Induction was not detected in fathers or nestlings in that experiment. Adaptive immune response is directly supported; B-cell activation/differentiation, T-cell help and immune regulation are core predictions. Innate recognition, complement, cytokines, inflammation, phagocytosis and leukocyte recruitment are plausible strong predictions for tissue-feeding ectoparasites, without claiming demonstrated efficacy against this fly. Bacterium-specific defenses are relevant to breached barriers but not evidence that the fly transmits a pathogen; virus-specific responses receive weak scores. Source: *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*, https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651 .

**Behavioral resistance and tolerance.** Video observations documented preening, ingestion of larvae, avoidance by standing on nestmates, and maternal probing of nares and nest material. These make sensory, motor and learning processes potentially supportive, but do not specifically implicate every neuronal developmental or synaptic pathway. Mockingbirds can tolerate parasitism through increased begging and parental provisioning; greater body size is a proposed contributor. Tolerance varies with resource conditions and declines in dry years. Nutrition, energy homeostasis, growth and mobilization of reserves are consequently relevant supporting mechanisms. Sources: O'Connor et al. (2010), above; Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ .

**Additional limits.** Insecticide-treated cotton incorporated by finches can reduce parasites, demonstrating an experimentally enabled behavioral control route rather than intrinsic detoxification-based fly resistance (Knutie et al. 2014, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058). Endocrine stress is a hypothesis with limited direct support: an experiment did not find parasite-treatment differences in baseline or stress-induced maternal corticosterone (Knutie et al. 2013, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/). Oxidative stress, autophagy, apoptosis and intracellular stress responses are generally downstream predictions, not established specific mechanisms. Reproductive meiosis, unrelated embryonic organ patterning, UV/radiation-specific processes and specialized neuronal development generally lack a specific link. Avian functional context was considered for mammal-worded annotations such as platelet processes. GO:0160240 was supplied without an informative name, could not be identified in the targeted search, and conservatively receives 1 rather than an invented mechanism.
