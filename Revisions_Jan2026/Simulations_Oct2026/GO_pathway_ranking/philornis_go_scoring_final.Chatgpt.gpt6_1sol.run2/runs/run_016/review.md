# A priori physiological relevance of GO processes to Philornis downsi

## Literature basis

Only the supplied term list was read locally. Scores were assigned independently from the literature below and general biology; no Galapagos bird genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results were used.

**Blood feeding and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nestling nares and other tissues; older larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, mainly at night. Nasal damage, open skin lesions, substantial blood loss, anemia, impaired growth, and mortality establish direct relevance of responses to wounding and erythrocyte replacement. Hemostasis, epithelial repair, extracellular-matrix repair, angiogenesis, iron handling, and hypoxia responses are strong physiological predictions, rather than demonstrated molecular adaptations. Small ground finch observations link heavier infestation to lower hemoglobin and more immature erythrocytes.
- Fessl et al. (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Dudaniec et al. (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x

**Immune resistance.** Experimental parasite reduction demonstrated parasite-binding antibody responses in adult female medium ground finches, without corresponding detectable responses in males or young nestlings. The negative antibody–parasite-abundance association was marginal, so antibody production is documented but effective protection is not established. Adaptive immunity and B-cell machinery are consequently relevant; innate recognition, cytokines, inflammation, complement, and leukocyte recruitment are reasonable predictions from exposed wounds and feeding, not all directly demonstrated against this fly. A later comparison did not find significant antibody responses in either host species, emphasizing context dependence.
- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/
- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/

**Tolerance and energy allocation.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, correlated with greater parental provisioning; comparable compensation was absent in medium ground finches. Mockingbird tolerance weakened in dry years when provisioning was lower. Nutrient responses, energy homeostasis, metabolic compensation, growth, and tissue maintenance therefore have plausible physiological roles, without implying every metabolic pathway is parasite-specific. Maternal corticosterone experiments did not support stress mediation, so endocrine links remain less direct.
- Knutie et al. (2016), above.
- *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites* (2019): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/
- Knutie et al. (2013), *Experimental test ... on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin's finches*: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0016648013003134

**Behavioral defenses and lasting effects.** Preening, scratching, larval removal, altered brooding/posture, and adult predation on flies have been observed, with limited evidence of effective resistance. Sensation, motor function, learning, and activity timing can support these responses but generic neural development is indirect. Leaf anointing occurs and plant extracts repel flies experimentally; field protection remains a hypothesis, further qualified by frequent nonbreeding-season anointing. Naris damage can affect adult song, supporting a limited connection to craniofacial integrity and vocal behavior.
- *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/
- Tebbich et al. (2021), *Darwin's finches habitually anoint their feathers ... during the non-breeding season*: https://doi.org/10.1111/eth.13153
- *Introduced parasite changes host phenotype, mating signal and hybridization risk* (2019): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6571467/

## Scoring interpretation

Each term was judged independently: 5 for a directly represented documented host mechanism; 4 for a core component or strong physiological prediction; 3 for plausible supporting or downstream processes; 2 for indirect/speculative links; 1 for no specific connection. Broad housekeeping functions and unrelated embryonic or reproductive processes received low scores rather than automatic elevation because all responses require living cells. Antiviral terms were not treated as direct anti-fly mechanisms. Platelet-specific terms received supporting relevance through analogous avian thrombocyte functions, rather than implying birds have mammalian platelets. Directional regulation was assessed for potential relevance, not presumed benefit.

The input's unnamed GO:0160240 was identified through publicly indexed ontology records as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance and scored as general transcriptional quality control; its supplied identifier was preserved.
