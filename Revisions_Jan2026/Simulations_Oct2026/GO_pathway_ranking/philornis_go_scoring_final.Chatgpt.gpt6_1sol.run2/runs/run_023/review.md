# A priori physiological relevance review

## Host damage and responses

Philornis downsi adults are free living; the parasitic larvae attack nestling birds and can also feed on brooding females. Early instars occupy the nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, especially at night. Tissue destruction, wounds and substantial blood loss cause anemia, impaired growth and nestling mortality. Nasal injury can leave persistent malformations. These observations give strong prior links to erythrocyte replacement, iron handling, oxygen delivery, hemostasis, epithelial integrity and tissue repair; not every molecular component of these responses has been demonstrated experimentally.

Small ground finches with heavier infestations had lower hemoglobin and more immature erythrocytes. Thus erythropoietic compensation is a documented host response, whereas hypoxia signaling and vascular remodeling are predictions from the documented anemia.

An experimental study of medium ground finches found increased fly-binding antibodies in mothers at parasitized nests. The association between stronger antibodies and fewer parasites was suggestive rather than definitive evidence of resistance, and did not rescue nestling survival. Males and nestlings did not show the same induced response. A separate finch–mockingbird comparison did not find a significant antibody response in either species. Consequently adaptive immunity is directly relevant, but its protective efficacy is variable and cannot be assumed. Inflammation, complement, leukocyte recruitment and associated signaling are strong mechanistic candidates, not all demonstrated defenses against this particular fly. Wound-associated microbial defense is a supporting prediction, not evidence that the fly is a proven bacterial or viral vector.

Mockingbirds can tolerate parasitism through increased nestling begging and greater parental provisioning, compensating for damage without reducing parasite density. The comparison did not show equivalent compensation in medium ground finches. This supports energy balance and nutritional compensation, with intermediary metabolism and nutrient signaling as supporting processes.

Observed or proposed behavioral resistance includes larva removal/preening, avoidance within nests, nest placement and timing, and self-anointing with guayabillo leaves. Leaf extracts have been tested for repellency. Finch incorporation of supplied insecticide-treated cotton reduces infestation; this demonstrates usable nesting behavior, not an endogenous insecticide pathway. Reduced maternal brooding in parasitized nests can worsen nestling thermoregulation. Neural, sensory, motor and endocrine processes can support these behaviors, but generic neural development or ubiquitous signaling is less specifically linked.

## Scoring approach

Each supplied term was considered as a host process. Documented response classes received 5; core components and strong predictions generally received 4; plausible support or downstream physiology received 3. Broad cellular machinery and weak behavioral/metabolic connections generally received 2. Unrelated organ patterning, reproduction, specialized housekeeping, or pathogen-specific processes without a fly-specific rationale received 1. Directional regulation terms were judged for relevance, not assumed to be beneficial. No score distribution was targeted. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression or epigenomic results were used. One search hit mentioned molecular results; those results were excluded. GO:0160240 was identified through the GO reference as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, while its input identifier was preserved.
