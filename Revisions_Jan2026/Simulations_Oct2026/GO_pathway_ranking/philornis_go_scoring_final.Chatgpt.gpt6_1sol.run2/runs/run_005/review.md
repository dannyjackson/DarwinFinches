# Independent physiological relevance review

## Scope and scoring basis

I reviewed published non-genomic literature on *Philornis downsi*, host physiology and host behavior before assigning the scores. I did not use genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression or selection-study results. Scores reflect a priori relevance, not evidence that a particular GO pathway is selected, activated or beneficial in finches. Every input term was judged individually. General constitutive cellular processes do not receive a high score merely because an immune cell uses them. Negative regulation can be relevant through limiting damage as well as through permitting resistance. Avian biology matters: platelet terms are treated as analogues of thrombocyte hemostatic functions, rather than as proof of mammalian platelets in birds.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood feeding, anemia and tissue injury.** Early larvae invade nestling nares and other tissues; older larvae largely shelter in nest material and feed externally, especially at night. Blood loss, anemia, wounds, impaired growth, malformed nares and mortality are central effects. This makes responses to wounding directly relevant and predicts roles for hemostasis, erythrocyte replacement, iron handling, oxygen-stress responses, epithelial repair and extracellular matrix rebuilding. The latter mechanisms are predictions from documented injury, not demonstrated pathway-level responses. Sources: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; Koop et al. (2011), *Experimental Demonstration of the Fitness Consequences of an Introduced Parasite of Darwin's Finches*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3092749/ .

- **Immune resistance.** Experimental parasite removal showed parasite-binding antibody responses in adult female medium ground finches. Stronger maternal responses tended to accompany fewer parasites, but did not rescue fledging success in that experiment. Nestlings and males did not show the same induced response. Thus adaptive immunity and antibody-producing B-cell mechanisms have a particularly close connection; innate defenses, immune regulation, inflammation and cytokine signaling are strong or supporting predictions, with specificity distinguishing scores. A high relevance score does not assert an effective nestling immune defense. Source: Koop et al. (2014), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24567824/ .

- **Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Nest cameras documented preening, occasional larval consumption, standing on nestmates, maternal probing of nares and nest material, and disrupted rest. These observations support sensory, motor and behavioral capacities in principle, but broad neuronal development or synaptic machinery has only an indirect connection. Multiple wounds and infections also make antibacterial defenses plausible supporting processes, without treating the fly as a demonstrated viral pathogen. Source: O'Connor et al. (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086 .

- **Tolerance and energetic compensation.** Experiments comparing mockingbirds and medium ground finches linked mockingbird tolerance to increased nestling begging and parental provisioning; the same compensation was not found in finches. A four-season experiment showed that mockingbird tolerance declined in dry years, linking defense to food availability. Energy homeostasis therefore has a direct documented connection, while nutrient sensing, absorption/transport, glucose regulation, lipid use and mitochondrial energy production are supporting processes of varying specificity. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ .

- **Other proposed defenses and important negative evidence.** Finches collect experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton, allowing nest self-fumigation. Leaf rubbing with *Psidium galapageium* has been observed, and extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth; a natural anti-fly fitness benefit remains a hypothesis. These findings support potential behavioral avoidance without implying endogenous insecticide biosynthesis. A maternal corticosterone experiment did not support elevated maternal physiological stress as the explanation for reduced reproduction, so generic hormone and stress pathways were not automatically treated as documented successful defenses. Sources: Knutie et al. (2014), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/ ; Cimadom et al. (2016), https://www.nature.com/articles/srep34559 ; Knutie et al. (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/ .

For broader synthesis, McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi, an Introduced Parasite of Galapagos Birds*, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 , distinguishes avoidance, resistance and tolerance and emphasizes limited evidence of effective behavioral or nestling immune resistance. Broad developmental processes, germline processes and routine nucleic-acid processing have no specific connection unless the term itself identifies a response-relevant function. Oxidative stress, autophagy and apoptosis are plausible supporting or indirect processes, rather than fly-specific documented defenses.
