# A priori physiological review for Philornis downsi

This assessment uses published natural-history, experimental parasitology and behavioral evidence, together with general biological knowledge. It does not use Galápagos bird genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression results. A search incidentally returned an expression study; its results were excluded. All 1,008 input terms were considered, without a target score distribution.

## Host injury and consequences

Early larvae invade nestling nostrils and tissues; later larvae feed externally, mainly at night, from the nest material. Direct effects include blood loss, anemia, tissue injury and high nestling mortality. These provide strong a priori grounds for blood replacement, iron handling, oxygen-stress responses, coagulation, epithelial repair and wound healing, although those compensatory pathways should not be confused with experimentally demonstrated molecular defenses. Parasite-reduction experiments document reduced growth and fledging in parasitized finches. Persistent naris damage can change song, giving a specific but indirect connection to sensory and behavioral processes.

## Resistance and immune mechanisms

Experimental removal of flies showed elevated fly-binding antibodies in adult female medium ground finches at parasitized nests; higher antibody levels tended to accompany fewer parasites. This response did not rescue offspring survival, and corresponding induced responses were not detected in males or young nestlings. Antibody-mediated immunity therefore receives the strongest direct connection; B-cell functions and adaptive immune activation are core components. Inflammation, skin barriers, innate recognition, cytokines, complement and cellular clearance are plausible proposed or supporting defenses, not evidence of proven successful resistance. Secondary bacterial defense has a supporting link through damaged tissue; antiviral pathways have no specific demonstrated fly-response link.

Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

## Tolerance, energy and behavior

Experiments comparing mockingbirds with finches found mockingbird tolerance associated with increased nestling begging and parental provisioning. Compensation varied among years and weakened during drought. This supports energy homeostasis and nutrient utilization as potential tolerance mechanisms without establishing every metabolic pathway as a specific fly response. Behavioral observations include agitation, preening and altered brooding; very young nestlings have limited motor ability. Decreased brooding may worsen thermoregulation. Finch anointing with guava leaves and laboratory repellent effects suggest possible behavioral self-medication, but fitness protection in the wild remains unproven.

## Limits and scoring interpretation

A corticosterone experiment in breeding female finches did not find a parasite-treatment effect on baseline or induced corticosterone, condition or hematocrit. Thus steroid/stress signaling was not treated as an established protective mechanism. Broad cellular machinery, neural development, embryonic patterning and reproductive-cell processes were not elevated merely because the parasite damages developing birds. Generic signaling/transport generally has only weak indirect relevance; energy compensation, repair support and stress mitigation can merit intermediate scores. Scores measure potential association, including damage and tolerance, not proof of adaptation or improved survival. Positive and negative regulators were assessed for the physiological process they regulate, rather than assuming that increased activity is beneficial.
