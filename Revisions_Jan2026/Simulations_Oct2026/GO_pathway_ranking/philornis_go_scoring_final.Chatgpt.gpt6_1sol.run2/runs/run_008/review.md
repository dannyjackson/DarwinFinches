# Independent literature basis for scores

## Host injury and candidate responses

Philornis downsi larvae feed on nestling blood and tissue. Early instars enter the nares and sometimes other cavities; later larvae usually shelter in nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Documented consequences include wounds, enlarged nasal openings, blood loss/anemia, impaired growth, and mortality. These provide strong physiological predictions for hemostasis, erythrocyte replacement and iron handling, oxygen-stress responses, epithelial protection, and tissue repair. The latter mechanisms are predictions from the injuries unless directly measured; their molecular components were not assumed to have been demonstrated in these birds.

Resistance can include acquired immunity. An experimental study found increased fly-binding antibodies in female medium ground finches at parasitized nests, with a marginal inverse association between antibody level and parasite abundance. Males and young nestlings did not show the same induced response, and maternal responses did not rescue reproductive success. Accordingly, adaptive immunity is directly relevant, while B-cell and T-cell machinery are core predicted components. Innate immunity, inflammatory regulation, complement, cytokines, and phagocytic clearance are credible supporting mechanisms rather than proof of effective resistance. Bacterial-defense pathways have an indirect wound-associated rationale; virus-specific responses have no specific connection established by this review.

Tolerance need not reduce fly abundance. Parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with increased parental provisioning, and tolerated burdens harmful to medium ground finches. Finch compensation was not universal. Resource-dependent tolerance also varied among years in mockingbirds. Energy balance, nutrient acquisition, glucose and lipid metabolism, growth, and stress physiology therefore have plausible roles, with molecular specificity determining the score. Anemia compensation, wound repair, and maintenance of growth were distinguished from demonstrated changes in feeding behavior.

Behavioral defenses documented or proposed include maternal and nestling preening, probing infested nest material or nares, agitation and movement away from feeding larvae, and altered brooding. Their effectiveness is limited and age dependent. Larval disturbance also suggests sleep/circadian and sensory contributions. Finch application of Psidium galapageium leaves to feathers is documented; extracts repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth experimentally, but a protective effect of the behavior in wild nests was proposed rather than established. Sensory, motor, learning, and neuroendocrine processes can support such behaviors, but generic neuronal development is substantially less specific than behavior itself. Artificially supplied insecticidal nest material represents a conservation intervention, not evidence for endogenous insecticide production by birds.

## Scoring interpretation

Scores reflect potential association, not proof of adaptation, direction of effect, benefit, or selection. Broad host immune/defense responses and response to wounding received direct-relevance scores; core repair, hemostatic, erythropoietic, and adaptive-immune components received strong-prediction scores. Supporting metabolic, cellular, vascular, inflammatory, and behavioral processes were judged individually. Generic housekeeping, developmental, reproductive, and unrelated pathogen pathways were not promoted merely because they occur in living organisms. No score distribution was targeted.

Only the specified input file was read locally. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results from Galapagos birds were used. A search incidentally surfaced a mixed phenotype/gene-expression publication; it was excluded entirely. GO:0160240 was identified from the official GO report as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance and scored without changing the input ID.
