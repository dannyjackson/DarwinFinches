# A priori relevance to Philornis downsi

Scores are based on published natural-history, experimental parasitology and behavioral evidence plus general biology. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression or selection results were used. Every supplied term was considered individually; no score distribution was targeted. A potentially relevant process need not be an effective defense, or operate in every species or life stage.

## Mechanisms and key sources

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nares and other tissues; later instars inhabit nest material and feed externally on host blood, often at night. Tissue destruction, anemia and mortality make blood replacement directly relevant and strongly predict hemostatic, oxygen-compensatory, iron-management and wound-repair mechanisms. These predicted mechanisms are not all demonstrated defenses. Source: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/

**Blood formation and growth.** Small ground finches with greater parasite intensity had lower hemoglobin and increased immature erythrocytes, supporting compensatory erythropoiesis. Experimental parasite reduction improved growth and fledging. Nutritional and metabolic support therefore matters, but each specialized metabolic pathway has a less direct connection. Sources: Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x ; *An experimental study on the effects of an introduced parasite in Darwin's finches* (2006): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0006320705002983

**Immune resistance.** Experimental parasitism increased parasite-binding antibodies in medium ground finch mothers; fathers and nestlings lacked this induced response. Stronger maternal responses tended to accompany fewer parasites, but did not rescue nestling survival. Adaptive immunity and B-cell processes consequently have strong relevance. Innate recognition, inflammatory regulation, cytokines and leukocyte functions are strong mechanistic predictions rather than proven effective larval clearance. Negative regulation may control injury and immune costs. Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

**Behavioral tolerance.** Parasitized mockingbirds increased begging and parental food delivery, consistent with nutritional compensation, whereas finches did not show equivalent responses. Mockingbird tolerance varied among years and was reduced under drought. This supports energy homeostasis and nutritional compensation, without establishing every metabolic or neural pathway as fly-specific. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Preening, occasional larval removal, nest sanitation, scratching and repositioning have been observed, but protection is limited and age dependent. Females may reduce brooding and stand more, potentially avoiding feeding larvae while imposing thermal costs on young. Motor, sensory, learning and circadian terms are indirect unless they identify these behaviors specifically. Sources: Koop et al. (2013), above; *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests* (2021): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/

**Proposed self-medication and lasting injury.** Finches rub Psidium leaves on feathers; laboratory repellency supports a proposed antiparasite role, without demonstrating protection of nestlings in nature. Nasal deformation can affect adult song and pairing, supporting tissue integrity and repair more directly than generic neural development. Sources: Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/ ; Kleindorfer et al. (2019), *Introduced parasite changes host phenotype, mating signal and hybridization risk*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6571467/

A synthesis consulted for avoidance, resistance and tolerance distinctions was McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi*: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103
