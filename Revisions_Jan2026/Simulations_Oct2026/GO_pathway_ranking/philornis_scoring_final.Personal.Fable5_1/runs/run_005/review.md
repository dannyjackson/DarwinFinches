# Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (run_005)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- *P. downsi* adults lay eggs in nests; first-instar larvae live inside nestlings' nares (and ear canals / body cavities), feeding on blood and tissue; second/third instars shelter in the nest base by day and feed externally on nestlings (and brooding females) at night (Fessl/Kleindorfer group; McNew et al. 2019; Knutie et al. 2016).
- Blood loss: nestlings may lose a substantial fraction of blood volume; parasitised nestlings have lower haemoglobin (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006 Austral Ecology; Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019) and lower plasma glucose, slower growth and lower body mass.
- Tissue damage: wounds/lesions on skin; enlarged, deformed nares (~106% larger in parasitised chicks; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016), keratin and soft tissue of the beak chewed; surviving adults have altered song and lower pairing success (Kleindorfer et al. 2019).
- Mortality: in-nest nestling mortality ~50% on average, up to 100%; chicks die at younger ages as infestation intensity has risen (Kleindorfer et al. 2014). Adult females: no detectable effect on corticosterone, haematocrit or body condition (Knutie et al. 2013).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. **Adaptive/humoral immunity (resistance)** – medium ground finch adults, especially brooding females, produce *P. downsi*-specific IgY antibodies (Huber et al. 2010); mothers at parasitised nests have higher antibody levels and mothers with higher antibodies tend to have fewer larvae in the nest, though this did not rescue fledging (Koop et al. 2013). Knutie et al. 2016 found no significant antibody response in finch or mockingbird nestlings. Implies adaptive immune response, B cell activation/differentiation, cytokine signalling, immune regulation.
2. **Innate/inflammatory response at feeding sites** – larval feeding produces lesions in nares and skin; inflammation, leukocyte chemotaxis, phagocytosis, TLR/pattern-recognition, complement, TNF/IL-1/IL-6/IL-8 signalling, and oxidative burst are the generic vertebrate response to blood-feeding ectoparasites and secondary bacterial infection of wounds.
3. **Tolerance via blood/energy compensation** – lowered haemoglobin and glucose imply erythropoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia responses, haemostasis/coagulation, and energy metabolism (glucose homeostasis, lipid mobilisation, insulin signalling, starvation/nutrient responses). Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly: parasitised nestlings beg more and parents provision more, offsetting energy loss; tolerance declines in dry years when provisioning is low (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Finches do not increase begging/provisioning.
4. **Tissue repair** – wound healing, epidermis/skin and keratinocyte processes, extracellular matrix and collagen organisation, angiogenesis, epithelial migration; cartilage/bone processes for naris/beak morphology.
5. **Behavioural defences** – nestlings preen, eat larvae, crowd onto nestmates; females probe nares and nest base (video studies, O'Connor et al.); parents brood less/stand on rim; more reactive nestlings have more deformed nares (Common et al. 2021); finches readily incorporate novel nest material and self-fumigate with permethrin-treated cotton (Knutie et al. 2014). Nervous-system terms were given low scores (behaviour is a weak, indirect genetic link); nociception/pain perception scored moderately.
6. **Stress physiology** – glucocorticoid response tested and not altered in adult females (Knutie et al. 2013); hormone/steroid-receptor terms scored weakly.
7. **Growth impairment** – reduced nestling growth implies IGF/insulin, TOR/nutrient-sensing and cell-growth processes as supporting mechanisms.

Terms not connected to blood loss, tissue damage, immunity, energy balance, growth or repair (e.g. neural development, cell-cycle machinery, RNA processing, basic transcription/translation, cilia, cardiac/limb morphogenesis) were scored 1.
