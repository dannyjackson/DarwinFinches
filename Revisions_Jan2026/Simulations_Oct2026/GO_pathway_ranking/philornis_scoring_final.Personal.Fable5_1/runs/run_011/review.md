# Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (run_011)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- *P. downsi* larvae are semi-hematophagous nest parasites. First instars live in the nares and ear canals of nestlings; later instars live in the nest base and emerge mainly at dusk/night to pierce the skin and feed on blood and tissue fluids (Fessl et al.; O'Connor et al. 2010; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Common & Kleindorfer 2019 chapter).
- Documented consequences: blood loss and reduced haemoglobin/haematocrit (anaemia) in nestlings (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006; Knutie et al. 2016); skin lesions/wounds; enlarged/deformed nares that persist into adulthood and alter beak and song (Kleindorfer et al.); reduced growth (mass, tarsus, primary feather length); nestling mortality typically 16-100% (often >50%); larvae also feed on brooding females through the brood patch (Huber et al. 2010; Knutie et al. 2013) and sometimes on adults subcutaneously; nocturnal feeding may disrupt nestling sleep and energy balance.

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. **Resistance – adaptive immunity.** Adult *Geospiza fortis* (especially brooding females) produce *P. downsi*-specific antibodies (Huber et al. 2010); higher antibody levels associated with lower parasite intensity in some studies but not with higher fledging success (Koop et al.). Nestlings and mockingbirds show weak/no antibody response (Knutie et al. 2016). Innate immunity (complement, white-cell responses) proposed but untested (Knutie et al. 2017 Tobago study).
2. **Resistance – inflammatory/wound responses.** Skin penetration implies local inflammation, haemostasis, wound healing and re-epithelialisation; these are implicit in all descriptions of lesions, though not experimentally dissected in finches.
3. **Tolerance – compensatory energetics.** Galapagos mockingbird nestlings beg more when parasitised and parents provision more, compensating for energy/blood loss; Darwin's finches do not show this (Knutie et al. 2016). Blood replacement (erythropoiesis) was considered as a tolerance mechanism but parasitised birds still had lower haemoglobin. Urban/high-food environments reduce lethal effects (Knutie lab, result details not used).
4. **Physiological stress.** Corticosterone (baseline and stress-induced), body condition and haematocrit of brooding females did not differ with parasitism (Knutie et al. 2013); hormone response remains a plausible but unsupported mechanism.
5. **Behavioural defences.** Nestling activity/responsiveness predicts naris deformation under high parasitism (Kleindorfer lab 2023); nestling avoidance of larvae (O'Connor et al.); females in parasitised nests stand more while brooding; allopreening and nest-rim standing proposed (Knutie et al. 2016); nest-material choice (self-fumigation exploitation).

## Scoring logic
- 5: immune system/defence/inflammatory response, wound healing, response to wounding, B-cell/antibody biology, erythropoiesis.
- 4: core components of these (cytokine/TNF/IL-6/IL-8/IL-17/TLR/NF-kB/JAK-STAT signalling, T-cell activation, haemopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, coagulation/platelets, complement, skin/epidermis/keratinocyte and ECM/collagen repair, fibroblast proliferation, TGF-beta, energy homeostasis).
- 3: supporting processes (angiogenesis/endothelial migration, chemotaxis/phagocytosis, ROS/oxidative stress handling, glucose/nutrient/starvation responses, hormone signalling, mechanical/pain perception, epithelial differentiation, growth-factor signalling for repair).
- 2: weak/indirect (general lipid/carbohydrate metabolism, generic adhesion/migration, apoptosis, circadian, behaviour-related neural terms, craniofacial/skeletal development relating to naris deformation).
- 1: no specific link (housekeeping, cell cycle, RNA processing, neural wiring, organogenesis unrelated to skin/blood/immune).
