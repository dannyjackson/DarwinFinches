# Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (run_002)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- *P. downsi* larvae are obligate nest parasites. First (and some second) instars feed inside the nares of nestlings; later instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings and, to a lesser extent, brooding females (Fessl et al. 2006; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review).
- Documented costs: blood loss and anaemia (haemoglobin ~28% lower in parasitised nestlings, ~0.8 g/dL per larva; nestlings losing ~25% blood volume die within hours), reduced growth (~56% lower body mass, ~30% shorter feathers), naris/beak malformation that persists to adulthood and alters song, and in-nest mortality averaging ~55% (up to 100%) in Darwin's finches (Fessl et al. 2006; Dudaniec et al. 2006; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Koop et al. 2011; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016).
- Adult brooding females: no detectable change in body condition, haematocrit, or baseline/stress-induced corticosterone (Knutie et al. 2013).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. **Immune (resistance)**: Medium ground finches produce *P. downsi*-specific antibodies (IgY, measured by ELISA); levels higher during nesting and in females, and experimentally induced by parasitism (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). In later work neither finches nor mockingbirds mounted a strong antibody response, and the response is not clearly protective (Knutie et al. 2016). Implied components: B-cell activation and antibody production, innate inflammatory responses at feeding wounds (cytokines, complement, phagocytes, Th2-type anti-ectoparasite responses), and defence against secondary bacterial infection of wounds.
2. **Tolerance (energetic compensation)**: Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly: parasitised nestlings beg more and parents provision more, offsetting damage; finches do not. Tolerance depends on food availability (rainfall), i.e., on the host's ability to replace lost energy and blood (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Implied physiology: energy/glucose homeostasis, nutrient sensing (insulin/IGF/TOR), growth regulation, lipid stores, thermoregulation, and regenerative erythropoiesis/iron handling to recover haemoglobin.
3. **Tissue repair**: feeding wounds in nares and skin require wound healing, haemostasis, epidermal/keratinocyte regeneration (also feather growth), extracellular-matrix remodelling and angiogenesis.
4. **Stress physiology**: corticosterone measured (no effect in females), but stress/glucocorticoid signalling is a plausible nestling response.
5. **Behavioural**: nestling preening and eating of larvae, nestlings standing on siblings, female probing of the nest base and reduced brooding, increased begging, and self-medication (rubbing *Psidium galapageium* leaves, which repel adult flies and impair larval growth) (O'Connor et al. 2010, 2014; Cimadom et al. 2016). These map weakly to GO terms (nociception, nervous system processes, learning, xenobiotic responses).

## Scoring logic
5 = immune/inflammatory response, adaptive/B-cell responses, wound healing, erythrocyte differentiation (anaemia recovery). 4 = regulation of immunity/cytokines, complement, haemopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, coagulation, energy/glucose homeostasis, starvation/nutrient responses, IGF signalling, epidermis/skin/keratinocyte development, stress response. 3 = supporting pathways (NF-kB, JAK-STAT, TLR, specific cytokines, phagocytosis, chemotaxis, ROS, metabolic fuel pathways, TOR/insulin, ECM/collagen, angiogenesis, hormone signalling, nociception, thermogenesis). 2 = indirect (MAPK, apoptosis, autophagy, generic growth/migration, antiviral, heat/ER stress). 1 = no specific link (cell cycle, DNA repair, transcription/RNA processing, cilia, neural/organ development, etc.). Transcriptomic/genomic studies of Galápagos birds were not used.
