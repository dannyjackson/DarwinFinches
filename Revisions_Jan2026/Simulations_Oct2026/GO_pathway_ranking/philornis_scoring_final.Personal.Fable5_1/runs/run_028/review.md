# Run 028 – Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi*

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies oviposit in nests; first-instar larvae live inside the nares (and ear canals) of nestlings, feeding internally; second/third instars live in the nest base and emerge at night (dusk/dawn) to pierce the skin and feed on blood and tissue fluids of nestlings and, occasionally, brooding females.
- Consequences documented: heavy blood loss with reduced haemoglobin/haematocrit (anaemia), lower plasma glucose, reduced body mass and feather growth, very high in-nest mortality (often >80 % in unlined/sham nests; fledging success doubled when parasites were removed by fumigation or insecticide-treated nest liners), enlarged/deformed nares that persist to adulthood and can impair song, and possible sleep disruption from nocturnal feeding. Proposed mechanisms of harm: energy drain from anaemia, reduced begging, parental under-provisioning.
- Adult females: no measurable effect of parasitism on corticosterone, body condition or haematocrit (females can leave the nest; nestlings cannot).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. **Adaptive/antibody (resistance)** – brooding female medium ground finches mount *P. downsi*-specific antibody (IgY/IgG-like) responses; higher antibody levels associated with lower parasite load but not with higher fledging success. Nestling finches and mockingbirds show little/no antibody response (immature immune system). Immune responses of nestlings suggested to affect larval development.
2. **Wound/skin repair and inflammation** – larvae create skin lesions and naris tissue damage; wound healing, epidermal regeneration, local inflammation, haemostasis and coagulation at feeding sites are the obvious a priori local responses.
3. **Tolerance / compensation** – blood loss implies compensatory erythropoiesis and iron handling; energy drain implies glucose/energy homeostasis and nutrition-dependent tolerance. Mockingbirds tolerate the fly (no fitness loss) partly via begging-driven parental provisioning (energy compensation), whereas finches show no change in begging, provisioning or plasma glucose; green warbler-finch parents do not compensate (females even feed less).
4. **Stress endocrinology** – corticosterone (glucocorticoid) axis tested in adults (no effect) but proposed for nestlings.
5. **Behaviour** – nestling avoidance of larvae and nestling responsiveness (more responsive nestlings have larger nares), parental brooding posture (standing upright), reduced parental effort/trade-off, song effects of beak deformation, nocturnal feeding affecting sleep/circadian activity.
6. **Secondary infection and oxidative stress** – open wounds and anaemia plausibly entail antibacterial defence and oxidative/hypoxic stress responses (supporting processes).

## Scoring logic
5 = antibody/B-cell response and wound/injury response (documented). 4 = core immune, inflammatory and cytokine machinery, skin/epidermal development, haematopoiesis/erythrocyte differentiation, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, coagulation, glucose/energy homeostasis. 3 = supporting processes: angiogenesis, ECM/collagen, cell migration/chemotaxis, phagocytosis, hormone/glucocorticoid signalling, insulin/growth-factor signalling, starvation/nutrient responses, oxidative stress, circadian, pain, antibacterial defence. 2 = indirect (general metabolism, generic signalling cascades, adhesion, skeletal/craniofacial development, behavioural/cognition terms). 1 = no specific link.

Transcriptomic/genomic studies of Galápagos birds (e.g. bioRxiv 2023.03.06.531275, and the urban-finch expression study) were encountered but their results were not used.
