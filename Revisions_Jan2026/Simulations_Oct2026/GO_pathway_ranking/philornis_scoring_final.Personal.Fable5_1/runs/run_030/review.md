# Run 030 – literature review: host responses of Galápagos land birds to *Philornis downsi*

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Larvae are obligate haematophagous nest parasites. First instars live inside the nares of nestlings (subcutaneously), later instars feed externally at night on blood and tissue of the abdomen, wings, ears and nares; adults (brooding females) are sometimes fed on too (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Wikipedia summary).
- Blood loss: haemoglobin falls ~0.8 units per larva, ~28% lower in parasitized nestlings; nestlings losing ~25% of blood volume die within hours. Reduced mass, shorter feathers, slower growth; in-nest mortality averages ~55% (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019).
- Tissue damage: naris enlargement (~39%), upper-beak malformation that can persist to adulthood and may affect song; larvae in ear canals.
- Adult females showed no change in body condition, haematocrit or baseline/stress corticosterone in a wet year (Knutie et al. 2013).

## Documented or proposed host defenses
1. **Antibody (IgY) immune response** – medium ground finches mount a *P. downsi*-specific IgY response during nesting, higher in parasitized nests (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). The response is small and does not clearly reduce parasite load; Knutie et al. 2016 found no significant antibody response in finches or mockingbirds. Still, adaptive (B/T cell) and innate immunity (inflammation at bite/naris sites, cytokines, complement, phagocytes) are the primary physiological resistance candidates.
2. **Wound healing / tissue repair** – larval feeding wounds in skin, nares and beak imply epidermal repair, keratinocyte differentiation, ECM/collagen deposition, angiogenesis, bone/cartilage remodelling of the beak.
3. **Haematological compensation for blood loss** – erythropoiesis, hematopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia responses, coagulation/thrombocyte activation at feeding sites, blood-pressure regulation.
4. **Tolerance via energy compensation** – Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly: nestlings beg more, parents provision more; parasitized nestlings had lower plasma glucose. In finches tolerance varies with rainfall/food availability (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Predicts glucose/insulin/lipid metabolism, nutrient sensing (TOR), starvation responses and growth-factor signalling.
5. **Behavioural defenses** – nestling preening and eating larvae, standing on siblings, altered begging; females brood less and preen nestlings; self-fumigation with permethrin cotton; nest-site effects (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Knutie et al. 2014). These predict only weak links to nervous-system/sensory terms (pain, hearing, cognition) and to xenobiotic responses.
6. **Stress endocrine axis** – corticosterone was tested but not affected in females; hormone-response terms scored as supporting (3) only.
