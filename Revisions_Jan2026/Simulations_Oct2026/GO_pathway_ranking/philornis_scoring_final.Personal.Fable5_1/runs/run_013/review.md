# Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (run_013)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- First-instar larvae feed inside the nares of nestlings; later instars burrow subcutaneously (ears, belly, wings, nares) and feed on blood and tissue. Parasitized nestlings show enlarged nares, open lesions, swelling and internal bleeding (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review; Fessl et al.).
- Blood loss causes anemia: haemoglobin ~28% lower in parasitized nestlings; ~0.8 unit drop per larva; nestlings losing ~25% of blood die within hours. Body mass reduced ~56%, feather growth ~30%. In-nest mortality averages ~55%; survival drops ~2% per added larva.
- Naris deformation (39% larger nares) persists into adulthood in some survivors, with knock-on effects proposed for song, foraging and preening.
- Larvae also attempt to feed on brooding adult females (unfeathered brood patch).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Humoral/acquired immunity (resistance):** Adult medium ground finches (esp. females) show elevated anti-*Philornis* antibody (IgY) levels during nesting (Huber et al. 2010); experimental parasite manipulation induces an antibody response in adults (Koop et al. 2013). Nestling antibody responses were weak/undetectable (Knutie et al. 2016). Antibody-driven resistance is the clearest documented immune mechanism; local inflammation at feeding lesions (swelling) is also documented.
2. **Haematological compensation / tolerance:** Reduced haemoglobin is the principal measured physiological cost; erythropoiesis, iron handling, hypoxia responses and haemostasis (stopping bleeding from feeding wounds) are the direct physiological counterparts. Mockingbirds tolerate the parasite partly via increased begging and parental provisioning; tolerance declines in dry years when food is limited (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019), implicating nutrient/energy homeostasis and growth pathways in tolerance.
3. **Tissue damage repair:** Open lesions, subcutaneous burrowing and naris erosion imply wound healing, skin/epidermal repair, extracellular matrix and keratinocyte processes, and defense against secondary bacterial infection of wounds.
4. **Stress physiology:** Corticosterone (baseline and stress-induced) measured in parasitized females showed no treatment effect (Knutie et al. 2013), but the stress axis is a studied candidate.
5. **Behavioral defenses:** Nestlings stand on siblings to escape larvae at the nest base, preen and eat larvae; females probe nest base, preen nestlings, eat larvae; finches rub *Psidium galapageium* leaves into plumage, which repels adult flies and impairs larval growth (Cimadom et al. 2016). These are behavioral and mostly not captured by cellular GO terms (nociception/pain perception is the most direct physiological link).

## Scoring logic
- 5: immune response, adaptive/humoral immunity, B cell activation/differentiation, inflammatory response and its regulation, wound healing / response to wounding, blood coagulation, erythrocyte differentiation.
- 4: innate immunity, cytokine/chemokine production and signaling (TNF, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17), NF-kB, T cell activation, complement, hemopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, platelet activation/aggregation, skin/epidermis development, keratinocyte differentiation, ECM/collagen organization, leukocyte adhesion.
- 3: phagocytosis, chemotaxis, TLR/PRR signaling, oxidative stress/ROS, angiogenesis, epithelial proliferation/migration, growth/nutrient signaling (TOR, insulin/IGF, glucose and energy homeostasis, starvation responses), general stress responses, bacterial defense, pain perception, blood pressure regulation, glucocorticoid receptor signaling.
- 2: metabolic/energy terms, cell adhesion/migration, apoptosis regulation, bone/cartilage/beak terms, circadian, learning/cognition, hormone responses, antiviral terms.
- 1: housekeeping, neural development/synaptic, cell cycle, DNA repair, RNA processing, organogenesis unrelated to skin/blood/immunity.
