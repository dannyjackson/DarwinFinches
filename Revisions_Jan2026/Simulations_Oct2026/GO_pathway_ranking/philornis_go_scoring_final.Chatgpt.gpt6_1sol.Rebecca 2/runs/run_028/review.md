# A priori review: Philornis downsi host responses

This rating uses parasite natural history, host physiology and behavioral experiments, plus general biological knowledge. It does not use host genomic, transcriptomic, expression, or selection results. A search result involving expression was excluded from the evidence base. No other local files or raters' work were consulted.

## Mechanisms and their evidential status

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nares and sometimes other tissues; later larvae largely reside in nest material and feed externally on nestlings, particularly at night. Consumption of blood and tissue causes anemia, lesions, impaired growth, mortality, and persistent nasal deformation. These make responses to injury directly relevant and hemostasis, erythrocyte replacement, iron handling, oxygen delivery, epithelial restoration, and wound healing strong physiological predictions. The latter predictions should not be mistaken for demonstrated P. downsi-induced molecular pathways. Sources: Fessl, Sinclair and Kleindorfer (2006), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; Koop et al. (2011), https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0019706 ; Kleindorfer et al. (2019), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6571467/ .

**Immune resistance.** Adult female medium ground finches produce fly-binding antibodies. An experimental manipulation found higher antibody levels in mothers with parasitized nests and an inverse association between maternal antibodies and parasite abundance. This did not rescue offspring survival. Comparable induced responses were not established in fathers or nestlings. Adaptive immune response is therefore directly supported; B-cell processes are core components, while inflammatory regulation, innate defense, leukocyte recruitment, and downstream cytokines are predictions of differing specificity rather than demonstrated universal mechanisms. Antibacterial defense is plausible after barrier injury but is not evidence that this fly is a proven bacterial vector. Sources: Huber et al. (2010), https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008605 ; Koop et al. (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

**Tolerance and energetic compensation.** A fly-removal experiment comparing Galapagos mockingbirds and medium ground finches found mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater parental provisioning and compensation for parasite effects. Finches did not show that response; neither species showed a significant induced antibody response in that experiment. Energy balance, nutrient uptake, metabolism, and growth support are thus relevant, but generic metabolic processes remain less specific than the documented behavioral compensation. Source: Knutie et al. (2016), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ .

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Preening, scratching, larval removal, and predation on adult flies have been observed, although their efficacy and frequency vary. Altered brooding can expose nestlings to thermal costs. Nest-material choice can be harnessed experimentally through insecticide-treated cotton; this is an assisted intervention, not proof of evolved insecticide use. Sensory processing, learning, neuromuscular function, and daily timing can support these behaviors, but broad neural-development and synaptic terms provide only indirect links. Sources: nest-camera observations, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/ ; Knutie et al. (2014), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/ ; McNew and Clayton (2018), https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 .

**Stress and damage limitation.** Hormonal stress, oxidative injury, and resource trade-offs are reasonable candidates, not all established responses. Manipulating flies did not significantly alter baseline or induced corticosterone, body condition, or hematocrit in breeding adult females in one study. This limits claims about a universal endocrine stress mechanism and does not negate nestling blood loss. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/ .

## Scoring decisions

Each supplied term was considered independently. Scores distinguish documented broad responses (5), core mechanisms or strong physiological predictions (4), plausible supporting processes (3), nonspecific or weak links (2), and processes with no specific connection (1). Positive and negative regulators can both be relevant because damage control and excessive responses can affect tolerance. Ubiquitous cellular necessity alone was not treated as strong evidence. Narrow embryonic patterning, gametogenesis, radiation responses, and most nucleic-acid housekeeping received low scores.

The platelet terms were interpreted cautiously as functional hemostatic analogies in a bird with thrombocytes, rather than proof of mammalian platelet biology.
