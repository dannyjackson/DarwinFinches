# A priori review and scoring basis

This assessment uses published natural-history, experimental parasitology, immunology and behavioral literature, plus general biological knowledge. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results from Galápagos birds were used. Each input term was considered as a potential host process, rather than as a process occurring in the fly. Scores describe physiological relevance, not evidence that a particular GO process is under selection or is an effective defense in nestlings.

## Mechanisms

**Damage and compensation.** Adult flies are free-living; larvae parasitize birds. Early stages occupy the nares and other tissues, while later larvae live in nest material and feed externally on nestling blood. Tissue injury and blood loss produce anemia, impaired growth and substantial mortality. Accordingly, responses to injury, tissue repair, epithelial integrity, hemostasis, erythrocyte replacement, iron handling and oxygen shortage have strong a priori links. Wound healing is a direct physiological counterpart to documented injury; detailed repair pathways remain predictions rather than experimentally demonstrated defenses. Matrix remodeling, vascular repair, metabolic fuel use and oxidative-stress control are supporting mechanisms. Specific embryonic organ-patterning processes were not elevated merely because larvae damage tissues.

**Immune resistance.** Medium ground-finch females can produce fly-binding antibodies, with an inverse association between antibody levels and nest parasite abundance in one experiment. This association does not establish causal protection: poor condition might also limit antibody production, and parasitized broods in that experiment still failed. Other experiments did not detect significant antibody responses in either finches or mockingbirds; nestling responses are especially limited. Adaptive immunity therefore has a direct link; B-cell activation/differentiation and immune regulation are core predicted components. Innate inflammation, cytokines, leukocyte recruitment, complement and phagocytosis are plausible resistance or injury-response machinery, without assuming that each has been demonstrated against this fly. Antiviral pathways are not direct anti-fly mechanisms. Antibacterial processes have potential relevance to damaged barriers and secondary infection, rather than evidence that the fly is itself bacterial or a demonstrated vector in these studies.

**Tolerance and nutrition.** Parasitized Galápagos mockingbird nestlings can beg more and obtain increased parental provisioning, apparently compensating for damage. This tolerance varies with annual conditions and deteriorates when rainfall and resources are limited. Compensation is not universal: several Darwin finches fail to increase provisioning, and highly infested green warbler-finch nests show reduced female provisioning and poorer nestling growth. Energy balance, nutrient sensing, glucose and lipid use, protein turnover, growth and repair can support tolerance; detailed intracellular pathways receive lower scores when their connection is nonspecific. A null result in one host or year does not rule out a physiological role across hosts.

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Observations include larval removal/preening, scratching, flapping and nestling repositioning; some are rare and their effectiveness is uncertain. Reviews discuss nest placement and breeding timing as possible avoidance mechanisms. Finch incorporation of experimentally supplied insecticide-treated nest material demonstrates a management-assisted behavioral route, not intrinsic insecticide production or a proven evolved natural defense. Sensory, motor, neural and circadian functions can support behavior but are usually only weakly or indirectly linked at the specificity of the supplied GO terms. Fly-control physiology, general cognition, or every neural developmental process was not automatically treated as a host defense.

## Scoring interpretation

Scores of 5 mark terms directly encompassing documented host-response mechanisms. Scores of 4 mark core machinery or strong physiological predictions from the observed injury, blood feeding, immunity and compensation. Scores of 3 identify plausible support/downstream processes; 2 indicates weak or nonspecific connections; 1 indicates no specific link, especially unrelated reproductive/embryonic programs and detailed nucleic-acid housekeeping. Regulatory direction alone does not make a term protective or irrelevant: both activation and restraint can matter in repair and immunopathology. GO:0160240 was supplied without a name; an official GO release identifies it as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, which was rated as general transcriptional housekeeping. No input name or identifier was changed.

## Key sources

- Fessl et al. (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi (Diptera: Muscidae) parasitizing Darwin’s finches and its impacts on nestling survival*. Establishes intranasal/tissue infestation, later external hematophagy, blood loss and combined injury/anemia effects. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Huber et al., *Ecoimmunity in Darwin’s finches: invasive parasites trigger acquired immunity in the medium ground finch (Geospiza fortis)*. Fly-binding antibodies and nesting/female exposure association. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2799526/
- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin’s finches and introduced nest flies*. Experimental parasite manipulation, antibody responses, behavioral observations and limits to protection. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/
- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin’s finches*. Species contrast in begging, provisioning and tolerance, without a significant antibody response in that experiment. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*. Multi-year experimental evidence that mockingbird tolerance and compensation depend on environmental resources. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963843/
- *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth under Philornis downsi parasitism in the Galapagos Green Warbler-Finch, classified as vulnerable by the IUCN* (2023). Tests and fails to support increased parental food compensation in this finch. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583/
- McNew and Clayton (2018), *Alien invasion: biology of Philornis flies highlighting Philornis downsi, an introduced parasite of Galápagos birds*. Review of host injury, immune resistance, behavioral resistance, avoidance and tolerance. https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103
- *Behavior of the avian parasite Philornis downsi (Diptera: Muscidae) in and near host nests in the Galapagos Islands*. Observed larval feeding and host scratching, flapping and preening; cautions about whether these actually remove larvae. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- *Innovations in invasive parasite control: enhancing nest treatment techniques to combat the threat of the avian vampire fly Philornis downsi in Galapagos*. Management-assisted self-fumigation and insecticide-treated nesting materials. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41503583/
- Official GO release, identity check for the unnamed GO:0160240 only. https://release.geneontology.org/2026-08-05/reports/go-cam-stats/go-cam-unique-go-bp-terms.html
