# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

## Literature basis

Adult flies are free-living; larvae attack birds. Early instars occupy nestling nostrils and other tissues, whereas older larvae principally shelter in nest material and feed externally, often at night. Blood feeding and tissue destruction cause substantial blood loss, anemia, reduced growth, nasal deformation, and nestling mortality. Brooding females can also be exposed. Persistent naris damage can alter adult song and mating outcomes; this is a consequence of injury rather than evidence that all neural or reproductive processes defend against flies.

Documented immune responses include increased parasite-binding antibodies in parasitized female medium ground finches. The experimental association with reduced parasite abundance suggests possible resistance, but did not rescue offspring survival. Nestlings and adult males did not show comparable induced responses in that experiment. Other comparisons did not detect significant antibody responses in either finch or mockingbird nestlings. Thus immune relevance is strong without assuming universal or effective immune protection. Inflammation, innate recognition, complement, phagocytes, cytokines, and lymphocyte functions are mechanistically plausible components; their individual pathways are generally predictions rather than demonstrated anti-Philornis mechanisms.

Behavioral observations include occasional removal or consumption of larvae, preening, scratching, parental attention to nestling nares, and nestlings moving above nestmates. Flycatcher observations also show nestling defensive movements and parent predation on adult flies. These observations support sensory and motor contributions, but do not demonstrate that every synaptic or brain-development process confers resistance. Nest-site and timing choices could modify exposure, although correlates of infestation do not establish adaptive avoidance.

Tolerance differs from resistance: it limits damage without necessarily lowering parasite abundance. Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging, accompanied by increased provisioning, consistent with compensating for parasite costs. Tolerance varied with environmental conditions and provisioning across years. Finch species do not uniformly compensate: a green warbler-finch study found lower female provisioning at high infestation. Nutrient handling, energetic homeostasis, growth, and resource allocation are therefore relevant supporting processes, with species-specific effectiveness. Blood replacement and iron balance, hypoxia responses, hemostasis, epithelial barriers, wound repair, and vascular repair are strong physiological predictions from the injuries; they are not claimed here as experimentally demonstrated molecular defenses.

## Scoring approach

Each supplied term was considered independently. Scores of 5 were reserved for direct immune/defense umbrella mechanisms supported by the literature; 4 denotes core immune machinery or strong predictions from blood and tissue damage; 3 denotes supporting repair, growth, metabolic, sensory, or stress processes. General cellular maintenance and very indirect behavioral substrates usually received 2, while specialized unrelated developmental, reproductive, genome-maintenance, and RNA-processing processes often received 1. Neither the number of genes in a term nor any desired score distribution was used. A term describing increased or decreased regulation can be relevant without implying that either direction is necessarily beneficial. Platelet terms were interpreted cautiously as comparative hemostatic annotations: birds use nucleated thrombocytes. GO:0160240 was resolved through the GO source below as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, without changing the supplied identifier or input term. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results from Galapagos birds were used.
