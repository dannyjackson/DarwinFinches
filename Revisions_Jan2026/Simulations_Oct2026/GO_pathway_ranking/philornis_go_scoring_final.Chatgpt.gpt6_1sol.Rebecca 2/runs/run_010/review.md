# A priori physiological relevance review

The scoring uses ecological, behavioral, parasitological and conventional immunological literature, together with general biology. No host genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression results were used. Each input term was judged for its own biological meaning; related processes received consistent judgments without imposing a score distribution. GO:0160240, supplied without a name, was identified as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance from the GO report below and retained under its original identifier.

## Mechanisms and strength of evidence

**Damage and physiological compensation.** Early larvae inhabit the nares and other tissues; later larvae live in the nest and feed externally on blood and tissue fluids, especially at night. Blood loss, reduced hemoglobin, wounds, persistent naris deformation, impaired growth and mortality establish the main physiological challenges. Hemostasis, replacement of erythrocytes, iron management and responses to reduced oxygen delivery are strong mechanistic predictions, rather than demonstrated molecular adaptations. Epithelial repair, extracellular matrix remodeling, inflammatory control and vascular repair are similarly well-motivated responses to injury. Antioxidant protection and cellular survival pathways are supporting possibilities, with less direct evidence.

**Immune resistance.** An experimental study found increased fly-binding antibodies in female medium ground finches, but not males or nestlings. The inverse association between female antibody levels and parasite abundance was suggestive rather than conclusive evidence of protection. Another experiment found no significant antibody response in either finches or mockingbirds. Adaptive immune response therefore directly captures a documented host response, while B-cell activation/differentiation, T-cell help and immune regulation are component mechanisms or predictions. Innate inflammation, complement and phagocytosis are plausible responses to tissue damage and foreign material; the literature reviewed does not establish that each is an effective fly defense. Antiviral pathways receive little or no fly-specific support, and secondary antibacterial defense remains a supporting prediction rather than proven protection against this fly.

**Tolerance and nutrition.** Galapagos mockingbirds can maintain reproductive performance despite comparable parasite densities, with increased nestling begging and parental provisioning consistent with compensation for energetic costs. That response is not universal: finches in the comparison did not compensate, and heavily parasitized green warbler-finch females reduced provisioning. Annual environmental variation also affects tolerance. Energy homeostasis directly represents this compensatory mechanism; nutrient sensing, substrate metabolism, transport and growth regulation can support it. Specialized embryonic patterning, reproductive-cell development and generic molecular housekeeping are not automatically high-scoring because growth or survival is affected.

**Behavior.** Nestlings may preen, remove or consume larvae, scratch and change position or stand on nestmates. Parental nest attendance, brooding and sanitation can change, but efficacy is limited or uncertain. Predominantly nocturnal larval feeding can disrupt rest and increase energetic costs. These observations support sensory, motor and circadian functions only in proportion to their specificity: a general neural developmental or synaptic term does not itself identify an antiparasite behavior. Incorporation of experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton demonstrates a management-assisted behavioral defense; it does not establish an evolved host xenobiotic-detoxification response.
