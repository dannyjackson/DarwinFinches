# Independent a priori mechanism review

## Biological basis

Philornis downsi larvae attack nestlings and can also blood-feed on brooding females. Early instars occupy nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nesting material and feed externally on blood. Direct consequences include tissue wounds, nasal deformation, reduced haemoglobin, impaired growth and high nestling mortality. These establish strong a priori links to blood replacement, iron regulation, oxygen limitation, haemostasis, epithelial repair and restoration of tissue integrity. These are physiological predictions from documented injury, not claims that every such compensatory pathway has been demonstrated experimentally.

Resistance and tolerance should be distinguished. Koop et al. experimentally demonstrated increased fly-binding antibodies in parasitized medium ground-finch mothers, with higher antibody levels tending to accompany fewer parasites; neither males nor nestlings showed the same induced response. An antibody response did not rescue offspring survival in that experiment. Inflammation, defensive peptides and lytic effectors are proposed ways host immunity could interfere with fly feeding; precise cytokines, complement components and intracellular immune pathways remain predictions rather than demonstrated Philornis-specific mechanisms. A later comparison did not find a significant antibody response in either finches or mockingbirds, underscoring context dependence.

Mockingbird nestlings can increase begging, accompanied by greater parental food provisioning and apparent compensation for parasite damage. This provides a direct tolerance mechanism involving energy balance. Multi-year work found tolerance varied with rainfall and provisioning: it is resource-dependent rather than an invariant property of mockingbirds. Nutrient uptake, carbohydrate and lipid use, mitochondrial energy production, growth regulation and stress protection are plausible supporting processes, but generic metabolic terms do not automatically constitute direct defenses.

Observed finch behaviors include self-preening, eating larvae and standing on other nestlings, interpreted as potential ways to reduce attacks. Neural transmission, sensory perception and muscle function support these behaviors, but general neural development or synapse construction has a much more remote connection. Incorporation of supplied insecticide-treated nest material can reduce parasites: this is assisted behavioral control, not evidence that birds naturally synthesize insecticides or that xenobiotic metabolism is a primary host defense. Leaf anointing is observed, and leaf compounds can repel flies in laboratory tests, but seasonal patterns did not substantiate a specific P. downsi defense function.

A corticosterone experiment in adult females found no parasite-treatment effect on baseline or stress-induced concentrations, condition or haematocrit. Endocrine stress remains plausible in some contexts, but is not a demonstrated universal response. Wound-associated bacterial defenses and antioxidant protection are plausible secondary mechanisms, whereas virus-specific pathways, reproductive cell processes and unrelated embryonic organ patterning have little specific support.

## Scoring approach

Each term was considered individually in its supplied order. Scores reflect mechanistic proximity, not a desired score distribution or evidence of selection. Direct antibody immunity and compensatory energy balance receive the highest ratings; core immune, blood-replacement, hypoxia and tissue-repair components receive strong predicted ratings; supporting physiology receives intermediate ratings. Broad cellular machinery and distant behavioral support receive lower ratings. No Galapagos bird genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression study was used. GO:0160240, supplied without a name, was identified from the official GO report as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance and judged accordingly; its supplied identifier is preserved.
