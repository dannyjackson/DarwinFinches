## Mechanisms and sources

**Blood and tissue damage.** Nonparasitic adults deposit eggs at host nests. Early larvae occupy nestling nares and damage tissue; later instars shelter in nest material and feed on hosts, particularly at night. Loss of blood, reduced hemoglobin, lesions and impaired development give strong independent grounds for erythrocyte replacement, iron handling, coagulation, wound repair, barrier maintenance and compensation for reduced oxygen delivery. These latter pathways are biological predictions, rather than claims that each was measured in finches. Sources: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089 ; Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x .

**Immune resistance, with limited demonstrated effectiveness.** Experimental removal of flies demonstrated parasite-binding antibody responses in medium ground finch mothers. The tendency for higher maternal antibodies to accompany fewer parasites suggests resistance, but did not rescue offspring survival. Males and nestlings lacked comparable induced responses in that experiment. Antibody production and adaptive immunity have direct support; B-cell processes, helper T-cell activity, inflammatory recruitment, innate recognition, complement and phagocytic clearance are mechanistic predictions with varying specificity. Cutaneous inflammation and irritation-driven grooming are proposed defenses, not established molecular outcomes. Source: Koop et al. (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Video observations document preening, larva consumption, nest-base and naris probing by mothers, and nestling positional avoidance. These motivate modest scores for sensory, neural and motor support, but do not make every developmental or synaptic process directly parasite related. Source: O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086 . Finches also apply Psidium leaves to feathers; extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth, while protection achieved by natural application remains uncertain: Cimadom et al. (2016), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/ . Use of experimentally supplied treated cotton demonstrates a behavioral opportunity rather than evolved physiological insecticide resistance: Knutie et al. (2014), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058 .

**Tolerance and resource allocation.** Mockingbirds can compensate for infestation through increased begging and parental provisioning; finches did not show the same compensation in the comparative experiment. Tolerance varies across years and decreases during drought. Nutrient uptake, fuel mobilization, energetic homeostasis, growth and repair therefore have plausible supporting roles, without assuming any particular metabolic pathway has been demonstrated. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 ; McNew et al. (2019), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963843/ .

**Important negative evidence.** Fly manipulation did not significantly change maternal baseline or stress-induced corticosterone, body condition or hematocrit in one experiment. Thus generic endocrine stress is plausible but not scored as a directly established defense: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/ . Antiviral-specific processes, gametogenesis, embryonic patterning and unrelated organogenesis receive low scores; generic cellular machinery receives weak scores unless a more specific connection is apparent. No target score distribution was imposed. Directional regulation can contribute to resistance or tolerance through activation, restraint or resolution; a negative-regulation term is not automatically irrelevant.
