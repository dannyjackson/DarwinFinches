# Philornis downsi host-response review (run_026)

## What the larvae do to the host
- First-instar larvae live in the nares and ear canals of nestlings, where they eat tissue and keratin. This causes naris enlargement and beak deformation that can persist into adulthood. Later instars live in the nest base and feed on blood and tissue at night. The result is blood loss (lower haemoglobin), reduced mass and growth, and high nestling mortality. Larvae are increasingly found during incubation, and attacks on brooding females are possible.
- Sources: Fessl et al. 2006 (https://publish.csiro.au/MU/MU04040); Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 (https://bmczool.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40850-016-0003-9/tables/1); review chapter (https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/68802); haemoglobin effects (https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/); naris deformation (https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/naris-deformation-in-darwins-finches-experimental-and-historical-/).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Resistance through adaptive immunity.** Brooding female finches produce P. downsi-specific antibodies (IgY), and higher antibody levels are linked to lower larval intensity. Urban finches show a pro-inflammatory response, and larger inflammatory and immune responses are proposed as a form of resistance. The mechanisms involve B cells, antibodies, inflammation and possibly a skin immune response at bite sites. Sources: Koop et al. 2013 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3930052/); Huber et al. 2010 (https://doaj.org/article/c0c1dd23b9f64ef2bc80d5833593b012); https://earth.com/news/urban-finches-have-surprising-resilience-against-deadly-vampire-fly.
2. **Tolerance through blood replacement and energy compensation.** Mockingbirds tolerate the parasite. Their parasitized nestlings have lower haemoglobin and higher plasma glucose, and replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis, iron balance) has been proposed as a tolerance mechanism. Parents feed parasitized broods more and the nestlings beg more. Tolerance depends on food and rainfall. Clotting and wound repair limit damage. Sources: Knutie et al. 2016 (https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/126.pdf; https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70770688-559c-41e2-ba8b-ead6e9e97f7d/full); McNew et al. 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884).
3. **Behavioral defenses.** Finches incorporate permethrin-treated cotton into nests (self-fumigation; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182). They rub feathers with Psidium galapageium leaves as a repellent (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5056383/). Nestlings change their behavior, which predicts naris deformation (https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/nestling-behaviour-predicts-naris-deformation-in-darwins-finches-/). Females alter brooding posture, and mockingbirds brood less in parasitized nests.
4. **Stress physiology and condition.** Effects on corticosterone have been tested in breeding finches (https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65df89250f3e94b9e5d97be8). Diet and protein intake, and early-life gut microbiota, are proposed to modulate defenses (https://doaj.org/article/1b1590f284c14baabdb237bf5e308578).

## Scoring logic
- **5:** hemostasis and coagulation, platelets, wound healing and response to wounding, erythrocyte differentiation, inflammatory response, adaptive and humoral (B cell) immunity.
- **4:** innate immunity and general immune regulation, cytokines, T cells, complement, haematopoiesis, iron and glucose homeostasis, keratinocyte and epidermis processes, hypoxia response, steroid hormone signaling.
- **3:** immune signaling hubs (NF-kB, JAK-STAT, TLR), phagocytosis, angiogenesis, VEGF, TGF-beta, extracellular matrix and collagen, insulin and nutrient signaling, oxidative stress, bone and beak development, learning and memory.
- **2:** generic signaling, apoptosis, metabolism, development and tissue processes.
- **1:** housekeeping cellular processes (RNA processing, DNA replication, synapse, cilia and others).

Excluded by protocol: transcriptomic and genomic studies of Galápagos birds (e.g., bioRxiv 2023.03.06.531275), which appeared in search results but were not opened or used.
