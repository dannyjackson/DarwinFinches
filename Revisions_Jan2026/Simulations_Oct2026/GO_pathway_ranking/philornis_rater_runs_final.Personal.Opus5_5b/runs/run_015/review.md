# Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds: a review for scoring

## What the larvae do to hosts
- The adult fly lays eggs in active nests. First-instar larvae go into the nestlings' nares and other body openings, and later instars feed on blood and tissue by night from the nest material. This causes blood loss, lower haemoglobin and anaemia, open wounds and tissue damage, enlarged or deformed nares and beak malformation. It also cuts growth and causes high nestling mortality, often above 50% and up to 100% in some years and species. Brooding females are also bitten.
- Hosts affected: Darwin's finches (Geospiza, Camarhynchus, Certhidea), Galápagos mockingbirds, yellow warblers and other passerines.

## Host defenses that have been documented or proposed
1. **Resistance through acquired (humoral) immunity.** Adult females and nestlings of the medium ground finch produce P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies. Antibody titres go up with parasite exposure and are linked to lower parasite load or better fitness. This points to B-cell activation, adaptive immunity and inflammation at bite sites, possibly including antibodies against anticoagulant or salivary components (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013).
2. **Tolerance.** Mockingbirds and some finches cope with heavy parasite loads without losing fitness. The mechanisms are thought to be replacing lost blood (erythropoiesis and haematopoiesis), wound repair, energy and resource allocation, and stress-hormone regulation. Food availability affects tolerance (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019).
3. **Haematology and physiology.** Parasitised nestlings have lower haemoglobin. Proposed links include iron homeostasis, oxygen and hypoxia responses, and oxidative stress. The effect of the parasite on corticosterone in breeding finches has been tested experimentally.
4. **Integument and wound healing.** Larvae damage skin and keratinised tissue at the nares and beak, so epidermis, keratinocyte and collagen repair, angiogenesis and coagulation or haemostasis are relevant.
5. **Behaviour.** Nestlings beg more, parents change how much they feed the young, females change brooding, and birds may self-medicate by rubbing Psidium galapagense leaves on their feathers. Nestling behaviour predicts naris deformation. Neural and endocrine links are weaker and indirect.
6. **Growth and allocation.** Nestling growth and naris or beak development suffer. Growth-factor and skeletal-development terms are therefore only weakly to moderately relevant.

## How the scale was applied
- 5: wound healing or response to wounding, inflammation, the immune response and adaptive immunity, B-cell activation, coagulation, erythrocyte differentiation.
- 4: the core immune signalling and cytokines, T cells, complement, platelets, haematopoiesis, iron homeostasis, oxidative stress and hypoxia, skin and keratinocytes, collagen, angiogenesis.
- 3: downstream signalling (MAPK, JAK-STAT, TGF-beta, VEGF, EGF), cell migration, endothelium, stress, hormones, energy and nutrient status.
- 2: general metabolism, development, apoptosis, autophagy, behaviour and cognition.
- 1: housekeeping processes (RNA processing, the cell cycle, synapses, cilia, the germline).
