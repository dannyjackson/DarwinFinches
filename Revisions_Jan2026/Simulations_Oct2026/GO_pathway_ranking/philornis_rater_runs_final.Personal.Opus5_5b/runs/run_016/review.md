# Philornis downsi and Galapagos land birds: mechanisms used for scoring

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae live in the nestlings' nares and ear canals. Later instars live in nest material and feed on nestling blood and tissue at night, which leaves open wounds.
- In nestlings this causes blood loss and lower haemoglobin (anaemia), lower growth and mass, and high mortality, often more than 50% and close to 100% in some years and species. Incubating females are also fed on.
- Damage to the nares and beak, including enlarged naris size, lasts into adulthood. It changes vocal performance and song, and so mating signals and possibly hybridization, and it may affect foraging.

## Host defences documented or proposed
- **Resistance, adaptive immunity:** finches make P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies (mainly adult females). Higher antibody titres go with fewer parasites. This suggests B-cell/humoral immunity, and possibly antigen-specific responses to larval salivary or feeding proteins.
- **Inflammation and innate immunity at feeding sites:** proposed, based on general ectoparasite and myiasis biology.
- **Tolerance:**
  - Making up for blood loss through haematopoiesis/erythropoiesis and iron balance.
  - Wound repair and clotting.
  - Energy intake and metabolism. In the mockingbird work, parasitized nestlings begged more and parents fed them more, which compensated for parasite damage. Finches did not raise begging, provisioning or plasma glucose.
  - Food supplementation, and the urban or human-food environment, increase finch tolerance.
- **Behaviour:**
  - Self-medication: finches rub Psidium galapagense leaves on their feathers as a repellent.
  - Finches take up permethrin-treated cotton into their nests ("self-fumigation").
  - Proposed: nest sanitation, preening and brooding.
  - Learning and cognition underlie these novel behaviours.
- **Secondary effects:**
  - Stress or glucocorticoid physiology, oxidative stress, hypoxia caused by anaemia, and xenobiotic responses to plant repellents.
  - Craniofacial and beak tissue development and remodelling, which links to the naris damage.
