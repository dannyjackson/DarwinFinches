# Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (run_013)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- *P. downsi* is an introduced muscid fly in Galápagos. Its larvae live in nests and feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings, and sometimes of brooding females. First-instar larvae enter the nares and feather quills. Later instars feed externally, mostly at night, and hide in the nest base during the day (McNew & Clayton 2018).
- **Blood loss and anaemia-like effects.** Parasitized nestlings have lower haemoglobin and lower mass, and lose more blood. Mortality is often very high and rises with parasite intensity (Knutie et al. 2016; Dudaniec et al. 2006; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016).
- **Tissue damage.** Larvae deform the nares and beak. The damage lasts into adulthood and changes song (Katsis et al. 2021; Kleindorfer et al. 2019).
- **Energetic and nutritional costs.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings had higher plasma glucose. In urban areas, nestlings with higher protein intake (δ15N) had fewer parasites and survived better (Knutie et al. 2016; urban-finch study 2024).
- **Indirect effects.** Heavy infestations reduce female brooding, which may disrupt the nest's thermal environment. The larvae do not appear to change adult corticosterone. No clear secondary infection has been shown (McNew & Clayton 2018).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (immune).** Nesting female medium ground finches produced *P. downsi*-specific IgY antibodies, and higher antibody levels went with fewer parasites (Koop et al. 2013). Nestlings showed little or no antibody response (Knutie et al. 2016). Pro-inflammatory activity has also been proposed as a resistance mechanism. Haemostasis and inflammation at bite and wound sites are general a priori predictions against a blood-feeding, tissue-damaging larva.
- **Tolerance.**
  - Mockingbird nestlings beg more, and their parents provision more, which offsets the cost of the parasites (Knutie et al. 2016).
  - Blood replacement (erythropoiesis) has been proposed as a way to tolerate blood loss.
  - Better nutrition, especially protein, raises tolerance (urban study).
  - Larger body size may buffer the costs.
  - Repair of wounds and tissue is an a priori tolerance route.
- **Behavioural.** Preening of larvae is rare. Some finches rub guava leaves on their feathers, possibly as a repellent. Nestlings crawl over nestmates to avoid larvae. Hosts may also time breeding to avoid the fly or adjust brooding. Parental provisioning and begging are central to tolerance.

## Scoring logic
- **5:** immune/antibody response, inflammation, wounding and wound healing, red blood cell production.
- **4:** core parts of these mechanisms: B-cell and antibody pathways, cytokines, haemostasis (coagulation and platelets), complement, iron homeostasis for heme, defense response.
- **3:** supporting processes: T-cell help, innate signalling, leukocyte migration, hypoxia, oxidative stress, epidermal and ECM repair, angiogenesis, energy and glucose homeostasis, nutrient and starvation responses, thermogenesis, pain perception.
- **2:** weak links: antiviral and antibacterial defense, generic growth signalling, bone and cartilage (beak), hormones, behaviour and learning, generic stress and metabolism.
- **1:** housekeeping, development and neural processes with no specific link.
