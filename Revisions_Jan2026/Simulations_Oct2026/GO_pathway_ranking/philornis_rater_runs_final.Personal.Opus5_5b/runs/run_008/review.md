# P. downsi and host responses: literature summary (run_008)

## What the larvae do
- First-instar larvae enter the nares and ear canals of nestlings. Later instars live in the nest base and feed at night on blood and tissue from nestlings, and sometimes from brooding females. Unlike most Philornis, they stay outside the skin.
- Effects on nestlings: open wounds, deformed nares and beaks (which later alter song), lower hemoglobin (blood loss and anemia), less mass gain and slower growth, and high mortality (up to 100%).
- No clear effect on adult condition: in females, hematocrit, corticosterone and body condition did not change with parasitism.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. **Adaptive/humoral immunity (resistance).** Adult female medium ground finches produce P. downsi-binding antibodies (IgY, measured by ELISA). Levels are higher during nesting and higher in females (who brood) than males. In experiments the response was induced by parasitism and was inversely related to parasite number, but it did not predict nestling survival. Nestlings showed no detectable antibody response. The possibility of maternal antibody transfer was raised.
2. **Tolerance through blood replacement and resources.** Hemoglobin is the main measure of cost. Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly: parasitized nestlings beg more, parents provision more, and nestling glucose rises. In mockingbirds, tolerance falls in dry, food-poor years. Finches show no compensation.
3. **Behavior.** Females brood less and stand on the nest rim, nestlings crawl over nestmates, and preening or removing larvae is rare. Rubbing Psidium galapageium leaves on feathers is proposed as self-medication with a repellent. Birds readily take permethrin-treated cotton into nests (a management tool, not a natural defense).
4. **Other.** Body size (larger hosts are more tolerant). The nestling microbiota was not changed by parasitism in cactus finches.
