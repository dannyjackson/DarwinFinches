# Philornis downsi and host responses: literature summary (run_019)

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in nests. First-instar larvae live in the nestlings' nares and ear canals, and later instars live in nest material and feed at night on nestling blood and tissue. This causes blood loss (lower hemoglobin and anemia), open wounds and tissue damage, malformed nares and beaks in birds that survive, slower growth, and high nestling mortality. Incubating females are also bitten.
- Sources: Fessl et al. 2006 / Philornis factsheet https://www.darwininitiative.org.uk/documents/DAR15005/20871/15-005%20Philornis%20factsheet%202009.pdf ; Galapagos Conservation Trust https://galapagosconservation.org.uk/?p=8221 ; Discover summary of beak deformation https://discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/parasites-are-destroying-the-beaks-of-darwins-famous-finches

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (adaptive and humoral immunity):** finches produce P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies. Higher antibody levels go with fewer or smaller larvae (Huber et al. 2010, Koop et al. 2013). https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3930052/ ; https://sicb.org/?p=25430
- **Innate and inflammatory responses, wound healing and haemostasis:** these are proposed at the feeding wounds, based on general host responses to myiasis and haematophagous ectoparasites.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate the parasite. Parental compensation through more food reduces the cost to nestlings (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology). Proposed tolerance mechanisms are blood regeneration (erythropoiesis and iron homeostasis) and growth maintained through nutrition. https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/70770688-559c-41e2-ba8b-ead6e9e97f7d/full ; https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/126.pdf
- **Provisioning, begging and nutrition:** effects depend on provisioning (warbler-finch study). Begging of parasitised nestlings does not honestly signal need. Food from urban areas can save nestlings from lethal effects. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10188583/ ; https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/darwins-finch-begging-intensity-does-not-honestly-signal-need-in-/ ; https://research.usfq.edu.ec/en/publications/urban-living-can-rescue-darwins-finches-from-the-lethal-effects-o/
- **Behavioural defense:** finches self-fumigate by rubbing Psidium galbanum leaves into their feathers (Cimadom et al. 2016). Nest sanitation and female brooding have also been proposed. https://phaidra.univie.ac.at/detail/o:1396724
