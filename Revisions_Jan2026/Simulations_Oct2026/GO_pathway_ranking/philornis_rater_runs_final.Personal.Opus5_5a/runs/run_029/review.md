# P. downsi host-response review (run_029)

## What the larvae do
- Adult flies lay eggs in host nests; larvae are semi-hematophagous and feed on the blood and tissue of nestlings and brooding females. First instars live in nestlings' nares and under the skin; later instars feed externally at night.
- Effects: blood loss and anemia (lower hemoglobin), open wounds and enlarged or deformed nares and beaks, slower growth, and high nestling mortality (16–100%). These effects have contributed to population declines in small tree-finches and warbler finches.

## Host defenses documented or proposed
- **Resistance (immune):** Finches produce parasite-specific IgY antibodies. Experimental parasite removal shows the response is induced by the parasite, and higher antibody titres are associated with fewer parasites (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). Inflammation and wound repair at feeding sites are also implied.
- **Tolerance:** Mockingbirds tolerate the parasite and finches do not. Tolerance is linked to compensation for energy and blood loss: parental provisioning, nestling glucose, and recovery of hemoglobin and erythropoiesis (Knutie et al. 2016). Food availability and urban human food modulate tolerance.
- **Stress physiology:** The effects of parasitism on corticosterone in breeding finches have been tested experimentally.
- **Behavioral:** Nestlings beg, and parents change provisioning and nest-lining behavior. Self-fumigation with permethrin-treated cotton is a management tool, not an evolved defense.
