# Philornis downsi host-response review (run_030)

## What the larvae do
- Larvae of the invasive muscid fly P. downsi live in the nest. Early instars enter the nares and ear canals of nestlings. Later instars live in the nest base and come out mainly at night to pierce the skin and feed on blood and tissue. This causes blood loss (lower haemoglobin), open wounds, tissue damage, enlarged or deformed nares and beaks, slower growth, and high nestling mortality. Incubating or brooding females may also be fed on.
- Beak and naris deformation acquired in the nest persists into adulthood. It alters song (vocal performance) and has been linked to mating-signal change and hybridisation.

## Documented and proposed host defences
- **Resistance (immunological):** Brooding female and nestling Darwin's finches produce P. downsi-specific IgY antibodies after exposure. Higher antibody levels are linked to lower parasite intensity (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013). Mockingbirds showed no significant antibody response. In a related nest-fly system, food supplementation raised parasite-binding IgY and reduced parasite load.
- **Tolerance:** Galápagos mockingbird nestlings tolerate P. downsi. Parasitised nestlings beg more and parents provision more, which offsets blood loss. Finches lacked this begging and provisioning response and showed no change in plasma glucose (Knutie et al. 2016). The general prediction is that tolerance depends on resources, through compensation for blood loss (erythropoiesis, iron), wound repair, energy balance and limits on immunopathology (regulation of inflammation).
- **Physiological stress:** Parasite effects on corticosterone in breeding finches have been tested experimentally (stress-hormone axis).
- **Behavioural:** Brooding females stand more upright in parasitised nests. Finches rub leaves of Psidium galapageium on their plumage, and the leaves repel mosquitoes and inhibit P. downsi larvae (Cimadom et al. 2016). Finches also incorporate experimentally provided permethrin-treated cotton into their nests (self-fumigation; Knutie et al. 2014).
