# Literature basis for independent GO scoring

This assessment uses non-omic studies of Philornis downsi natural history, physiological injury, immune responses, and behavior. Host genomic, transcriptomic, and gene-expression results were excluded, including incidental search results. Scores concern potential mechanistic association with a host response, not proof of adaptation, protection, or selection. Each term was assessed individually without a target score distribution.

## Host injury and physiological compensation

Early larvae inhabit the nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, particularly at night. Blood feeding and tissue injury cause anemia, impaired growth, and substantial nestling mortality. These observations make blood replacement, oxygen delivery, and repair especially relevant. In small ground finches, parasite intensity was associated with reduced hemoglobin and increased immature erythrocyte counts, consistent with a regenerative response.

- Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects of the introduced ectoparasite Philornis downsi on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's Small Ground Finch*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x ; accessible author-institution abstract: https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/

## Immunological resistance

An experimental reduction of flies showed that parasitized medium ground finch mothers developed higher fly-binding antibody levels. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but this did not rescue reproductive success. Males and nestlings did not show comparable induced responses in that experiment. Antibody-mediated immunity therefore has direct support, whereas particular cytokines, inflammatory pathways, complement, and cellular effectors are mechanistic predictions rather than demonstrated effective defenses against this fly.

- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

## Nutritional and behavioral tolerance

A comparative experiment found that parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with increased parental provisioning and tolerance of parasite damage. Medium ground finches did not show the same compensatory response. Mockingbird tolerance varied across years and weakened during drought, emphasizing resource availability. Green warbler-finches likewise showed no significant increase in provisioning with higher infestation in a later study. Thus, compensation is species- and environment-dependent, not a universal finch response.

- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1
- McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/
- Pike et al. (2023), *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth under Philornis downsi parasitism in the Galapagos Green Warbler-Finch*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583/

## Behavioral resistance and proposed self-medication

Video observations documented nestling preening, larval ingestion, avoidance, and maternal probing of nest material and nestling nares. Such observations support sensory and behavioral responses but do not establish reliable parasite control. Finches also rub Psidium galapageium leaves on their plumage; extracts repelled flies and inhibited larval growth experimentally, although protective effects of natural feather treatment remain proposed. Incorporation of supplied insecticide-treated cotton reduced nest parasites: this is assisted nest treatment, not evidence of intrinsic host xenobiotic resistance.

- O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086
- *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent* (2016): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/
- Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058

## Translation to GO scores

Directly captured documented responses received the strongest scores. Core adaptive immune components, erythroid support, iron regulation, hypoxia responses, hemostasis, epithelial repair, vascular repair, and inflammatory regulation received strong mechanistic consideration. Wound healing, coagulation, and specific molecular inflammatory pathways were treated as predictions where direct Philornis-specific demonstration was lacking. Energy metabolism, growth, cellular migration, redox maintenance, and sensory processes were evaluated as supporting responses. Generic transcription, translation, trafficking, and cell-cycle machinery were not elevated merely because all defenses require them. Embryonic patterning, gametogenesis, and unrelated environmental stressors generally lacked specific links. Antiviral terms were not treated as responses to the fly itself; antibacterial responses were considered only as plausible secondary consequences of damaged barriers. Positive and negative regulation were judged for relevance, not automatically equated with benefit or harm.
