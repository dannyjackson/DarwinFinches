# Literature basis for the independent GO ratings

The ratings use ecological, behavioral, hematological, and immunological literature on Philornis downsi, together with general biological knowledge. No Galápagos-bird genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or selection results were used. Search hits concerning such studies were excluded. Only the supplied input file was read locally.

## Host damage and physiological compensation

Adult flies are free-living; their larvae attack birds. Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues, while later larvae live in nest material and take blood meals from nestlings. Tissue destruction and blood loss can cause anemia, impaired growth, deformity, and death. Brooding females can also be exposed. These effects motivate blood replacement, oxygen-delivery compensation, hemostasis, epithelial protection, and tissue repair.

- Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects of the introduced ectoparasite Philornis downsi on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's Small Ground Finch*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x

Dudaniec and colleagues reported reduced hemoglobin with greater parasitism and more reticulocytes in nestlings with lower hemoglobin. The latter supports an actual erythropoietic response. Coagulation, iron handling, hypoxia responses, angiogenesis, collagen organization, and epithelial repair are strong mechanistic predictions from injury and anemia, but the reviewed studies do not establish that each named molecular pathway is activated or protective.

## Resistance and behavioral responses

Experimental removal of parasites showed that adult female medium ground finches at parasitized nests produced more fly-binding antibodies. Stronger maternal responses were associated with fewer parasites, but did not prevent reproductive failure in that experiment. Males and nestlings did not show corresponding induced responses. This directly supports adaptive immunity as a candidate mechanism, without assuming uniformly effective immunity.

- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

That study also examined preening, nest sanitation, agitation, and parental posture. It did not establish enhanced behavioral defense sufficient to rescue nestlings. Antibody-associated skin inflammation, itching, and interference with larval feeding were proposed mechanisms rather than demonstrated pathways. Accordingly, lymphocyte functions, inflammatory regulation, leukocyte recruitment, complement, and injury sensing receive strong or supporting ratings according to specificity, rather than being labeled universally documented.

Finches incorporate experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton into nests, reducing parasite abundance. They also apply guayabillo leaves to feathers; laboratory assays support effects against flies, but a fitness benefit from this natural anointing behavior was not established. These observations support behavioral defense and sensory/motor participation, not an inference that every neural-development process is parasite-specific.

- Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058
- Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/

## Tolerance and resource allocation

Parasitized Galápagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging and received more food in a comparative experiment, maintaining fitness despite parasite loads. Medium ground finches did not show the same compensation; neither species showed a significant antibody response in that study. Mockingbird tolerance subsequently varied across years and was weaker in dry, resource-poor conditions. Thus nutritional compensation is documented but conditional, and the immune findings vary among studies. Energy balance, nutrient sensing, protein and fuel metabolism, growth maintenance, and physiological recovery are relevant candidates.

- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/

Green warbler-finches with heavier infestation also had lower nestling mass without a corresponding increase in parental provisioning, reinforcing that compensation cannot be generalized across passerines.

- Pike et al. (2023), *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth under Philornis downsi parasitism in the Galapagos Green Warbler-Finch*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583/

## Scoring interpretation

Scores describe potential physiological association, not evidence of selection, adaptive benefit, or demonstrated pathway activation. A negative regulator can be relevant through restraint of damage or altered response strength. Directly supported immune and erythropoietic processes receive the highest ratings; strong injury/immune/nutritional predictions receive 4; supporting metabolism, signaling, and behavior receive 3 where warranted. Broad cellular machinery generally receives 2. Unrelated reproductive, embryonic, radiation-specific, and virus-specific processes generally receive 1. No score distribution was targeted. Mammalian platelet terms receive a supporting rating for analogous avian thrombocyte hemostasis, not an assumption that birds possess mammalian platelets.
