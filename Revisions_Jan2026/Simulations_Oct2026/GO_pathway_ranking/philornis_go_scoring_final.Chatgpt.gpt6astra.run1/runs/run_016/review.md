# Literature basis for independent GO ratings

## Host injury and physiological responses

Philornis downsi is a larval parasite, whereas adults are free-living. Early larvae invade nestling nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally on nestling blood. Tissue injury and anemia contribute to mortality. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi (Diptera: Muscidae) parasitizing Darwin's finches and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/

In small ground finches, higher parasite intensity was associated with lower hemoglobin, more immature red blood cells, and greater mortality. This supports compensatory erythropoiesis as a direct host response. Hemostasis, wound repair, iron availability, oxygen sensing, vascular responses, and protection from secondary oxidative injury are strong physiological predictions from blood and tissue loss; their effectiveness against this parasite is not established by these observations. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects of the introduced ectoparasite Philornis downsi on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's Small Ground Finch (Geospiza fuliginosa)*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x ; https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/

## Resistance, tolerance, and behavior

- **Immune resistance:** Experimental parasite removal showed that adult female medium ground finches at parasitized nests had higher fly-binding antibody levels. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but this did not rescue reproductive success. Males and nestlings did not show corresponding induced responses in that experiment. Thus antibody-mediated adaptive immunity has direct evidence, while B-cell function and supporting T-cell pathways are core components. Innate recognition, inflammation, complement, cytokines, and phagocytic clearance are plausible defenses or damage responses, not demonstrated effective anti-Philornis mechanisms in that study. Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*: https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

- **Tolerance and energy compensation:** Parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings begged more, associated with increased parental provisioning and apparent compensation for parasite damage. Medium ground finches lacked this compensatory pattern in the same comparison, and neither species showed a significant antibody response there. Mockingbird tolerance also varied among years and declined during drought, implicating food availability and provisioning. These findings motivate energy balance, nutrient use, and growth maintenance, without identifying particular metabolic enzymes as defenses. Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ . *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites* (2019): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963843/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/

- **Behavioral resistance:** Within-nest video documented self-preening, larval consumption, nestling avoidance behavior, and females probing nestling nares and nest material. Such behaviors are not uniformly expressed or sufficient for survival. Sensory processing and motor control can support these mechanisms; generic neural development is a much weaker link. O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086 ; https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/video-analysis-of-host-parasite-interactions-in-nests-of-darwins-/

- **Proposed self-medication:** Finches were observed rubbing Psidium galapageium leaves onto feathers. Experiments found that leaf preparations repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth. This supports a proposed behavioral defense, but does not demonstrate its protective effectiveness on wild birds. *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent* (2016): https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34559 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/

- **A negative result matters:** Parasite manipulation did not significantly alter baseline or acute stress-induced corticosterone, condition, or hematocrit in breeding female medium ground finches. I therefore did not treat a maternal endocrine stress response as established. Knutie et al. (2013), *Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/

## Rating principles

Each term was judged separately. A 5 denotes a directly documented host-response mechanism; 4 denotes a core component or strong prediction; 3 denotes plausible physiological support; 2 denotes a weak or indirect connection; 1 denotes no specific connection. Generic housekeeping, unrelated embryonic patterning, and germline processes were not promoted merely because every response requires living cells. Specific antiviral processes were not equated with resistance to a blood-feeding fly. Positive and negative regulation can both contribute to an appropriately controlled response; scores do not predict which direction would be beneficial.

Platelet terms were evaluated for their conserved hemostatic relevance to avian thrombocytes, without assuming that birds possess mammalian anucleate platelets. GO:0160240 was supplied without a descriptive label. Its official definition, RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, was checked solely to interpret the term: https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo/term/GO%3A0160240 . Its input identifier was retained unchanged.

No host genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or epigenomic findings were used. Search results from such studies were excluded from the evidence basis. No other rater's files or results were consulted. No target score distribution was imposed.
