# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi and Galápagos land birds

This review uses ecological, behavioral, parasitological, and physiological studies, together with general biological knowledge. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression findings from Galápagos birds informed the scores.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood feeding, tissue injury, and anemia.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; older larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally, particularly at night. Tissue destruction and substantial blood loss reduce nestling survival. This creates a strong physiological basis for hemostasis, tissue repair, epithelial barriers, vascular repair, and compensation for reduced oxygen delivery. The latter mechanisms are predictions unless separately documented. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089
- **Red-cell replacement.** In small ground finches, greater parasite intensity was associated with lower hemoglobin and more immature circulating erythrocytes. This directly supports erythropoietic compensation; iron handling, oxygen-sensitive responses, and circulatory adjustments are strong physiological extensions. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- **Antibody-mediated responses, with limited demonstrated protection.** An experimental study found elevated fly-binding antibodies in parasitized female medium ground finches. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but this was not proof of causally effective resistance, and no parasitized nests fledged offspring. Males and nestlings did not show comparable induced responses. Adaptive immunity and its B-cell components therefore have direct or strong support; inflammatory, innate, complement, and helper-cell processes are plausible mechanistic extensions rather than demonstrated molecular pathways. Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651
- **Behavioral resistance and sensory support.** Nest cameras documented grooming, occasional larval consumption, nestlings standing on nestmates, and females probing nestlings' nares or nest material. These observations support behavioral responses but do not establish reliable protection. Nociception and sensory-motor control are plausible supporting mechanisms; individual neuronal developmental pathways are much less specific. O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086
- **Energy compensation and tolerance.** Galápagos mockingbirds can increase begging and associated parental provisioning while maintaining fitness despite parasites; medium ground finches did not show this compensation in the same study. Later experiments found mockingbird tolerance declined in dry years, showing that food availability and environmental conditions constrain compensation. These findings motivate energy balance, nutrient use, growth, and behavioral regulation, without establishing every metabolic pathway as a specific defense. Knutie et al. (2016): https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 ; McNew et al. (2019): https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2019.0049
- **Chemical protection through behavior.** Finches use experimentally supplied permethrin-treated cotton in nests, reducing fly burdens. Wild finches also anoint feathers with Psidium galapageium; laboratory assays showed effects on flies, but protection of wild birds was not established. These support behavioral flexibility and proposed prophylaxis, not an inference that host detoxification pathways directly combat larvae. Knutie et al. (2014): https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058 ; Cimadom et al. (2016): https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34559
- **Persistent structural effects.** Larval damage can leave enlarged nares into adulthood and alter foraging, making epithelial integrity and craniofacial tissue maintenance relevant. This does not make all embryonic skeletal or neuronal development directly parasite-related. Kleindorfer et al. (2022), *Functional traits and foraging behaviour*: https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.14061
- **Negative evidence matters.** An experimental study of breeding female medium ground finches did not find parasite-dependent changes in baseline or acute-stress corticosterone, body condition, or hematocrit. I therefore did not treat a generalized endocrine stress response as demonstrated. *Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin's finches* (2013): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/

## Scoring interpretation

Each supplied term received an individual judgment on the requested 1–5 scale, without a target score distribution. Direct immune responses, erythropoietic compensation, and responses to injury received the strongest support. Predicted wound repair, hemostasis, immune components, and hypoxia compensation generally scored 4; downstream energetic, redox, signaling, and repair-support processes generally scored 3. Generic cellular machinery and broad behavioral support scored lower, and unrelated reproduction, development, or stimulus-specific processes received little or no specific support. Negative regulation can be relevant to limiting damage or controlling a response; a high score does not imply that increasing that process is beneficial. Scores indicate potential association, not proven adaptation or protective efficacy.

Platelet terms were interpreted as the homologous hemostatic functions carried out by avian thrombocytes, while retaining the input identifiers unchanged. The entry labeled only GO:0160240 was checked against official GO: RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance, a general RNA-quality-control process without a specific fly-response link (score 1).
