# Independent a priori review

The ratings use the supplied GO terms, the non-omic literature below, and general biological knowledge. No Galápagos bird genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression findings were used. No score distribution was targeted.

## Mechanisms and evidential limits

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; later larvae reside in nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Larval feeding damages tissue and removes blood, causing anemia, impaired growth, and sometimes extensive nestling mortality [1, 5, 7]. Hemostasis, blood-cell replacement, iron availability, oxygen delivery, epithelial repair, extracellular-matrix reconstruction, and angiogenesis are therefore strong physiological predictions. These predictions should not be mistaken for experimentally demonstrated evolved defenses.

**Immune resistance.** Experimental parasite removal demonstrated higher fly-binding antibody levels in mothers at parasitized medium-ground-finch nests. The same response was not detected in fathers or nestlings, and maternal antibodies did not rescue nestling survival [2]. Adaptive immunity and its B-cell/T-cell machinery are consequently relevant even when ineffective. Inflammatory, complement, cytokine, and innate-recognition mechanisms are strong or supporting predictions, rather than equally well-documented responses. Antiviral processes have a much weaker specific connection; the fly is not an intracellular viral infection.

**Tolerance and resources.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, with increased parental provisioning apparently compensating for parasite costs; comparable compensation was absent in medium ground finches [3]. Mockingbird tolerance varied among years and declined under resource-poor, dry conditions [4]. Green Warbler-Finches showed no total provisioning increase, and female provisioning was lower with high infestation [5]. Energy balance, nutrient use, growth maintenance, and blood/tissue replacement therefore matter. Particular glucose, lipid, mitochondrial, or nutrient-signaling pathways remain supporting physiological inferences.

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Larval removal by preening or movement, adult fly capture, and nest-material inspection have been observed, including in Galápagos Flycatchers; observations do not establish sufficient population-level protection [7]. Finches apply guayabillo leaves to feathers, and extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth experimentally; protection of free-living birds remains a hypothesis [6]. Sensation, motor control, learning, and activity timing can support these behaviors, but generic neural development or synaptic processes are only indirect links.

**Stress and damage limitation.** A manipulation found no parasite-treatment effect on maternal corticosterone, hematocrit, or condition [8]. Endocrine stress regulation is thus plausible, not a demonstrated universal response. Redox balance, autophagy, proteostasis, and apoptosis were rated as potential downstream support, without assuming that the reviewed studies demonstrated those molecular responses.

## Rating interpretation

Scores represent potential physiological relevance, not beneficial direction, proven adaptation, or evidence of selection. Positive and negative regulation can both participate in defense or tolerance. General housekeeping processes received weak-link scores when their connection required several inferential steps; unrelated reproductive, radiation-specific, and particular embryonic-patterning processes received low scores. Mammalian “platelet” terminology was interpreted for its conserved hemostatic function relevant to avian thrombocytes.

The input label for GO:0160240 repeats its identifier. The official GO entry identifies it as “RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance,” a general transcription-quality-control process; its identifier was preserved unchanged: https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo/term/GO%3A0160240
