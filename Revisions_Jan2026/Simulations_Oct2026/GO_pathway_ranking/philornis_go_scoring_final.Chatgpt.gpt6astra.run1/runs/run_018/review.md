# A priori physiological relevance review: Philornis downsi

The ratings use published ecological, behavioral and physiological evidence plus general biology. They do not use host genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression or epigenomic results. Incidental search hits in those categories were excluded. No other rater's materials were consulted.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood feeding, injury and anemia.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; later larvae occupy nest material and feed externally on nestling blood, especially at night. Tissue damage and substantial blood loss cause anemia and mortality. Nestling growth and nasal/beak structure can also be impaired. These observations strongly motivate blood replacement, iron homeostasis, oxygen delivery, hemostasis, epithelial repair and extracellular-matrix remodeling, although these compensatory pathways were not all experimentally measured. Sources: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086

- **Immunological resistance.** Experimental parasite removal showed higher P. downsi-binding antibody levels in parasitized medium-ground-finch mothers. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, but did not rescue offspring survival. Males and nestlings lacked a detectable induced antibody response in that experiment. This directly supports adaptive immunity and strongly predicts roles for B cells, supporting T-cell processes and immune regulation. Innate inflammation, complement, leukocyte recruitment and clearance of damaged tissue remain biologically strong predictions, not demonstrated universal protective mechanisms. Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

- **Nutritional and developmental tolerance.** Parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging and received more food, apparently compensating for parasite damage. Comparable compensation was absent in medium ground finches. Mockingbird tolerance also varied across years and was weaker during drought. Green warbler-finches showed no increase in total provisioning; highly infested nests had lighter nestlings and lower female provisioning. Energy balance, nutrient handling, growth and allocation to blood/tissue replacement are therefore relevant, without assuming all hosts can compensate. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28792593/ ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ ; Pike et al. (2023), *Parental food provisioning and nestling growth under Philornis downsi parasitism*: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-023-02049-9

- **Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Observations include nestling preening, eating larvae, repositioning and standing on siblings, and maternal probing of nares and nest material. Such behavior varies among studies and does not establish reliable protection. Leaf rubbing with Psidium galapageium is observed, and extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth; its protective benefit in natural nests remains proposed. Birds also incorporate supplied insecticide-treated cotton into nests, reducing parasites, but this is human-assisted control. Sensory processing, motor performance and behavioral plasticity can support these mechanisms; highly specific neural developmental terms have weaker links. Sources: O'Connor et al. (2010), above; Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27721475/ ; Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/

- **Stress-response limits.** A parasite-removal experiment found no significant effect on baseline or stress-induced corticosterone, condition or hematocrit of breeding female medium ground finches. Endocrine stress pathways remain plausible and context dependent, rather than directly confirmed protective responses. Source: Knutie et al. (2013), *Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/

## Application of the scale

Each term was judged separately, without a target score distribution. A high score means mechanistic relevance, not proof of selection, beneficial upregulation or demonstrated protection. Both positive and negative regulators can be relevant. Broad housekeeping processes received weak or no specific links unless their stated function supplied a clearer physiological connection. Generic antibacterial defenses received supporting relevance because wounds can compromise barriers; viral, reproductive and embryonic specializations were not equated with anti-fly defense. Platelet terms were interpreted through the analogous avian thrombocyte/hemostasis function.
