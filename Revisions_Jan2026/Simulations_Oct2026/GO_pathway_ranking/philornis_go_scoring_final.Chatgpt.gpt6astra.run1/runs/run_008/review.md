# Independent a priori review: host responses to Philornis downsi

## Evidence and mechanisms

**Feeding injury, anemia, and blood replacement.** Early larvae occupy nestling nares and other cavities and damage tissue; later larvae reside in nest material and feed externally, especially at night. Tissue injury and blood loss impair nestling growth and survival. The life-cycle study distinguishes tissue damage from the anemia caused by hematophagy [1]. In small ground finches, greater parasite intensity was associated with lower hemoglobin and increased immature erythrocytes (reticulocytes), directly supporting compensatory erythropoiesis [2]. Thus blood formation is particularly relevant. Oxygen limitation, iron homeostasis, cardiovascular compensation, hemostasis, and tissue repair are strong physiological predictions, although those individual molecular processes were not all experimentally measured.

**Resistance and immune regulation.** Experimentally parasitized adult female medium ground finches produced more fly-binding antibodies. Their inverse association with parasite abundance suggested defensive activity but did not establish effective protection: parasitized nests still failed, and males and nestlings did not show comparable induced responses [3]. This supports adaptive immunity and its B-cell, T-cell, and signaling machinery. Innate recognition, leukocyte recruitment, inflammation, complement, and clearance of damaged tissue are plausible core defenses or responses to breached barriers; specific cytokines are less directly established than antibody responses. Regulation that restrains inflammation can also be relevant to tolerance.

**Tolerance, food acquisition, and resource allocation.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater parental provisioning and maintenance of reproductive success. Medium ground finches lacked the same compensatory response in that experiment [4]. A subsequent multiyear experiment found that mockingbird tolerance declined under limited rainfall, implicating resource availability and provisioning [5]. Energy balance, nutrient responses, mobilization of reserves, growth, and the resources required for blood and tissue replacement therefore have defensible links. This does not make every metabolic or biosynthetic process a specific parasite-response mechanism.

**Behavioral responses.** Video observations include preening, flapping, scratching, and occasional larval removal; their effectiveness is limited or uncertain [6,7]. Leaf anointing with insect-repellent plants is a proposed defense, not established protection against infestation [7]. Finches also incorporate experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton into nests, reducing parasite abundance [8]; this is an assisted behavioral intervention, not evidence for an endogenous insecticide pathway. Sensory, motor, learning, and circadian processes can support these behaviors, but generic synaptic machinery merits a weaker inference than the behavior itself.

**Important limits.** An experiment on breeding female medium ground finches found no parasite-treatment effect on baseline or stress-induced corticosterone, body condition, or hematocrit [9]. Endocrine stress pathways remain possible supporting mechanisms rather than demonstrated explanations for reproductive failure. Likewise, oxidative stress, secondary infection, and extensive cellular damage-control pathways are reasonable downstream possibilities, not directly proven universal consequences. Fly larvae are not viruses or intracellular pathogens.

## Scoring interpretation

Every term was judged separately, without a target score distribution. Score 5 identifies a process directly encompassing a documented mechanism or response; 4 identifies a core component or strong prediction; 3 a plausible supporting/downstream process; 2 a weak indirect connection; and 1 no specific link. Broad transcriptional machinery, meiosis, and unrelated embryonic organogenesis generally provide little parasite-specific information. Positive and negative regulation were judged for relevance, not assumed benefit. Platelet-specific terms were treated as indirect analogues of avian thrombocyte-mediated hemostasis.

Only the specified input file was read locally. No bird genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or selection results informed these judgments. The input's unnamed GO:0160240 was resolved as RNA polymerase II transcription initiation surveillance using the GO reference [10]; its supplied ID was retained unchanged.
