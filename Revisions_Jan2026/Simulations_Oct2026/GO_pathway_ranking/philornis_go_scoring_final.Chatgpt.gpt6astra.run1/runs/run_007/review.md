# Independent a priori assessment: Philornis downsi host-response relevance

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae inhabit the nares and other tissues; later larvae largely occupy nest material and feed externally on nestlings. Tissue destruction, substantial blood loss, anemia, impaired growth, mortality, and persistent naris damage provide the principal physiological anchors. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006) documented the larval stages and damage. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006) found lower hemoglobin with heavier infestation and increased immature erythrocytes, directly supporting compensatory blood-cell production. Hypoxia responses, iron handling, hemostasis, vascular integrity, epithelial repair, and extracellular-matrix remodeling are strong mechanistic predictions; their particular molecular pathways were not measured in those studies.

**Immune resistance.** Koop et al. (2013) experimentally demonstrated fly-induced binding antibodies in female medium ground finches. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with a tendency toward fewer parasites, but did not rescue fledging in that experiment. Males and young nestlings did not show equivalent induced responses. Knutie et al. (2016) did not detect significant antibody-mediated responses in either host species in their comparison. Thus adaptive/humoral immunity is documented, but effective protection is contingent rather than established universally. B-cell activation and differentiation, T-cell help, cytokines, inflammatory signaling, complement, and innate recognition are core or plausible supporting mechanisms. Antibacterial defense may support damaged skin and wound defense; virus-specific processes lack an equally specific connection to this blood-feeding fly.

**Behavioral resistance and its limits.** The McNew & Clayton (2018) review and Koop et al. (2013) describe or discuss preening, agitation, nest sanitation, and reduced brooding or standing to avoid larvae. Young nestlings may lack effective motor defenses; avoidance by females may also compromise nestling thermoregulation. Cimadom et al. (2016) documented leaf rubbing and showed that guayabillo extracts repel adult flies and impair larval growth, supporting a self-medication hypothesis without proving its protective effect in wild nests. Knutie et al. (2014) demonstrated that birds can incorporate experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton. This establishes a useful management-assisted behavior, not intrinsic pesticide production or a demonstrated natural detoxification adaptation.

**Tolerance and resource allocation.** Knutie et al. (2016) found increased begging and parental provisioning in parasitized mockingbirds, consistent with nutritional compensation; comparable responses were absent in their medium ground finches. McNew et al. (2019) showed that mockingbird tolerance varied among years and declined under low rainfall. Energy balance, nutrient use, growth, and sensory/motor processes supporting feeding therefore have plausible links, with specificity determining the score. Knutie et al. (2013) found no significant effect of parasitism on adult female corticosterone in their experiment, so a generalized endocrine stress response was not treated as demonstrated.

## Scoring decisions and limitations

All 1,008 input entries were considered individually, with no target score distribution. Scores distinguish directly represented documented mechanisms (5), core components or strong predictions (4), supporting processes (3), weak indirect links (2), and no identified specific link (1). Regulatory direction does not itself establish benefit or harm: both positive and negative regulation can contribute to controlled repair or immune responses. Broad housekeeping processes were not elevated merely because they are necessary for every organism. Early embryonic patterning, gamete-specific functions, and unrelated stimulus responses generally have little specific support. Platelet terms were interpreted cautiously through the conserved hemostatic roles of avian thrombocytes, without assuming birds possess mammalian platelets.

GO:0160240 has only its identifier as its supplied name. Exact-ID searches and attempts to consult public ontology services did not resolve its definition. Its required score of 1 is provisional, meaning no specific link could be identified from the available label; it is not evidence of biological irrelevance. This is the one entry whose semantic identity remains unverified.

Only the designated input file was read locally. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression findings from Galapagos birds were used, including incidental search-result material of that kind. Ratings concern potential biological relevance, not evidence that a pathway is under selection, that a response is beneficial, or that all hosts express it.
