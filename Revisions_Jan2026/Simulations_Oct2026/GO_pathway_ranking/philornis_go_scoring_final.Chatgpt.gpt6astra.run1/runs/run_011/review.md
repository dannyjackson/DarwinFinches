# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi host responses

The ratings use published ecological, behavioral, parasitological, and immunological evidence, together with general biological knowledge. No bird genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or selection results informed the scores. Each supplied GO entry was judged separately; no target score distribution was imposed.

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Adult flies are free-living; their larvae parasitize birds. Early larvae invade the nares and other tissues, consuming blood and tissue; later larvae live in nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Documented consequences include substantial blood loss, anemia, reduced hemoglobin, damaged nares, impaired growth, and nestling mortality [1,2]. The published Philornis review reports increased reticulocyte numbers in parasitized small ground finches, consistent with compensatory erythropoiesis [2]. Blood-cell replacement is therefore particularly relevant. Iron homeostasis, oxygen sensing, hemostasis, vascular responses, epithelial repair, and extracellular-matrix rebuilding are strong physiological predictions, although their individual molecular pathways have not generally been demonstrated in this host–parasite system.

**Immune resistance.** Experimental parasite reduction demonstrated a P. downsi-binding antibody response in adult female medium ground finches, but not equivalent responses in males or young nestlings. Higher female antibody levels were marginally associated with fewer parasites, without demonstrated rescue of reproductive success [3]. Another finch–mockingbird comparison found no significant antibody response [4], so acquired immunity is neither universal nor sufficient protection. Antibody production directly motivates adaptive and B-cell mechanisms; antigen handling, helper-cell functions, complement, inflammatory signaling, and regulation of tissue-damaging inflammation are mechanistic predictions. Skin inflammation and host effector molecules could interfere with feeding, as proposed in the immunological paper, but these specific effects were not established experimentally for P. downsi. Antiviral pathways are not direct anti-fly mechanisms. Antibacterial defense has a plausible secondary role around disrupted skin and wounds.

**Tolerance and energetic compensation.** Mockingbirds could maintain fledging success despite parasite burdens that harmed finches. Parasitized mockingbird nestlings begged more, with increased parental provisioning; this compensation was not demonstrated equivalently in medium ground finches [4]. Across years, mockingbird tolerance weakened under dry conditions and covaried with parental provisioning [5]. Nutrient sensing, energy homeostasis, glucose and lipid mobilization, protein supply, and growth maintenance therefore have plausible links. Oxidant control, mitochondrial maintenance, and cellular stress responses are supporting predictions, rather than established molecular defenses.

**Behavioral defense and associated physiology.** Observations include preening, nest sanitation, larval removal, agitation or repositioning, and nestling attempts to avoid larvae. Responses vary with age and species; young nestlings may lack effective motor defenses. Parasitized female finches reduced brooding and stood more, potentially avoiding larvae while altering offspring thermal conditions [3,6]. These observations motivate somatosensation, pain/irritation detection, motor control, behavioral learning, and thermoregulatory responses. Generic neural development and synaptic machinery have weaker links than processes specifying an applicable behavior.

Finches have also been observed rubbing Psidium galapageium leaves onto their feathers. Extracts repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth in experiments, but protection of wild birds by the behavior remains unproven [7]. Incorporation of human-provided insecticide-treated cotton demonstrates a nest-building route to parasite suppression [8]; it does not establish intrinsic host insecticide detoxification as a natural defense.

## Scoring interpretation and limitation

A score of 5 denotes a term directly encompassing a documented host response, not proof of protective benefit or selection by this parasite. Scores of 4 identify core machinery or strong predictions; 3 identifies plausible support; 2 denotes an indirect or speculative connection; 1 denotes no specific identified connection. Regulatory terms were judged for relevance regardless of whether they increase or decrease a process. Generic housekeeping, reproduction, and unrelated embryonic patterning were not elevated merely because all living hosts require them. Hemostatic GO labels referring to platelets were interpreted functionally in relation to avian thrombocytes.

The supplied label for **GO:0160240** is only the identifier itself. Exact-identifier searches and attempted ontology lookups did not yield a verifiable definition. It is retained unchanged and given a provisional score of **1**, reflecting no identifiable specific mechanism from the available information; this entry requires definition review before biological interpretation.
