# A priori review: host responses to Philornis downsi

The ratings use ecological, behavioral, immunological and physiological literature plus general biological knowledge. No results from genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression studies of Galapagos birds were used. A gene-expression study appeared in search results and was excluded. No other rater's files or results were consulted.

## Mechanisms and supporting literature

**Blood feeding, anemia and injury.** Adult flies are nonparasitic; early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues, whereas older larvae largely occupy nest material and feed externally on nestlings. Blood loss, anemia, nasal injury and mortality are documented. These findings motivate strong a priori links to hemostasis, replacement of erythrocytes, iron handling, oxygen supply, epithelial repair and vascular repair. The latter pathways are physiological predictions, not claims that their molecular activity was measured in these studies. Source: Fessl, Sinclair and Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ . Reduced hemoglobin associated with infestation was also reported by Dudaniec, Kleindorfer and Fessl (2006): https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/ .

**Immune resistance.** Experimental parasite removal showed parasite-binding antibody responses in adult female medium ground finches; neither males nor young nestlings showed the same detectable response. Higher female antibody levels tended to accompany fewer parasites, but this did not rescue nesting success. The paper discusses inflammation, interference with blood feeding and defensive molecules as potential mechanisms. Thus adaptive immunity is directly supported; B-cell processes and supportive T-cell pathways are core candidates. Innate recognition, inflammatory signaling, leukocyte recruitment and complement remain predictions rather than demonstrated effective resistance mechanisms in this system. Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Nest cameras documented preening, ingestion of larvae, nestlings climbing on one another, and female probing of nest material and nestling nares. Their occurrence depends on age and context: very young nestlings in the antibody experiment showed little effective defensive behavior. Sensory detection, motor behavior, learning and behavioral plasticity can support these defenses, but generic neuronal development has a much weaker association. Source: O'Connor et al. (2010), *Video analysis of host-parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches*: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086 .

**Tolerance and nutritional compensation.** Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging in an experiment, with associated increases in parental provisioning and maintained reproductive success despite parasitism. Medium ground finches did not show comparable compensation. Mockingbird tolerance subsequently varied with annual conditions and was reduced during limited rainfall. These results support energetic balance and nutrient supply as potential tolerance mechanisms; metabolic pathways are generally supporting processes, not automatically direct defenses. Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 ; McNew et al. (2019), https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2019.0049 . Green Warbler-Finches did not increase overall provisioning under greater infestation, and female provisioning decreased, reinforcing species-specific limitations: Pike et al. (2023), https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-023-02049-9 .

**Chemical avoidance through behavior.** Finch application of Psidium galapageium leaves to feathers is documented; experiments demonstrated repellent effects on adult flies and reduced larval growth. Its protective effectiveness on wild birds remains incompletely established. This supports behavioral innovation and sensory discrimination as possibilities, not endogenous insecticide synthesis by birds. Source: Cimadom et al. (2016), https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34559 .

**Stress and damage limitation.** Oxidative protection, apoptosis control and cellular stress management are plausible consequences of injury, oxygen limitation or energetic demand. They are generally supporting candidates. Endocrine stress responses cannot simply be assumed: an experiment found no significant parasite-treatment effect on baseline or acute stress-induced corticosterone in breeding female medium ground finches. Source: Knutie et al. (2013), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/ .

## Scoring interpretation and limitation

Scores reflect physiological relatedness, not effectiveness, expected selection direction, or evidence of selection. Both positive and negative regulators can govern a relevant response. A score of 5 is reserved for terms directly encompassing documented responses; 4 denotes core components or strong predictions; 3 denotes supporting mechanisms; 2 denotes indirect links; 1 denotes no identifiable specific connection. Generic housekeeping, embryonic patterning and gametogenesis do not gain high scores simply because all living hosts require them. Viral pathways receive low scores: pathway overlap with immunity does not establish that this fly is an intracellular or viral challenge. Platelet-specific terms receive a supporting score because avian thrombocytes, rather than mammalian platelets, perform the corresponding hemostatic functions.

This is an uncertainty flag, not evidence that the unidentified process is biologically unrelated; the entry should be revisited when its definition is available.
