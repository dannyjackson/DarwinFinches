# A priori review: host responses to Philornis downsi

## Evidence and mechanisms

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy the nares and other tissues; later larvae live in nest material and feed on nestlings, particularly at night. Their combined effects include tissue destruction, anemia, impaired growth, and substantial nestling mortality. The primary life-history study directly establishes these routes of injury: [Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), The life-cycle of Philornis downsi](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/). In small ground finches, greater infestation was associated with lower hemoglobin and more immature erythrocytes, directly supporting compensatory blood-cell production: [Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x). Iron balance, oxygen delivery, hemostasis, epithelial repair, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and vascular repair are therefore strong physiological predictions. Specific molecular pathways within those processes are predictions, not measurements from these studies.

**Immune resistance and its costs.** Experimental parasite removal demonstrated fly-binding antibody induction in adult female medium ground finches, but not males or nestlings. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer parasites, although protection was not established conclusively and parasitized nests still failed. Inflammation, itching, and disruption of parasite feeding were proposed mechanisms: [Koop et al. (2013), Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651). This supports adaptive immunity directly and B-cell function, helper-cell activity, cytokine regulation, and inflammatory signaling as mechanistic candidates. Complement and phagocytosis are strong general-biological predictions for defense and wound clearance, rather than demonstrated Philornis-specific molecular responses. Antibacterial processes have a plausible secondary connection through open wounds; antiviral processes lack an equivalent specific link.

**Tolerance through nutrition and growth.** In an experimental comparison, parasitized Galápagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging and received more provisioning, whereas medium ground finches did not show comparable compensation. Neither species showed a significant parasite-induced antibody response in that experiment: [Knutie et al. (2016), Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/). Mockingbird tolerance declined under dry conditions, with provisioning implicated in the difference: [McNew et al. (2019), Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/). Compensation is not universal: highly infested green warbler-finch nests had lower nestling mass and reduced female provisioning: [Pike et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-023-02049-9). These findings support energy balance, nutrient responses, replacement of lost tissue and blood, and maintenance of posthatching growth.

**Behavioral resistance.** Video observations documented nestling preening, ingestion of larvae, climbing onto nestmates, and female probing of nares and nest material: [O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010), Video analysis of host–parasite interactions in nests of Darwin's finches](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086). Such observations justify sensory and motor contributions without establishing effective parasite control. Finches also apply Psidium galapageium leaves to feathers; extracts repel adult flies and inhibit larval growth experimentally: [Cimadom et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34559). However, later observations found more leaf anointing outside breeding, weakening a specifically Philornis-directed explanation: [Tebbich et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1111/eth.13153). Learning, memory, and broad neural processes consequently receive modest supporting scores, not direct-mechanism scores.

**Stress and downstream effects.** An experiment found no parasite-treatment effect on adult female corticosterone, condition, or hematocrit: [Knutie et al. (2013), Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/). Endocrine responses remain plausible in other ages or conditions, but are not established here. Hypoxic and oxidative responses are physiological predictions from anemia, injury, inflammation, and energetic demands.

## Scoring approach

Scores reflect potential association, not proven protection, selection, or evolutionary direction. Both increased and decreased regulation can contribute to defense or tolerance. A score of 5 is reserved for processes directly encompassing documented responses; 4 denotes core components or strong predictions; 3 denotes plausible support; 2 denotes remote or nonspecific connections; and 1 denotes no specific connection. Generic transcription, replication, intracellular transport, embryonic patterning, and reproductive processes were not promoted merely because living cells require them. Platelet-related terms were interpreted through their conserved hemostatic relevance, recognizing that birds use nucleated thrombocytes.

All 1,008 terms were judged individually, without targeting a score distribution. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression study results were used.
