# A priori physiological review of Philornis downsi host responses

I rated all 1,008 input terms independently using the non-genomic literature below and general biological knowledge. Scores express mechanistic relevance, not evidence that a pathway was selected or that a response is effective. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or epigenomic findings were used. Broad housekeeping processes received weak scores unless their actual function supplied a more specific connection. No score distribution was targeted.

## Host injury and compensatory physiology

Early larvae occupy nestling nares and damage soft tissue; later larvae feed externally on blood from the nest material. Tissue injury and anemia are central proximate causes of impaired growth and mortality (Fessl et al. 2006). Lower hemoglobin and increased immature erythrocytes with greater infestation support erythrocyte replacement as a direct host response (Dudaniec et al. 2006). Thus erythropoiesis is directly relevant; hematopoiesis, iron management, responses to hypoxia, coagulation, vascular integrity, and replacement of damaged tissue are strong mechanistic predictions. Wound repair, epidermal/keratinocyte processes, extracellular matrix and angiogenesis are plausible repair mechanisms, without claiming their molecular operation has been demonstrated in these birds. More general energy metabolism, growth, cell proliferation, proteostasis and redox defenses are supporting processes. Blood loss does not specifically implicate every cardiac developmental or ion-channel process.

## Resistance and immune defenses

Koop et al. (2013) experimentally found increased fly-binding antibodies in parasitized medium ground finch mothers. Antibody levels were inversely associated with parasite abundance, but effective protection was not established, and males and nestlings did not show comparable induced responses. Other experiments found no significant antibody response in the tested host groups (Knutie et al. 2016). Consequently immune response and adaptive/B-cell mechanisms have direct or strong relevance, but cannot be described as universal effective defenses. Innate recognition, cytokine signaling, inflammatory regulation, complement, phagocytosis and leukocyte activity are reasonable mechanistic predictions or support for injury defense. Bacterial defense is additionally plausible at feeding wounds, but secondary infection is not assumed to be a proven cause of mortality. Specifically antiviral processes have little specific connection to this ectoparasite.

## Behavioral resistance, avoidance and tolerance

Observed or proposed defenses include preening and allopreening, larval removal/consumption, nest sanitation and moving above nestmates; their frequency and efficacy are limited or incompletely demonstrated (McNew and Clayton 2018). Nest construction can be harnessed experimentally: birds incorporate supplied permethrin-treated cotton, reducing parasite abundance (Knutie et al. 2014). This is an assisted behavioral intervention, not evidence for an evolved endogenous insecticide pathway. Sensation, pain detection, movement, learning and circadian timing can support behavioral defenses, but generic synaptic or neural-development terms are not automatically strongly relevant.

Mockingbirds can tolerate comparable parasite burdens through increased nestling begging and parental provisioning, whereas finches in the same study did not compensate similarly (Knutie et al. 2016). Tolerance varies among years and weakens under resource limitation (McNew et al. 2019). Energy homeostasis and nutrient allocation therefore have direct relevance; carbohydrate/lipid/amino-acid metabolism and growth signaling are supporting candidates. Nasal damage persists and affects adult foraging behavior (Kleindorfer et al. 2022), supporting consideration of repair and feeding performance without equating all craniofacial embryogenesis with a defense.
