# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi and host responses

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood loss, tissue injury, and development.** P. downsi adults are free-living; larvae parasitize birds. Early instars enter the nares and other tissues, while later instars generally occupy nest material and feed externally, predominantly at night. Feeding causes wounds, anemia, impaired growth, and frequent nestling death. Surviving birds can retain enlarged or deformed nares. Fessl, Sinclair and Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, documents these feeding stages and consequences: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ . This supports blood replacement, hemostasis, hypoxia compensation, and repair as relevant candidate mechanisms. Erythrocyte differentiation is particularly well grounded by reported compensatory immature erythrocytes; iron handling and hematopoietic progenitor differentiation are strong supporting predictions. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer and Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival*: https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/ . Coagulation, vascular repair, epithelial repair and inflammation are biological predictions, not claims that each specific molecular pathway has been measured in this system.

**Resistance and immunity.** Koop et al. (2013) experimentally found elevated fly-binding antibodies in mothers at parasitized nests, with stronger responses associated with fewer parasites. Males and nestlings lacked comparable induced responses, and maternal immunity did not rescue fledging in that experiment. Thus adaptive immunity is directly implicated, but effective protection cannot be assumed. B-cell development/activation, T-cell help, cytokine signaling and innate activation are strong supporting mechanisms rather than individually demonstrated defenses: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ . Bacterial defense could support wound protection, but secondary bacterial infection was not established by this review. Virus-specific processes have little specific connection to this fly; generic antiviral terms were not treated as demonstrated antiparasite defenses.

**Tolerance and nutrition.** Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, found greater begging and associated parental feeding in parasitized mockingbirds, consistent with compensatory nutrition. Similar compensation was not detected in medium ground finches, and neither species showed a significant induced antibody response in that experiment: https://doi.org/10.1890/15-0119.1 . *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites* further shows that mockingbird tolerance varies with environmental conditions and provisioning: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ . Energy balance, nutrient sensing and intermediary metabolism can therefore support tolerance; ubiquitous metabolic machinery alone is less specific. Growth and tissue maintenance are plausible consequences, while embryonic patterning and specialized organ development have much weaker links to the post-hatching injury.

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** Preening, scratching, larval removal, movements away from larvae and parental interactions with flies have been observed, although protection is incomplete. Parent presence affects fly access, and altered brooding may affect thermal conditions. Relevant evidence includes Pike et al., *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/ . Motor, sensory, learning and circadian pathways have possible supporting roles, but generic synaptic machinery is too nonspecific to count as a direct defense. Finch use of insecticide-treated nesting material demonstrates assisted self-fumigation, not an evolved intrinsic detoxification mechanism: Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.03.058 . Leaf-rubbing with potentially repellent plant material has been proposed as self-medication, but its protective effect remains uncertain. The synthesis by Fessl et al. (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi*, discusses these proposed behaviors and resistance/tolerance distinctions: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 .

**Limits and scoring.** A corticosterone experiment did not detect a treatment effect in breeding females, so an established maternal endocrine stress mechanism was not assumed: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/ . Regulatory direction alone does not establish benefit or harm. Generic transcription, transport and protein modification usually have weak links unless the term identifies a relevant mechanism.
