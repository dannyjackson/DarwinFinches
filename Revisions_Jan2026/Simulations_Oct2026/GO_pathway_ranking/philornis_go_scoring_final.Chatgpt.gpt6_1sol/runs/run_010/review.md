# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

This independent rating used the supplied GO terms, published ecological/physiological studies, and general biological knowledge. No genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results from Galápagos birds were used. Scores describe mechanistic relevance, not evidence that a particular GO pathway is selected or activated in these birds.

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nestling nares and consume tissue; later larvae shelter in nests and feed externally, often at night. Blood loss, anemia, lowered hemoglobin, damaged nasal structures, reduced growth, and mortality establish strong priors for erythrocyte production, hematopoiesis, iron balance, hemostasis, oxygen delivery, and tissue repair. These compensatory pathways are predictions from documented injury unless directly measured; they are not all demonstrated defenses. Nasal injury can also impair subsequent feeding and song. Sources: Fessl et al., *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi...* (2006), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; Kleindorfer et al., *Functional traits and foraging behaviour...* (2022), https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.14061 .

**Resistance and immunological limits.** Experimental parasite removal showed that female medium ground finches at parasitized nests produced stronger fly-binding antibody responses. Higher antibody levels tended to accompany lower parasite abundance, but this did not rescue fledging success. Males and young nestlings did not show comparable induced responses. Antibody-mediated resistance is documented; lymphocyte activation/differentiation and cytokine signaling are components or strong predictions. The authors propose inflammation, defensive peptides, and impaired parasite feeding as possible effectors, rather than demonstrating each molecular pathway. Innate defenses and regulation of inflammatory damage are therefore relevant, with uncertainty. Source: Koop et al., *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds...* (2013), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

**Tolerance and energetic compensation.** In an experimental comparison, mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater parental provisioning and tolerance despite parasite densities similar to finches. Finches did not show the same compensatory response. Subsequent work showed that tolerance depends on environmental conditions and resource availability. This supports energy balance, nutrient use, growth maintenance, and feeding-related behavior; particular metabolic enzymes remain supporting or indirect candidates. Sources: Knutie et al., *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites...* (2016), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ ; McNew et al., *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites* (2019), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ .

**Behavior and physical defenses.** Preening, agitation, and repositioning have been observed, but effective defense is limited by nestling age and motor ability. Changes in maternal brooding may alter thermoregulation. Video observations also suggest loss of nasal mucosal structures could affect respiratory protection and water retention; these are plausible consequences rather than established secondary infections. Leaf anointing with guayabillo has been proposed as repellent self-medication, with uncertain adaptive function. Experimentally supplied permethrin-treated cotton can be incorporated into nests and reduce fly infestation; this is assisted behavior, not proof of an evolved insecticide defense. Sources: O’Connor et al., *Video analysis of host–parasite interactions...* (2010), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/oryx/article/video-analysis-of-hostparasite-interactions-in-nests-of-darwins-finches/BF956C9751763DAFAEE36345498A4A01 ; Tebbich et al., *Darwin’s finches habitually anoint their feathers...* (2021), https://doi.org/10.1111/eth.13153 ; Knutie et al., *Darwin’s finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton* (2014), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/ .

## Scoring interpretation

5 denotes a term directly capturing a documented host mechanism, chiefly adaptive immune response. 4 denotes core immune components or strong predictions such as erythropoiesis, hypoxia response, wound repair, and coagulation. 3 denotes plausible supporting processes, including barrier maintenance, repair signaling, energetic compensation, oxidative protection, and cellular movement. 2 denotes weak or indirect links, including generic transport/signaling machinery and most specialized neural processes without demonstrated behavioral specificity. 1 denotes no specific mechanistic connection, such as meiosis, many embryonic patterning processes, and detailed RNA/DNA housekeeping processes. Scores were assessed individually; essentiality alone was not treated as evidence of a fly response. Avian thrombocytes, rather than mammalian platelets, motivate relevance of conserved coagulation-associated terms. Antiviral pathways received only indirect relevance because this literature does not establish the fly as a viral challenge. The input gives GO:0160240 only as its own ID; its identity could not be established from the literature lookup, so it received 1 rather than an invented interpretation. No target score distribution was imposed.
