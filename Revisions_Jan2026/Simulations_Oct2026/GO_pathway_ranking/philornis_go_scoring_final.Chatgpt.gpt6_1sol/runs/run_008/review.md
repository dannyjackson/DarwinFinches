# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

The scores assess potential host responses, not evidence that a particular pathway has evolved in these birds. Only the supplied input was read locally. Published natural-history, experimental parasitology, immunological, and behavioral evidence informed the assessment; no bird genomic, transcriptomic, or gene-expression results were used. A search incidentally returned a mixed gene-expression study, which was excluded.

## Mechanisms and evidence

**Blood loss and tissue injury.** Early larvae occupy nares and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally on nestlings. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, reports substantial blood loss. This establishes a strong prior for erythrocyte production, iron management, hemostasis, wound responses, and tissue repair. Hypoxia responses and vascular repair are strong physiological predictions; their molecular pathways need not have been measured to be relevant. Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006) links parasitism with reduced hemoglobin and altered immature erythrocyte counts, supporting compensatory blood-cell production rather than assuming anemia is just a generic stressor.

- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-the-introduced-ectoparasite-philornis-downsi-on-haemog/

**Immune resistance.** Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*, experimentally demonstrated fly-binding antibody responses in nesting female medium ground finches. Higher antibody levels tended to accompany fewer parasites, but the association was marginal and did not establish protection: parasitized nests still failed. Males and nestlings showed no detectable induced antibody response. Adaptive immunity and B-cell mechanisms therefore have particularly strong priors, without assuming all ages or species mount effective responses. Inflammation and defensive molecules that could impede larval feeding are proposed mechanisms; cytokine signaling, innate immunity, leukocyte recruitment, and complement receive grades reflecting this distinction. Virus-specific mechanisms are much less directly applicable to a blood-feeding ectoparasite.

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

**Tolerance through nutrition.** Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, found that parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with increased parental provisioning; equivalent compensation was not evident in medium ground finches. McNew et al. (2019) showed that mockingbird tolerance declined in dry years, implicating limits to nutritional compensation. Energy homeostasis and nutrient responses consequently have strong priors. Glucose/lipid/amino-acid metabolism and mitochondrial energy production are supporting processes, not direct demonstrations of particular molecular adaptations.

- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2019.0049

**Behavioral resistance and avoidance.** O'Connor, Robertson & Kleindorfer (2010) documented nestling preening, larval consumption, and standing on nestmates, with female probing of nares and nest material. Pike et al. (2021) additionally recorded Galapagos flycatcher predation on flies and larval removal. These observations support sensory, motor, and learning processes indirectly; they do not make every neural developmental pathway a parasite-defense mechanism. Knutie et al. (2014) demonstrated nest incorporation of insecticide-treated cotton, a behavior aided by human intervention rather than proof of naturally evolved insecticide production or detoxification.

- https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605310000086
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/

**Persistent structural effects.** Kleindorfer & Sulloway (2009) documented naris and beak malformation. Such damage supports local epithelial/skin repair and morphogenesis only where biologically appropriate; it does not specifically implicate unrelated embryonic organ patterning. McNew & Clayton's (2018) review provides the resistance/tolerance/avoidance framework and emphasizes that observed defenses may remain inadequate.

- https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-8312.2009.01309.x
- https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103

## Scoring interpretation

Each term was assessed individually. Five denotes a direct match to a documented mechanism (for example erythrocyte differentiation or immune response); four a core component or strong prediction (such as coagulation, iron homeostasis, skin repair, and inflammation); three plausible support (such as energy metabolism, oxidative-stress handling, and general cell migration); two weak or nonspecific connections, including much cellular housekeeping; one no specific physiological link, especially unrelated embryonic anatomy, gametogenesis, and specialized neural construction. Negative regulation can be relevant to controlling damage and was not automatically downgraded. Broad terms were not treated as proof of specific downstream mechanisms. Platelet terms were interpreted cautiously as supporting avian thrombocyte-mediated hemostasis, not evidence that birds possess mammalian platelets.
