# Independent literature review and scoring rationale

Published parasitological, ecological, physiological and behavioral studies informed these ratings. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or selection results from Galápagos birds were used. Each term was judged individually without a target score distribution.

## Mechanisms

**Blood loss and tissue damage.** Free-living adult P. downsi produce parasitic larvae. Young instars infest nares and tissues; older instars generally hide in nests and feed externally, especially at night. Blood loss, anemia, lesions, impaired growth and mortality establish strong a priori connections to erythrocyte production, iron handling, hypoxia responses, hemostasis and tissue repair. These are mechanistic predictions from documented injuries, not demonstrations of every corresponding host pathway. Oxidative-stress management, cell survival and energy metabolism are plausible supporting processes.

**Immune resistance.** Fly-binding antibodies are documented in adult female medium ground finches, including an experimental parasite-removal study. Higher maternal antibodies were associated with fewer parasites, but effective causal protection was not established and offspring survival was not rescued. Comparable responses were absent in males and nestlings in that experiment, and a later finch–mockingbird comparison found no significant antibody-mediated response. Acquired immunity therefore has direct relevance without implying universal or effective protection. B-cell functions are core components; innate recognition, cytokines, inflammation, phagocytosis and leukocyte recruitment are strong predictions from antigen exposure and injury, rather than documented fly-specific molecular mechanisms. Antibacterial protection at wounds is plausible; antiviral responses have little specific support.

**Behavioral resistance.** Observations include preening, occasional eating of larvae, parental probing of nest material and nestling nares, scratching, and standing above nestmates. Galápagos flycatchers capture adult flies and remove larvae. Sensory detection and movement support these defenses, but the literature does not establish specific neurotransmitters or neural developmental programs. Proposed avoidance also includes nest positioning and reproductive timing. Feather anointing with native guava leaves is observed, and extracts repel flies in laboratory tests; a field benefit against P. downsi remains uncertain. Permethrin-treated cotton enables experimentally assisted nest self-fumigation, not a demonstrated endogenous detoxification adaptation.

**Tolerance and nutrition.** Parasitized mockingbird nestlings can increase begging, apparently driving greater parental provisioning and compensating for damage. Comparable compensation was absent in the studied finches. Mockingbird tolerance varies among years and weakens during drought, supporting resource constraints. Nutrient use and energy homeostasis are plausible physiological support, while ubiquitous individual metabolic pathways carry less specific evidence.

## Scoring interpretation

Score 5 directly describes a documented host mechanism; 4 indicates a core component or strong mechanistic prediction; 3 covers plausible support or downstream effects; 2 indicates a weak or indirect link; 1 indicates no specific link. General RNA processing, genome maintenance, meiosis and unrelated embryonic morphogenesis usually lack a specific connection. A low score does not imply biological unimportance; a high score does not establish adaptation or successful defense.
