# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

## Literature basis

Early larvae occupy nestling nares and consume host tissue; later larvae live in nest material and take blood meals from nestlings. Blood loss, reduced haemoglobin, poor growth, mortality, and persistent nasal injury make restoration of blood cells, haemostasis, barrier repair, and compensation for impaired oxygen delivery the central physiological expectations. These are distinct from a claim that every supporting molecular pathway has been measured in this system.

* Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*. Larval natural history and substantial nestling blood loss. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182006001089
* Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), *Effects ... on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin's small ground finch*. Reduced haemoglobin and increased immature erythrocytes support regenerative erythropoiesis as a host response. The reticulocyte finding was accessible through the contemporaneous review by Dudaniec & Kleindorfer, rather than independently verified from the complete primary paper. Primary DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x ; review: https://doi.org/10.1071/MU04040

Adult female medium ground finches experimentally exposed to flies produced more fly-binding antibodies. Higher antibody levels were associated with somewhat fewer parasites, but did not rescue reproduction in that experiment. Neither adult males nor young nestlings showed the same response. Thus adaptive immunity is directly relevant, while B-cell activation/differentiation and associated T-cell support are core predictions. Innate recognition, inflammation, cytokine regulation, complement and phagocytic clearance are credible supporting defenses or repair mechanisms; specific effectors have not all been established against this fly. Excessive inflammation can also damage hosts, so regulatory and negative-regulatory terms retain relevance.

* Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

Tolerance must be distinguished from reducing parasite abundance. In experimental comparisons, parasitized Galapagos mockingbird nestlings increased begging, and greater begging was associated with parental provisioning and compensation for fly damage. Medium ground finches did not show an equivalent response. Energy balance, nutrient acquisition and utilisation, growth maintenance, and blood replacement therefore have an independent tolerance rationale. Generic metabolic processes receive only intermediate or weak scores unless their link is especially specific.

* Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/

Observed or proposed behavioral defenses include nest sanitation, self- and allopreening, larva removal, nestling agitation/repositioning, and altered brooding. Expression varies with age and species and is often insufficient. Avoidance through nesting choices and timing is plausible but not uniformly demonstrated. General neural and sensory machinery is consequently a less specific prediction than these behaviors themselves.

* McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi, an Introduced Parasite of Galapagos Birds*. Review of resistance, tolerance, avoidance and behavioral limitations. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103
* *Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi ... in and Near Host Nests in the Galapagos Islands*. Fly timing and exposure context, including mostly nocturnal larval feeding and documented defensive observations. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/

Glucocorticoid-mediated stress compensation is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated universal explanation: experimental manipulation did not detect a maternal corticosterone response. This limits scores for nonspecific hormone/stress pathways. Likewise, secondary bacterial defense and oxidative protection are plausible consequences of wounds and physiological stress, rather than proven main effects.

* Knutie et al. (2013), *Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin's finches*. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0016648013003134

## Scoring interpretation

Scores assess potential association rather than benefit, proof of selection, or expression change. A 5 marks directly represented antibody/adaptive responses, regenerative erythropoiesis, or wound recovery. A 4 marks core immune components and strong predictions from blood loss or tissue injury. A 3 marks credible supporting metabolism, repair, signaling or behavioral physiology. A 2 marks weakly connected generic cellular machinery or speculative connections. A 1 marks processes without a specific rationale, including many embryonic organ patterns and gamete processes. Platelet terms are interpreted cautiously as physiological haemostatic analogues in birds, which have thrombocytes. Highly specific neural developmental terms are not automatically promoted because preening involves the nervous system. GO:0160240 has no informative name in the input and could not be resolved in the searches; it receives 2 for unresolved/weak specificity, not an invented mechanism.

All terms were considered individually, without a target score distribution. Only the supplied input file was read locally. No genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression, or epigenomic results from Galapagos birds were used; incidental search matches to such studies were excluded as evidence.
