# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi host responses

## Parasite injury and candidate physiological mechanisms

Early larvae invade nestling nares and other tissues; older larvae live in nest material and feed on nestlings, chiefly at night. Blood withdrawal and tissue destruction cause anemia, impaired growth, deformity and mortality. Incubating females can also be attacked. These observations give strong mechanistic priors for compensatory erythropoiesis, iron regulation, hypoxia responses, hemostasis, skin/barrier restoration and wound repair. These compensatory mechanisms are predictions from injury, rather than claims that each molecular pathway has been demonstrated in finches. Tissue repair and controlled inflammation could confer tolerance without reducing larval numbers. Nasal damage can persist, affecting foraging and song; generic neural development or skeletal development is nevertheless only indirectly connected.

Sources: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*, Parasitology, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ ; Cimadom et al. (2021), *Timing of infestation influences virulence and parasite success*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7882474/ ; Kleindorfer et al. (2022), *Functional traits and foraging behaviour: Avian vampire fly larvae change the beak and fitness of their Darwin's finch hosts*, https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.14061 .

## Resistance: immunity and behavior

An experimental reduction of parasites showed elevated fly-binding antibodies in female medium ground finches from parasitized nests. Higher maternal antibody levels were associated with fewer flies, but this did not establish antibody-mediated protection or rescue fledging success. Fathers and nestlings did not show the same induced response. Consequently adaptive immunity is directly relevant, with B-cell processes core components; innate recognition, complement, inflammatory regulation and immune-cell recruitment are reasonable predictions rather than documented pathway-level responses. Viral-specific defenses receive little weight: the fly's hematophagy alone does not establish viral transmission.

Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ .

Preening/removing larvae, agitation and repositioning, and standing on nestmates have been observed, but evidence of effective resistance is limited. Changes in female brooding may impair chick thermoregulation. Avoidance through nest placement or breeding timing is proposed; adaptive avoidance has not been established generally in Galapagos hosts. Four finch species were observed rubbing guayabillo leaves into feathers. Laboratory extracts repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth; this does not demonstrate protection in natural nests. Finches also incorporate experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton, demonstrating a possible behavioral route for assisted defense, not an endogenous detoxification mechanism.

Sources: McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi*, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 ; Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/ ; Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/ .

## Tolerance and energetic compensation

In experimental comparisons, Galapagos mockingbirds retained reproductive performance despite infestation, whereas medium ground finches were harmed. Increased nestling begging and correlated parental provisioning supported energetic compensation as a tolerance mechanism in mockingbirds. This response was not detected in the compared finches; neither species showed a significant antibody response in that experiment. A four-season mockingbird study found that tolerance varied among years and was poorer in dry conditions, with provisioning consistent with resource-dependent compensation. Thus nutrient sensing, energy homeostasis, glucose and lipid utilization, growth maintenance and stress physiology are relevant, but most individual metabolic processes remain supporting mechanisms rather than demonstrated fly-specific adaptations.

Sources: Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ ; McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/ .

## Scoring interpretation

Each supplied GO term was evaluated on its own physiological specificity. Scores of 5 identify processes directly encompassing documented immune responses or energetic compensation. Scores of 4 identify core immune components and strong predictions from blood loss or tissue injury. Scores of 3 indicate plausible supporting metabolism, repair, inflammatory signaling or behavioral capacity. Scores of 2 indicate generic cellular machinery or weak physiological connections; scores of 1 indicate processes with no specific connection, such as many meiotic, embryonic patterning, RNA-specialist, DNA-repair and viral processes. General necessity for life was not treated as sufficient evidence of a strong link. Activating and inhibitory regulation can both be relevant to host defense or damage limitation. Avian thrombocytes fulfill hemostatic roles, so platelet-labeled processes are treated as functional analogues, not evidence that birds have mammalian platelets. No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression results from Galapagos birds were used, and no other local files were consulted.
