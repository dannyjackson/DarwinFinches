# Independent a priori review: Philornis downsi host responses

## Literature and physiological rationale

Philornis downsi larvae attack nestlings and can feed on brooding females. Early larvae invade nares and other tissues; older larvae generally live in nest material and emerge to feed externally, particularly at night. Blood removal and tissue injury produce anemia, impaired growth and high mortality. Survivors can retain enlarged nares, with associated changes in song and foraging. These observations directly motivate considering blood replacement and injury responses; hemostasis, iron handling, oxygen homeostasis and repair of skin, epithelium and extracellular matrix are strong physiological predictions, rather than all being experimentally established defenses.

- Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), “The life-cycle of Philornis downsi … and its impacts on nestling survival.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl (2006), “Effects … on haemoglobin level and nestling survival in Darwin’s Small Ground Finch.” Lower hemoglobin accompanies heavier parasitism; increased reticulocyte counts in anemic nestlings support compensatory erythropoiesis. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x
- Kleindorfer et al. (2019), “Introduced parasite changes host phenotype, mating signal and hybridization risk.” https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2019.0461
- Kleindorfer et al. (2022), “Functional traits and foraging behaviour: Avian vampire fly larvae change the beak and fitness of their Darwin’s finch hosts.” https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.14061

Immune resistance has some direct support, but effectiveness and host life stage matter. Experimental parasite removal showed higher fly-binding antibodies in exposed medium ground finch mothers. Higher antibody levels tended to accompany fewer parasites, but this did not rescue reproductive success; males and nestlings did not show equivalent induced defenses. Antibody-associated adaptive immunity therefore has direct relevance. B-cell processes and supporting T-cell processes are core predicted components. Innate immunity, inflammatory signaling and complement are plausible defenses or injury responses, but these specific pathways should not be represented as established effective mechanisms against this fly. Antibacterial defense has an indirect wound-associated rationale; antiviral pathways lack a comparable specific rationale.

- Koop et al. (2013), “Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds.” https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.651

Tolerance can involve energy compensation. In an experimental comparison, parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater provisioning, and tolerated parasite loads that harmed finches. This capacity varied among years, with reduced tolerance during low rainfall. Nutrient sensing, glucose/energy regulation and resource mobilization are consequently relevant; individual general metabolic processes are supporting mechanisms rather than evidence of a specific fly defense. Adult female finch corticosterone did not differ with experimental parasite exposure, so a universal induced endocrine stress response is not established.

- Knutie et al. (2016), “Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin’s finches.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- “Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites” (2019). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/
- Knutie et al. (2013), “Experimental test of the effect of introduced hematophagous flies on corticosterone levels of breeding Darwin’s finches.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23892015/

Behavioral resistance includes occasional larval removal through preening, scratching or consumption, and parental probing of nestlings/nest material. These observations do not demonstrate reliable protection. Postural changes and nocturnal feeding provide a rationale for behavioral and circadian responses, without making every neural-development or synaptic term strongly associated. Birds also incorporate experimentally supplied permethrin-treated cotton, which can reduce parasites; this is an assisted material-use behavior, not evidence of endogenous insecticide metabolism.

- “Behavior of the Avian Parasite Philornis downsi … in and Near Host Nests in the Galapagos Islands.” https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/
- Knutie et al. (2014), “Darwin’s finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/
- McNew & Clayton (2018), “Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi.” https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103

## Scoring interpretation

Each supplied term was judged independently. Scores distinguish directly documented responses (5), core components or strong predictions (4), plausible supporting processes (3), weak links (2), and processes without a specific link (1). Generic housekeeping necessity alone does not establish association. Developmental specificity, unrelated pathogens, and cellular mechanisms were considered on their own merits. Platelet terms received supporting rather than maximal scores because avian hemostasis uses nucleated thrombocytes. No score distribution was targeted. Genomic, transcriptomic and gene-expression results from Galápagos birds were not used.
