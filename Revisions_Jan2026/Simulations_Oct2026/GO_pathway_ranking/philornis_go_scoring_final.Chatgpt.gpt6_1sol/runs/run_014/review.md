# A priori review: host responses to Philornis downsi

## Evidence and mechanisms

**Host injury.** Adult flies are free living; their larvae parasitize birds. Early instars invade nestling nares and other tissues, while older larvae shelter in nest material and take blood meals, frequently at night. Tissue injury and substantial blood loss cause anemia, impaired growth, and mortality. Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ...*, documents these mechanisms: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/ . This supports strong prior links to erythrocyte replacement, iron balance, oxygen sensing, hemostasis, epithelial protection and repair. These compensatory processes are biological predictions; the paper does not establish each molecular pathway as an effective defense.

**Immune resistance.** Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*, experimentally found higher fly-binding antibody levels in mothers at infested nests. Antibody levels correlated inversely with parasite numbers, but causality and protective efficacy remain uncertain: no parasitized nests fledged young in that experiment. Males and nestlings did not exhibit corresponding induced responses. The authors discuss inflammatory skin responses, defensive molecules and antibody-associated itching as possible mechanisms. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/ . Acquired immunity therefore has direct support; B-cell functions are strong mechanistic predictions, while individual inflammatory, innate and cytokine pathways have varying inferential distance. Absence of a measured nestling antibody response does not establish that all immunity is irrelevant.

**Tolerance and nutritional compensation.** Knutie et al. (2016), *Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*, found comparable parasite densities but different fitness effects. Parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater parental provisioning, whereas finches did not show corresponding increases. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/ . Energy balance, nutrient handling and growth maintenance are consequently relevant, without implying that every metabolic process directly mediates tolerance.

**Behavioral defense and avoidance.** McNew & Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies ...*, reviews occasional preening/removal or consumption of larvae, probing of nestlings' nares, and nestling positioning. Nest placement and breeding timing may alter exposure. Females may reduce brooding when heavily parasitized, potentially impairing nestling thermoregulation. These observations support sensory, motor, learning and thermoregulatory processes at differing degrees of specificity; generic nervous-system development is a weak inference. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103 . Cimadom et al. (2016) observed finches applying Psidium galapageium leaves to feathers; extracts repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth experimentally. A protective benefit in wild birds was proposed rather than demonstrated. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/ .

**Stress is not automatically an established response.** A parasite-removal experiment found no significant parasite-treatment effect on the acute corticosterone response of breeding females. Hormonal and stress pathways remain plausible but should not be treated as universally demonstrated defenses. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383185/ .

## Scoring principles

Each term was assessed for the host process it names, not for possible roles of particular genes. Scores distinguish directly supported mechanisms (5), core components or strong predictions (4), plausible support/downstream processes (3), weak generic connections (2), and no specific link (1). An injury consequence can motivate a compensatory-response prediction but does not prove that response occurs. Broad housekeeping pathways received weak scores; organ-specific embryogenesis, reproduction, radiation responses and virus-specific processes generally lack a fly-specific prior. Antiviral pathways were not treated as evidence of resistance to an extracellular fly. GO:0160240 has only its identifier as its supplied name and could not be resolved by the search; its score of 1 records no identifiable specific link, rather than a known negative mechanism.

Only the supplied term file was read locally. No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression results from Galapagos birds were used; incidental search results concerning such work were excluded. The review and scores concern plausible associations, not proof that selection on a term is caused by this parasite.
