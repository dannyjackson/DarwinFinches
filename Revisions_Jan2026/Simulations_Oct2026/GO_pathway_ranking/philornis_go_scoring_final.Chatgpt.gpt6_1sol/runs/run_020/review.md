# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

## Evidence and mechanisms

The fly's adults are free living; the damaging stages are larvae. Early instars occupy the nostrils and other tissues, while later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally on nestlings, especially at night. Tissue destruction and repeated blood withdrawal cause anemia and can kill nestlings. Fessl, Sinclair and Kleindorfer (2006) described substantial blood losses and high mortality. These observations provide strong physiological grounds for considering erythrocyte replacement, hematopoiesis, hemostasis, iron availability, oxygen supply, epithelial integrity and wound repair. Repair and compensatory hematopoiesis are predictions from the documented damage, rather than demonstrations that particular molecular pathways confer resistance.

- Fessl et al. (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi (Diptera: Muscidae) parasitizing Darwin's finches and its impacts on nestling survival*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/
- McNew and Clayton (2018), *Alien Invasion: Biology of Philornis Flies Highlighting Philornis downsi, an Introduced Parasite of Galápagos Birds*: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ento-020117-043103

An experimental study demonstrated increased fly-binding antibodies in parasitized medium ground finch mothers. Higher antibody levels tended to accompany fewer parasites, but the response did not rescue reproductive success. Fathers and young nestlings did not show equivalent induced defenses. This supports adaptive immunity and its B-cell and T-cell support machinery without implying that all immune pathways have been demonstrated. Innate recognition, inflammatory signaling, phagocytosis and protection against secondary microbial contamination are plausible predictions; antiviral pathways are much less specifically connected to this extracellular insect parasite.

- Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds: Darwin's finches and introduced nest flies*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

Tolerance can differ from resistance. Experimental comparison found that parasitized mockingbird nestlings begged more, with increased parental food delivery consistent with compensation for parasite damage. Comparable compensation was not detected in medium ground finches. Neither species showed a significant antibody response in that comparison, illustrating variation among host ages, species and studies. Four years of experimental mockingbird work further showed that tolerance declined in dry years, when parental provisioning could not adequately compensate. Nutritional sensing, glucose and lipid metabolism, energy balance, growth and physiological stress therefore have plausible relevance; this does not establish any particular ubiquitous metabolic pathway as a direct defense.

- Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/
- McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963843/ ; https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2019.0049

Potential behavioral defenses include self-preening, allopreening, nest sanitation and nestling agitation or avoidance. Their occurrence and efficacy vary, particularly with nestling age. Parasitized females may brood less and stand more, potentially reducing their own exposure while compromising nestling thermoregulation. Guava-leaf rubbing is a proposed self-medication behavior supported by repellent experiments, but its protective effect in occupied nests remains uncertain. Collection of experimentally supplied insecticide-treated cotton demonstrates that nest-building behavior can be harnessed for control; it does not demonstrate an evolved pesticide-detoxification mechanism. Sensory perception, learning and motor functions were treated as supporting or indirect, rather than evidence for arbitrary neural developmental processes.

- Behavioral evidence and limitations: Koop et al. (2013), above, and McNew and Clayton (2018), above.
- Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*: https://www.nature.com/articles/srep34559
- Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24801182/

## Scoring interpretation

Scores apply to the biological process itself, not to whether every gene annotated to it could conceivably influence fitness. Direct immune responses receive the strongest ratings; blood replacement and wound-related mechanisms receive strong predicted relevance. More generic signaling, metabolism, cellular stress, tissue maintenance and behavior-related processes receive supporting or weak ratings according to specificity. Specialized embryonic patterning, reproduction, genome maintenance and many basal RNA processes have no specific demonstrated connection and generally receive low ratings. Regulatory direction alone was not interpreted as beneficial or harmful: limiting an excessive response can also aid tolerance. No score distribution was targeted.

Only the supplied input file was read from the machine. Genomic, transcriptomic, gene-expression and epigenomic results were excluded from the evidence used for scoring, including incidental search hits.
