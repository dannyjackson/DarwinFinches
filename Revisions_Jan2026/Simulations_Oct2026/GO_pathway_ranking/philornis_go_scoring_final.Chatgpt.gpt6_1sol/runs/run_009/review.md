# A priori physiological relevance to Philornis downsi

The scores use published parasitological, immunological, physiological and behavioral evidence, together with general biology. They do not use selection results, host genomic studies, transcriptomic studies or gene-expression results. An excluded expression study appeared incidentally in search results; its findings were disregarded.

## Mechanisms and evidence

- **Blood feeding, tissue injury and anemia.** First and early second instars occupy nestling nostrils and other tissues; later larvae shelter in nest material and feed externally. Blood loss, nasal injury, impaired growth and mortality establish a direct physiological challenge. This supports strong a priori relevance of erythrocyte replacement, hematopoiesis, iron handling, oxygen sensing and hemostasis. These compensatory pathways are predictions from the documented damage, rather than claims that each molecular mechanism has been demonstrated in finches. Source: Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer (2006), *The life-cycle of Philornis downsi ... and its impacts on nestling survival*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16899139/

- **Immune resistance.** An abundance-manipulation experiment demonstrated increased fly-binding antibodies in adult female medium ground finches. Higher maternal antibodies tended to accompany fewer parasites, but this relationship was marginal, and immunity did not rescue reproductive success. Adult males and nestlings did not show the same induced antibody response. Antibody-mediated adaptive defense is therefore directly supported; B-cell activation and differentiation, T-cell help, cytokine signaling and complementary innate mechanisms are components or predictions, with effectiveness left open. Skin inflammation and itching were explicitly discussed as potential means of impeding feeding and prompting preening, not established as effective mechanisms in this experiment. Source: Koop et al. (2013), *Experimental demonstration of a parasite-induced immune response in wild birds*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/

- **Tolerance through nutrition and parental behavior.** Experimental comparisons found that parasitized mockingbird nestlings increased begging, associated with greater provisioning, whereas medium ground finches did not show equivalent compensation. Neither species showed a significant antibody-mediated response in that comparison. Energy acquisition and allocation can therefore buffer damage without reducing parasite abundance. This motivates relevance of energy homeostasis, nutrient sensing and metabolic support, without treating all metabolic functions as direct fly defenses. Source: Knutie et al. (2016), *Galápagos mockingbirds tolerate introduced parasites that affect Darwin's finches*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27220210/

- **Environmental limits and behavioral resistance.** A four-season mockingbird experiment found that tolerance weakened in low-rainfall years; provisioning and food availability offer an explanation. The paper also reviews nestling preening and standing on nestmates as attempted resistance whose fitness benefits remain uncertain. These findings support resource-dependent tolerance and sensory/motor behavior, but do not justify assuming that any neural developmental process is a specific defense. Source: McNew et al. (2019), *Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance to parasites*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/

- **Repellency and nest behavior.** Finches were observed rubbing Psidium galapageium leaves into feathers. Extracts repelled adult flies and inhibited larval growth experimentally; protection of free-living birds was proposed rather than established. A separate cotton experiment showed that finches can incorporate supplied permethrin-treated fibers into nests, reducing infestation. These support the possibility of behavioral protection and nest-material use, while distinguishing assisted control from an evolved physiological defense. Sources: Cimadom et al. (2016), *Darwin's finches treat their feathers with a natural repellent*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5056383/ ; Knutie et al. (2014), *Darwin's finches combat introduced nest parasites with fumigated cotton*, https://collections.lib.utah.edu/dl_files/e2/e6/e2e681eb623a2f8914b473a19c1532693f058ead.pdf

## Scoring interpretation

Each supplied term was considered independently. A 5 indicates a directly supported host defense mechanism; 4 indicates a core component or strong physiological prediction; 3 indicates supporting repair, stress, metabolism, signaling or behavior; 2 indicates a weak or nonspecific connection; and 1 indicates no specific connection. Generic transcription, intracellular transport and cell-cycle functions are not automatically strong evidence of defense simply because immune or repair cells require them. Opposing regulators can both be relevant: controlling inflammation and maintaining energetic balance can promote tolerance as well as resistance. Antiviral and antibacterial processes receive lower relevance than directly applicable parasite defenses; secondary infection is plausible but not established by this review. Embryonic organ patterning, germline processes and unrelated radiation responses generally have no specific link.

Wound repair, epithelial restoration, vascular responses, oxidative balance and hypoxia responses are biological predictions from blood and tissue loss, not claims of measured molecular changes. Platelet-named terms are interpreted with avian thrombocyte-mediated hemostasis in mind. GO:0160240 was supplied with its identifier repeated instead of an informative name, and no specific physiological link could be established; it was retained and assigned 1. Scores measure potential relevance, not proof of adaptation, effectiveness, or association with observed selection.
