# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_021)

Scope: published ecological, physiological and behavioural literature on *P. downsi* and Galapagos land birds (Darwin's finches, Galapagos mockingbird, vermilion flycatcher). No genomic, transcriptomic or gene-expression studies of Galapagos birds were consulted or used.

## What the larvae do to the host

- Adult flies are free-living; eggs are laid in nests (sometimes directly on nestling nares). First and early second instars live as agents of myiasis inside the nasal cavities, ear canals and developing feather quills; late second and third instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to rasp the skin and feed on blood, serum and tissue (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/parasitology/article/abs/lifecycle-of-philornis-downsi-diptera-muscidae-parasitizing-darwins-finches-and-its-impacts-on-nestling-survival/597D469905598A7105BBE59990009B32; Fessl, Heimpel & Causton 2018, Annu. Rev. Entomol., https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/133.pdf; Lahuatte et al. 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/).
- Blood loss of 18-55% of nestling blood volume; reduced haemoglobin / anaemia; lower hemoglobin predicts nestling death (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006, Austral Ecology, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x).
- Open lesions, swelling and subcutaneous bleeding in ears, belly, wings and nares; secondary wound infections reported; reduced growth (mass, tarsus), lower body condition and ~55% mean in-nest mortality (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016, BMC Zoology, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40850-016-0003-9; IUCN GISD summary, https://www.iucngisd.org/gisd/species.php?sc=1400).
- Permanent naris and beak malformation (keratin, tissue and blood consumed inside the beak), persisting to adulthood and altering song, foraging and mate choice (Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009, https://academic.oup.com/biolinnean/article-abstract/98/3/577/2532299; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989416300336; Kleindorfer et al. 2019 Proc B, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6571467/).
- Brooding adults (especially females) are also bitten.

## Documented or proposed host defences

1. Adaptive (humoral) immunity: P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies are produced by adult finches of at least eight species; levels are higher in females, in nesting birds and experimentally higher in mothers of parasitised nests. Antibody response did not rescue nestling survival in finches; nestlings themselves show weak antibody responses (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0008605; Koop et al. 2013 Ecol. Evol., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/; Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology, https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/126.pdf).
2. Innate / inflammatory responses to skin wounding and myiasis (swelling, lesions, secondary infection): inferred from the pathology; a strong a priori expectation for ectoparasite feeding and wound infection.
3. Haematological compensation: anaemia from blood loss implies erythropoiesis, iron handling, hypoxia responses and haemostasis / coagulation as key physiological responses.
4. Tissue repair: wound healing, re-epithelialisation, keratinocyte and epidermal processes, extracellular matrix deposition and angiogenesis in damaged skin, nares and ear canals.
5. Tolerance via energy compensation: Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly because parasitised nestlings beg more and parents provision more; tolerance collapses in drought years; finches generally do not compensate (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019 Proc B, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/; O'Connor et al. 2014; Heyer et al. 2021; Green warbler-finch study https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188583). Nestling size and surface-to-volume ratio (thermal/energetic reserves overnight) proposed as a tolerance factor. This implicates growth, energy/glucose/lipid homeostasis, starvation and nutrient-sensing pathways.
6. Behavioural defences: nestling and female preening of larvae, nestlings standing on nestmates, longer female brooding associated with fewer parasites (Kleindorfer et al. 2021 Proc B, https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2021.1668), nest sanitation, and self-fumigation with permethrin-treated cotton (Knutie et al. 2014). Reported to have little protective effect. Nociception/itch sensing is a plausible proximate trigger of these behaviours.
7. Gut microbiota changes in parasitised mockingbird nestlings (Knutie et al. 2018 Ecosphere, https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ecs2.2286) - weak, indirect.

## Scoring logic applied

- 5: immune response, inflammatory response, adaptive/B-cell humoral immunity, wound healing and response to wounding, erythrocyte differentiation.
- 4: hemopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, coagulation/platelets, complement, innate immunity, cytokine production/signalling, keratinocyte/epidermis/skin, ECM and collagen organisation, T-cell activation.
- 3: supporting processes - chemotaxis, phagocytosis, NF-kB/TLR/JAK-STAT, bacterial defence (wound infection), angiogenesis and epithelial proliferation/migration, oxidative stress/ROS, energy and nutrient homeostasis, growth-factor (insulin/IGF/EGF/TGF-beta) signalling, stress responses, nociception, blood pressure regulation.
- 2: weak/indirect - general metabolism, apoptosis, MAPK, autophagy, thermogenesis, circadian, antiviral, bone/cartilage/keratin structures, cell migration/adhesion machinery.
- 1: no specific link - housekeeping (transcription, splicing, translation, cell cycle, DNA repair, cilia, neurons/synapses, organ development, gametogenesis).
