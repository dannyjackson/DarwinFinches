# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_014)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adults are free-living; larvae are obligate semi-haematophagous parasites of nestlings (and brooding females). First-instar larvae feed inside the nares and developing feather quills (myiasis), consuming keratin, blood and tissue; later instars live in the nest base and feed externally at night on blood and tissue (Fessl et al. 2006, Parasitology; McNew & Clayton 2018, Annu. Rev. Entomol.).
- Blood loss of ~18-55% of nestling blood volume; measurable anaemia (reduced haemoglobin/haematocrit), reduced growth and mass, ~55% average in-nest mortality (up to 100% in some years) (Dudaniec et al. 2006; Fessl et al. 2006; Koop et al. 2011; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Knutie et al. 2016).
- Tissue damage: skin wounds, naris enlargement/beak malformation persisting into adulthood (Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016), with downstream effects on foraging and song (Common et al.; Kleindorfer et al. 2019).
- Secondary effects: changes in nestling gut microbiota (Knutie et al. 2018); hypotheses of oxidative stress and telomere shortening as welfare indicators (Tebbich, Univ. Vienna project).

## Documented or proposed host defences
1. Resistance via immunity: P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies demonstrated in medium ground finches; adult females at parasitized nests mount higher antibody levels, nestlings do not; antibody response did not rescue fitness (Huber et al. 2010; Koop et al. 2013; Knutie et al. 2016). Inflammatory/innate responses to ectoparasite saliva and feeding wounds are the standard a priori expectation (Wikel 1996 cited therein).
2. Tolerance via energy compensation: Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly through increased begging and parental provisioning; finches do not. Tolerance tracks rainfall/food and body size (surface-area/heat retention during nocturnal feeding) (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019). Predicts glucose/energy homeostasis, lipid mobilisation, growth (IGF/insulin) and thermogenesis pathways.
3. Haematological compensation: erythropoiesis, iron handling, hypoxia response and haemostasis/coagulation against blood-feeding larvae (Dudaniec et al. 2006; Knutie et al. 2016).
4. Tissue repair: wound healing, re-epithelialisation, keratinocyte/skin differentiation, angiogenesis, ECM remodelling, and beak/naris tissue (keratin) repair.
5. Stress physiology: corticosterone/HPA axis tested experimentally (Knutie et al. 2013, Gen. Comp. Endocrinol.); glucocorticoid and steroid hormone signalling scored as supporting.
6. Behavioural defences: nestling preening of larvae and standing on nest mates (little fitness benefit), parental leaf-preening with Psidium galapageium repellent leaves, nest sanitation; begging behaviour (Koop et al. 2013; Cimadom et al. 2016; Bush & Clayton 2018 review). Nociception, learning/cognition and circadian processes received weak-to-moderate scores.
