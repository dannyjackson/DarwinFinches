# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_024)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- P. downsi adults are free-living; larvae are obligate parasites of nestling (and brooding adult female) land birds. First/early second instars live as myiasis agents inside the nares and other tissues (beak keratin, soft tissue, feather quills, ear canal); older second and third instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to feed externally on blood (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/parasitology/article/abs/lifecycle-of-philornis-downsi-diptera-muscidae-parasitizing-darwins-finches-and-its-impacts-on-nestling-survival/597D469905598A7105BBE59990009B32).
- Consequences: blood loss of 18-55%, anaemia and reduced haemoglobin (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006, Austral Ecology, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2006.01553.x; Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology), skin lesions/wounds on abdomen, nares and ear (Koop et al. 2011 PLoS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0019706), reduced growth/mass, ~55% mean in-nest mortality (Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 BMC Zoology review, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40850-016-0003-9), and permanent naris/beak malformation in survivors that alters song and mating signals (Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer et al. 2019 Proc B, https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rspb/article/286/1904/20190461/85119/Introduced-parasite-changes-host-phenotype-mating).
- Pathogenesis of philornid myiasis in other hosts: larvae penetrate integument, elicit tissue damage and an energy drain on nestling growth (Uhazy & Arendt 1986, J Wildl Dis, https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/61438). Overview of genus biology: McNew & Clayton 2018 Annu Rev Entomol "Alien invasion" (https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/133.pdf).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. Acquired (antibody-mediated) immunity: P. downsi-binding antibodies are detectable in medium ground finches, higher in breeding females (which brood and are also fed upon) and higher at parasitised nests; higher maternal antibody levels associated with fewer parasites (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0008605; Koop et al. 2013 Ecol Evol, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3930052/). Nestlings, in contrast, showed weak/no antibody response. This supports scoring adaptive immunity, B-cell processes, immune response and their regulators highly.
2. Innate/inflammatory and wound responses: feeding by hematophagous larvae produces open lesions; general ectoparasite immunology (inflammation, complement, coagulation/platelets against anticoagulant saliva, heterophil/leukocyte recruitment, cytokine and chemokine production, wound healing and re-epithelialisation of skin and nares) are strong a priori predictions although not specifically measured in Galapagos birds.
3. Tolerance via energy compensation: Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate P. downsi (no fitness loss despite similar parasite density) apparently via increased begging and parental provisioning that offsets blood/energy loss; finches do not compensate (Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology, https://darwin.biology.utah.edu/PDFs/126.pdf; McNew et al. 2019 Proc B "Annual environmental variation influences host tolerance", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408884/). Body size/surface-area to volume ratio and nightly thermal/energy balance proposed as a tolerance determinant. Supports moderate-to-high scores for energy homeostasis, glucose/insulin/IGF signalling, starvation and nutrient responses, lipid storage, thermogenesis, growth, and haematopoietic/erythropoietic recovery from anaemia (iron homeostasis, hypoxia response).
4. Behavioural defences: nestling preening and consumption of larvae, flapping, standing on nestmates; parental pecking at nest material/nestling nares; begging changes (O'Connor et al. 2014; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Pike et al. 2021 J Insect Behav, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8813692/). These justify low-moderate scores for nociception, learning/memory/cognition, neuropeptide and circadian processes.
5. Secondary effects: gut microbiota of parasitised nestlings is altered (Knutie et al. 2018 Ecosphere, https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ecs2.2286); open wounds plausibly invite bacterial infection, so antibacterial defence terms were given a weak-moderate score. Antiviral-specific terms were scored 1 (avian pox is a separate parasite).

## Scoring logic summary
- 5: wound healing/response to wounding, erythrocyte differentiation, B cell activation, adaptive immune response, immune response.
- 4: complement, coagulation/platelet activation, haemopoiesis, iron homeostasis, hypoxia response, energy homeostasis, inflammatory response and its (positive) regulation, innate immune response, cytokine-mediated signalling, defence response, immune-system regulation terms, B cell differentiation/BCR signalling/proliferation.
- 3: supporting processes (chemotaxis, phagocytosis, T cell terms, NF-kB/JAK-STAT, specific pro-inflammatory cytokines, skin/epidermis/keratinocyte, ECM/collagen, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, insulin/IGF/starvation/nutrient responses, gluconeogenesis, lipid storage, steroid hormone signalling, pain perception, response to stress).
- 2: generic growth/proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, MAPK, lipid/glucose metabolism, behaviour-related neural terms, xenobiotic responses, barrier/junction terms, antibacterial (Gram-negative) and LPS responses.
- 1: housekeeping, development of unrelated organs, neural wiring, antiviral terms, etc.
