# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_001)

Scope: published ecological, physiological and behavioural literature on the avian vampire fly
(Philornis downsi) and Galapagos land birds (Darwin's finches, Galapagos mockingbird, flycatchers).
No transcriptomic/genomic studies of Galapagos birds were consulted or used. Full-text fetching was
blocked in this environment, so the review rests on abstracts and indexed excerpts of the papers below.

## What the larvae do to hosts

- Adult flies are free-living; larvae are obligate, semi-hematophagous parasites of nestlings (and
  occasionally brooding females). First (and early second) instars live inside the nares/nasal
  cavities and other tissues as agents of myiasis, consuming keratin, blood and tissue; later instars
  live in the nest base and emerge mainly at night to feed externally on blood and tissue
  (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology; O'Connor et al. 2010; Common et al. 2019).
- Documented host damage: blood loss of 18-55% of nestling blood volume, reduced haemoglobin and
  haematocrit (anaemia), multiple skin wounds/lesions and secondary infections, grossly enlarged and
  deformed nares and beak (persisting to adulthood, with effects on song, foraging and mate choice),
  reduced growth rate and body mass, and in-nest mortality averaging ~55% and reaching 100%
  (Fessl & Tebbich 2002; Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006; Fessl et al. 2006; Huber 2008;
  Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Koop et al. 2011; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Kleindorfer et al. 2019;
  Kleindorfer et al. 2022; reviews: Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; McNew & Clayton 2018; Fessl et al. 2018).
- Mortality is attributed to anaemia/hypovolaemia and tissue damage, with nocturnal blood loss leaving
  small nestlings energetically depleted and unable to beg the next morning (Knutie et al. 2016;
  McNew et al. 2019).

## Host defences documented or proposed

1. Immune (resistance): P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies are induced in medium ground finch mothers
   at parasitized nests (Koop et al. 2013) and in mockingbirds (Knutie et al. 2016), i.e. an
   antigen-specific humoral (B cell) response; nestlings mount weaker responses. Generic
   anti-ectoparasite immunity against blood-feeding dipteran larvae involves local inflammation,
   cytokine/chemokine production, leukocyte recruitment, phagocytes, complement and T-cell help,
   so these are strong a priori predictions. Wounds and skin lesions also raise the risk of
   secondary bacterial infection (Fessl et al. 2006; GISD account), implicating innate antibacterial
   defence.
2. Tolerance (compensation): Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the same parasite density that kills
   finches; parasitized mockingbird nestlings beg more and parents provision more, compensating for
   blood/energy loss (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology). Tolerance is labile and fails in drought years when
   food is scarce (McNew et al. 2019, Proc B). Finches generally do not increase provisioning
   (Knutie et al. 2016; Heyer et al. 2023 green warbler-finch), although begging and food provisioning
   have been investigated in small ground finches (O'Connor et al. 2014). Larger body size and lower
   surface-area:volume ratio are proposed to help nestlings withstand nocturnal blood loss. Physiological
   tolerance therefore implicates energy homeostasis, glucose/lipid metabolism and starvation
   responses, erythropoiesis/haemoglobin replacement, iron homeostasis, hypoxia responses,
   haemostasis, wound healing and re-epithelialisation of skin and nares, and growth (IGF/insulin) under
   nutrient limitation.
3. Behavioural: preening of larvae by parents, nest sanitation, and incorporation of
   insecticide-treated material into nests ("self-fumigation") have been reported or exploited for
   management; nestling behavioural type predicts naris deformation (Katsis et al. 2021).
4. Stress physiology: parasitism did not change corticosterone of breeding females
   (Knutie et al. 2013), but HPA/glucocorticoid signalling remains a plausible mediator of nestling
   begging and energy allocation.

## How this translated into scoring

- 5: wound healing/response to wounding; inflammatory, innate, adaptive and general immune response
  and their regulation; B-cell activation/differentiation/proliferation/BCR signalling (IgY response);
  haemopoiesis and erythrocyte differentiation (replacement of lost blood).
- 4: cytokine/TNF/IL-1/IL-6/IL-8/IL-12/IL-17/IFN-gamma/chemokine production and signalling; NF-kB,
  TLR, inflammasome, complement, phagocytosis, respiratory burst; T-cell activation; leukocyte
  adhesion and cell chemotaxis; defence against bacteria/LPS; skin/epidermis development and
  keratinocyte differentiation (damaged skin and nares); extracellular matrix organisation;
  response to hypoxia; iron homeostasis; blood coagulation; energy homeostasis; response to stress.
- 3: supporting processes: angiogenesis/vascular development, collagen/basement membrane, epithelial
  proliferation and migration, fibroblast proliferation, TGF-beta/EGF/JAK-STAT/p38 signalling,
  platelet activation, oxidative stress and ROS handling, glucose/insulin/IGF signalling,
  gluconeogenesis, starvation and nutrient responses, fatty-acid oxidation, thermogenesis
  (nocturnal energy balance), hormone/steroid receptor signalling, nociception, craniofacial
  (palate) development, intermediate filaments (keratin), membrane repair.
- 2: generic cell proliferation/migration/adhesion, apoptosis regulation, MAPK/ERK/JNK, lipid and
  carbohydrate metabolism, bone/cartilage, circadian rhythm, antiviral responses, autophagy, and
  similar processes with only indirect links.
- 1: housekeeping (transcription, RNA processing, translation, DNA repair, cell cycle, cilia,
  gametogenesis, neuronal/synaptic function, organogenesis of heart, kidney, eye, etc.).
