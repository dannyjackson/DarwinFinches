# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_022)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adults are free-living; larvae are obligate parasites of nestlings (and brooding females). First/early second instars live inside the nares and other soft tissues (myiasis), consuming keratin, blood and tissue inside the beak; older second and third instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to feed externally on nestling blood (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology; Common et al. 2019 IntechOpen chapter).
- Documented consequences: blood loss estimated at 18-55% of nestling blood volume, reduced haemoglobin and anaemia with elevated reticulocyte counts (regenerative erythropoiesis), multiple body wounds and secondary infections, reduced growth rate and body mass, enlarged/deformed nares and beak that persist into adulthood, ~50% (up to 100%) nestling mortality, altered adult song and foraging in survivors (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006; Fessl et al. 2006; Huber 2008; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Koop et al. 2011; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Kleindorfer et al. 2019; Katsis et al. 2021).
- Larvae aggregate on one nestling per brood at night ("tasty chick"), which begs weakly the next day and dies first (O'Connor et al. 2014).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. Haemostasis / blood-volume maintenance and erythropoietic recovery (anaemia, reticulocytosis; hypoxia and hypovolaemia predicted) - Dudaniec et al. 2006; Fessl et al. 2006.
2. Tissue-damage response: wound healing, re-epithelialisation of skin and nasal/beak keratinised epithelium, inflammation, control of secondary bacterial infection at wound sites - Fessl et al. 2006; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review.
3. Antibody-mediated (IgY) immune response: P. downsi-binding antibodies in at least eight finch species; higher in brooding females and in mothers at parasitised nests; nestlings show weak/undetectable response; the response did not rescue fledging success (Huber et al. 2010, PLoS ONE; Koop et al. 2013, Ecol Evol; NSF award summary). General nest-fly literature indicates inflammation/oedema and granulocyte effectors as mechanisms by which antibodies impair larval feeding (Grab et al. 2019).
4. Tolerance via energy compensation: Galapagos mockingbirds beg more and parents provision more, maintaining fledging success at equal parasite density; finches do not; tolerance collapses in drought; nestling blood glucose and haemoglobin used as tolerance markers; larger nestling body size / lower surface:volume proposed to buffer nocturnal blood loss (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology; McNew et al. 2019, Proc B; Knutie et al. 2018, Ecosphere).
5. Behavioural defences: preening and nest-standing by parents; longer female brooding reduces larval numbers; nestling activity/behavioural type predicts naris damage; self-anointing with Psidium galapageium leaves whose volatiles repel P. downsi; use of insecticide-treated cotton/dispenser material (Koop et al. 2013; Kleindorfer et al. 2021, Proc B; Katsis et al. 2021; Cimadom et al. 2016, Sci Rep; Tebbich et al. 2021, Ethology).
6. Nutritional/stress physiology and gut microbiota shifts in parasitised nestlings (Knutie et al. 2018).
