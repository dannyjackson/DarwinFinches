# Run 018 - Literature review: host responses of Galapagos land birds to Philornis downsi

## What the larvae do to hosts
- P. downsi adults are free-living; larvae are obligate, semi-haematophagous parasites that live in the nest base and feed (mostly at night) on the blood and tissue of nestlings and, to a lesser degree, brooding females. First-instar larvae feed inside the nares and in developing feather quills; later instars feed externally on skin (abdomen, ear canal, nares), leaving open wounds (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology; O'Connor et al. 2010 Oryx video study; McNew & Clayton 2018 Annu Rev Entomol).
- Consequences for nestlings: blood loss of 18-55% of blood volume, anaemia / reduced haemoglobin and haematocrit, reduced growth and body mass, naris and beak malformation that persists to adulthood, infected body wounds, and in-nest mortality averaging ~55% (range 20-100%) (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006 Austral Ecol; Fessl, Kleindorfer & Tebbich 2006 Biol Cons; Huber 2008; Koop et al. 2011 PLoS ONE; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Katsis et al. 2021; Kleindorfer et al. 2021 Proc B).
- Adult effects: brooding females are also fed upon; a corticosterone (HPA-axis) study measured condition, haematocrit and glucocorticoids in breeding G. fortis in relation to experimental fly removal (Knutie et al. 2015, PMC4383185). Surviving adults with malformed nares show altered song and foraging (Kleindorfer et al. 2019, 2022).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. Adaptive (antibody) immunity - documented. P. downsi-binding antibodies in medium ground finches and at least eight finch species; higher in females (who brood) and in parents at parasitized nests; experimentally induced by parasitism; mockingbirds also mount antibody responses. The response did not rescue nestling survival in finches (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE; Koop et al. 2013 Ecol Evol; Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology).
2. Innate immune / inflammatory response and wound defence - inferred from the lesions. Larval feeding produces open, often infected wounds and naris tissue loss; a host response necessarily involves inflammation, leukocyte recruitment, phagocytosis, cytokine signalling, antibacterial defence against secondary infection, haemostasis and wound healing / re-epithelialisation (skin, keratinocytes, dermal matrix). These are the strongest a priori physiological predictions.
3. Haematological compensation (tolerance to blood loss) - anaemia and low haemoglobin are the best-documented physiological cost; predicted compensatory mechanisms are erythropoiesis, haemopoiesis, iron handling, hypoxia response and oxygen-carrying capacity.
4. Energetic / growth tolerance - Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly by increased nestling begging and parental provisioning, which fails in drought years; finches do not compensate. Tolerance therefore involves energy balance, nutrient sensing, growth, thermoregulation (small nestlings lose heat and blood overnight) (Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology; McNew et al. 2019 Proc B).
5. Stress endocrinology - HPA / glucocorticoid response to parasitism in breeding adults (Knutie et al. 2015).
6. Behavioural defences - female in-nest attendance predicts fewer parasites; females probe nest bases, preen nestlings and consume larvae; nestlings self-preen, reposition or stand on siblings; nestling activity level predicts naris deformation; nest building with insecticide-treated material (self-fumigation). These predict nervous-system / sensory (e.g. nociception) processes only weakly at the GO level (Kleindorfer et al. 2021 Proc B; Katsis et al. 2021 Biol J Linn Soc; O'Connor et al. 2010; Knutie et al. 2014; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 BMC Zoology review).
7. Beak / naris tissue - larvae consume keratin, blood and tissue inside the beak; regrowth or resistance of nasal/beak epithelium and keratin is a plausible supporting process; developmental beak-shape pathways are only speculatively linked.
