# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_028)

## What the larvae do to the host
- P. downsi adults are free-living; larvae are obligate semi-hematophagous nest parasites. First-instar larvae enter the nares (and sometimes ear canals / other body cavities) of nestlings and feed internally on nasal tissue; second and third instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to feed externally on blood through the skin of nestlings and brooding females (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology; O'Connor et al. 2010).
- Documented pathology: blood loss of 18-55% of nestling blood volume, reduced haemoglobin and raised proportions of immature erythrocytes (anaemia with compensatory erythropoiesis), open wounds and tissue damage, permanent naris/beak malformation in survivors, reduced growth and body mass, in-nest mortality averaging ~55% and reaching 100% in some years/species (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006; Fessl et al. 2006; Huber 2008; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Koop et al. 2011; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review; McNew & Clayton 2018 review).
- Naris deformation persists into adulthood and alters song and foraging (Kleindorfer et al. 2019; Kleindorfer et al. 2022), so craniofacial tissue repair/morphogenesis during the nestling period is directly relevant.
- Secondary consequences: elevated corticosterone in brooding females (Knutie et al. 2013), altered gut microbiota (Knutie et al. 2018), reduced future reproduction in mockingbirds (McNew et al. 2020), and candidate welfare indicators under study (haematocrit, baseline corticosterone, oxidative stress, telomere length, breathing rate, sleep; Tebbich project summary).
- In related subcutaneous Philornis species larvae burrow between dermis and musculature, causing myiasis with an inflammatory capsule and a large energetic drain (Uhazy & Arendt 1986).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. Resistance (immunological): Darwin's finches and mockingbirds mount a P. downsi-binding IgY antibody response; mothers at parasitized nests have higher antibody levels, but this did not rescue nestling survival in finches (Koop et al. 2013; Knutie et al. 2016; Knutie et al. 2018). General ectoparasite immunology predicts cutaneous inflammation, mast cell/basophil/eosinophil recruitment, Th2-skewed cytokine signalling, complement and antibody production at bite sites (Wikel 1999; Fontaine et al. 2011).
2. Tolerance (physiological/behavioural): Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly; parasitized nestlings beg more and parents provision more, compensating energetically for blood loss; tolerance declines in dry years. Larger body size / lower surface-to-volume ratio is proposed to aid tolerance. Finches do not show this compensation (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019; O'Connor et al. 2013). Tolerance mechanisms proposed in the literature include tissue repair, replacement of lost blood (erythropoiesis), and energy/nutrient allocation.
3. Haematological compensation: increased immature erythrocyte fraction in parasitized nestlings implies upregulated erythropoiesis; haemoglobin is the standard condition metric (Dudaniec et al. 2006; Minias 2015 review).
4. Endocrine stress response: corticosterone elevated in parasitized females; glucocorticoid signalling modulates begging, energy mobilisation and immunity (Knutie et al. 2013).
5. Behavioural defences: nestlings preen off larvae and stand on nest-mates (limited efficacy); nestling behavioural type predicts naris damage (Katsis et al. 2021); adults anoint feathers with Psidium galapageium leaves whose volatiles repel P. downsi (Cimadom et al. 2016; Tebbich et al. 2021); finches incorporate permethrin-treated cotton into nests (Knutie et al. 2014). Behavioural plasticity/innovation and nociception are therefore weakly relevant.
6. Wound-related secondary infection (bacterial) and skin barrier integrity are plausible supporting processes given open lesions.

## How this maps to GO scoring
- 5: antibody/B-cell and general immune/inflammatory response terms; wound healing and response to wounding; erythropoiesis/haemopoiesis; defence response.
- 4: innate/adaptive components (cytokine production, TLR/NF-kB/JAK-STAT, complement, T cell activation, leukocyte adhesion, chemotaxis, phagocytosis), haemostasis (coagulation, platelet activation), hypoxia response, glucocorticoid/steroid hormone signalling, oxidative-stress response, skin/epidermis development and keratinocyte differentiation, iron homeostasis, energy/glucose homeostasis and response to nutrient levels/starvation (tolerance via energetic compensation), ECM/collagen organisation (tissue repair), regulation of cell growth / body size.
- 3: downstream supporting processes: angiogenesis, epithelial proliferation/migration, cell adhesion, apoptosis regulation, craniofacial/skeletal/cartilage/bone development (naris and beak), lipid/fatty acid metabolism, insulin/IGF/TOR signalling, mitochondrial respiration, circadian rhythm and sleep, pain perception, defence against bacteria, telomere maintenance, cellular senescence, general stress response.
- 2: generic signalling, development, neural, cytoskeletal and transport terms with only speculative links.
- 1: housekeeping processes (transcription, splicing, translation, DNA repair, cilium assembly, spermatogenesis, cardiac morphogenesis etc.) with no specific link.
