# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_020)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies are free-living; eggs are laid in nests (or in nestling nares). First-instar larvae live and feed inside the nares, ear openings and developing feather quills, consuming keratin, blood and tissue; second/third instars hide in the nest base by day and feed semi-hematophagously on nestlings (and brooding females) at night (Fessl et al. 2006 Parasitology; Fessl et al. 2018 chapter; McNew & Clayton 2018 Annu Rev Entomol).
- Documented costs: nestling blood loss of roughly 18-55%, reduced haemoglobin/anaemia, multiple body wounds and secondary infections, grossly deformed/enlarged nares and beak malformation that persists to adulthood, reduced growth rate and body mass, and nestling mortality that averages ~50% and reaches 100% in some years (Dudaniec et al. 2006 Austral Ecol; Fessl et al. 2006 Biol Conserv; Huber 2008; Koop et al. 2011 PLoS ONE; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; O'Connor et al. 2010; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 BMC Zool review).
- Naris deformation alters adult beak function, foraging and song (Kleindorfer et al. 2019 Proc R Soc B; Kleindorfer et al. 2022 Funct Ecol).

## Host defenses documented or proposed
1. Antibody-mediated (adaptive/humoral) immunity: P. downsi-binding IgY antibodies detected in at least eight finch species; higher in females (which brood) and experimentally elevated in mothers at parasitised vs fumigated nests, though without rescuing nestling survival; mockingbirds and finches showed no significant antibody response in a later comparison (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE; Koop et al. 2013 Ecol Evol; Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology).
2. Tolerance via energy compensation: Galapagos mockingbird nestlings beg more and parents provision more, maintaining fledging success and body mass despite similar parasite densities; finches generally do not, and parasitised finch begging does not honestly signal need; tolerance varies among years with food availability (Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology; McNew et al. 2019 Proc R Soc B; O'Connor et al. 2014; Grab et al. 2019). Larger body size / lower surface:volume thought to aid tolerance. Tolerance mechanisms explicitly proposed: repairing damaged tissue and replacing lost red blood cells (erythropoiesis), fuelled by energy intake.
3. Haematological recovery: fumigated nestlings have 14-40% higher haemoglobin; recovery of lost blood implies erythropoiesis, iron handling, haemostasis/coagulation at wounds, and responses to hypoxia.
4. Wound repair and epidermal/keratin tissue regeneration (nares, skin, feather follicles), with risk of bacterial infection of wounds.
5. Stress physiology: corticosterone of breeding adults tested experimentally (Knutie et al. 2013 Gen Comp Endocrinol); glucocorticoid and oxidative-stress costs of nest ectoparasites are well documented in other systems (Protocalliphora, mites: Lopez-Arrabe et al. 2015; Pryor & Casto 2015).
6. Behavioural defences: preening/removal of larvae (occasional, not widespread), females reducing time brooding, nestling positioning behaviour, self-fumigation with permethrin-treated cotton, nest-material choice (Koop et al. 2013; Knutie et al. 2014; Katsis et al. 2021; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016). Larvae are nocturnal and cryptic, limiting detection; nociception/itch would be the sensory trigger for removal behaviour.

## How this drove scoring
- 5: humoral/adaptive immunity (B cells, antibody), immune/defense response umbrella terms, erythrocyte differentiation, wound healing, response to wounding.
- 4: innate/inflammatory signalling (cytokines, TLR, NF-kB, complement, phagocytosis, T-cell help), haemopoiesis, coagulation/thrombocyte activation, hypoxia response, iron homeostasis, energy homeostasis, skin/epidermis/keratinocyte and ECM/collagen repair, defense against bacteria (wound infection).
- 3: supporting processes: angiogenesis/vascular repair, epithelial proliferation/migration, glucose/lipid/insulin metabolism and nutrient-sensing (tolerance), oxidative stress and ROS, general stress response, steroid-hormone signalling, chemotaxis, JAK-STAT, thermogenesis, nociception, blood-pressure regulation.
- 2: weak links (apoptosis, autophagy, growth, circadian, cognition/learning, xenobiotic response, beak skeletal development, viral/IFN, cell adhesion/migration).
- 1: no specific link (housekeeping, neurodevelopment, cell cycle, RNA processing, etc.).
