# Literature review: host responses to Philornis downsi (run_006)

## What the larvae do to the host
- Adult flies are free-living; females oviposit in nests. First- and early second-instar
  larvae live as agents of myiasis inside nestling nares (also ear openings, feather quills),
  consuming keratin, blood and tissue; later second- and third-instar larvae live in the nest
  base and feed nocturnally and externally on nestling (and incubating female) blood
  (Fessl, Sinclair & Kleindorfer 2006, Parasitology; Fessl et al. 2018 book chapter).
- Blood loss of 18-55% of nestling blood volume; reduced haemoglobin / anaemia
  (Dudaniec, Kleindorfer & Fessl 2006, Austral Ecology); skin lesions, wounds; naris and
  beak malformation that persists to adulthood (Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer &
  Sulloway 2016); reduced growth and mass, ~50-100% nestling mortality (Koop et al. 2011
  PLOS ONE; O'Connor et al. 2010; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review).
- Histopathology of philornid myiasis in other hosts: larvae embedded between dermis and
  muscle, macrophage/plasma-cell infiltrates, vascular congestion, and host responses that
  drain the nestling energy budget (Uhazy & Arendt 1986, J. Wildl. Dis.).
- Downstream effects: altered song (naris deformation) and hybridization risk
  (Kleindorfer et al. 2019 Proc B).

## Documented or proposed host defences
1. Immune resistance: P. downsi-binding antibody (IgY) responses in mothers and nestlings
   across at least eight finch species; maternal antibody levels higher at parasitised nests,
   but did not rescue fledging success (Huber et al. 2010; Koop, Owen, Knutie, Aguilar &
   Clayton 2013, Ecol. Evol.). Inflammatory/innate responses inferred from myiasis
   histopathology. Galapagos birds otherwise appear to lack effective resistance
   (McNew et al. 2019 Proc B).
2. Tolerance: Galapagos mockingbirds tolerate the fly by increased nestling begging and
   parental provisioning (energy compensation); tolerance collapses in drought years
   (Knutie et al. 2016, Ecology; McNew et al. 2019). Finch nestlings do not beg more and
   parents do not compensate (Knutie et al. 2016; O'Connor et al. 2014).
   This implies energy/nutrient metabolism, growth regulation, haematopoietic recovery
   (erythropoiesis, iron handling), hypoxia responses, haemostasis and wound repair as the
   physiological substrate of tolerance.
3. Behavioural defences: preening with leaves of Psidium galapageium whose volatiles repel
   P. downsi (Cimadom et al. 2016, Sci. Rep.; Tebbich et al. 2021, Ethology); parental and
   nestling preening/removal of larvae, nestling activity predicting naris damage
   (Katsis et al. 2021; Lahuatte et al. 2022); use of insecticide-treated cotton in nests
   (Knutie et al. 2014).

## Scoring logic applied
- 5: immune response (innate, adaptive, B-cell/antibody), inflammatory response, wound
  healing/response to wounding, defense response, erythrocyte differentiation.
- 4: core components of these (cytokine production/signalling, NF-kB, TLR, T-cell help,
  complement, chemokines, IL-6/TNF/IL-1/IFN-gamma/IL-17 signalling, leukocyte adhesion),
  haemostasis (coagulation, platelet activation), hypoxia response, iron homeostasis,
  haematopoiesis, skin/epidermis/keratinocyte biology, ECM/collagen repair, energy
  homeostasis (tolerance).
- 3: supporting processes: angiogenesis, chemotaxis, phagocytosis, oxidative stress,
  ROS, MAPK/JNK/JAK-STAT, TGF-beta, glucose/lipid/fatty-acid metabolism and starvation
  responses, hormone (glucocorticoid) responses, insulin/IGF growth signalling,
  cartilage/ossification (beak/naris), blood pressure, nociception.
- 2: weak/indirect (general proliferation, adhesion, apoptosis, autophagy, broad
  signalling pathways, circadian, zinc, memory/learning).
- 1: no specific link (housekeeping, neural wiring, cilia, cell cycle, RNA processing, etc.).
