# Run 015 – Literature review: host responses to *Philornis downsi* (a priori basis for GO scoring)

## What the larvae do to hosts
- Adult flies are non-parasitic; eggs are laid in nests (or in nestling nares). First-instar larvae feed inside the nares and ear canals of nestlings (consuming keratin, blood and tissue); 2nd/3rd instars live in the nest base and emerge at night to pierce the skin and feed semi-haematophagously on nestlings and brooding females (Fessl et al. 2006, Parasitology; O'Connor et al. 2010, Oryx video analysis; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016 review).
- Documented pathology: 18–55 % overnight blood loss, ~28 % lower haemoglobin, elevated immature red blood cell counts (anaemia with regenerative response), reduced mass gain and feather growth, skin lesions/wounds on abdomen and head, naris and beak malformation that persists into adulthood, ear-canal damage, ~55 % mean in-nest mortality (Dudaniec et al. 2006; Fessl et al. 2006; Koop et al. 2011; Huber 2008; Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Kleindorfer et al. 2019).
- In other *Philornis* species, histopathology of myiasis shows macrophage/plasma-cell infiltration, vascular congestion and an energetic cost of the host response (Uhazy & Arendt 1986).

## Host defences documented or proposed
1. **Immune resistance (humoral/adaptive):** *P. downsi*-binding IgY antibodies in adult medium ground finches, higher in brooding females and experimentally induced by parasitism; nestlings also assayed; at least eight finch species show antibody responses; mockingbirds have antibodies too (Huber et al. 2010 PLoS ONE; Koop et al. 2013 Ecol Evol; Knutie et al. 2016 Ecology; Knutie et al. 2018 Ecosphere). Local inflammation at feeding sites is expected (ectoparasite myiasis).
2. **Tolerance / energetic compensation:** mockingbird nestlings beg more, parents provision more, nestlings fledge despite blood loss; finch parents generally do not compensate; tolerance collapses in drought (Knutie et al. 2016; McNew et al. 2019 Proc B; O'Connor et al. 2014; Heyer et al. 2023 J Ornithol). Implies metabolic/energy homeostasis, growth maintenance and nutrient-sensing processes.
3. **Haematological compensation:** regenerative erythropoiesis (immature RBCs), haemoglobin maintenance, iron handling and hypoxia responses to anaemia (Dudaniec et al. 2006; Knutie et al. 2016).
4. **Tissue repair:** wound healing, haemostasis/coagulation, re-epithelialisation and keratin/skin repair of lesions and nares; beak/naris morphology changes (Galligan & Kleindorfer 2009; Kleindorfer & Sulloway 2016; Koop et al. 2011 lesion photos).
5. **Behavioural defences:** nestling preening and eating of larvae, female nest probing and standing erect, altered brooding; anointing feathers with *Psidium galapageium* leaves that repel adult flies (O'Connor et al. 2010; Kleindorfer & Dudaniec 2016; Cimadom et al. 2016 Sci Rep; Tebbich et al. 2021). Nestling behavioural reactivity predicts naris deformation. Physiological correlates: nociception/itch detection, stress-hormone signalling, nocturnal (circadian) feeding timing.

## Scoring logic
5 = wound healing/response to wounding, erythrocyte differentiation, adaptive immune response, inflammatory response. 4 = core immune (innate/B/T cell activation, cytokine regulation, NF-kB, complement), haemostasis (coagulation, platelet activation), hemopoiesis/iron/hypoxia, ECM/collagen/skin/epidermis/keratinocyte repair. 3 = supporting processes (cytokine/TLR/TNF/IL signalling, chemotaxis, phagocytosis, ROS, oxidative stress, energy/nutrient/starvation responses, insulin/IGF growth signalling, angiogenesis, epithelial proliferation/migration, pain perception, blood pressure, bacterial defence of wounds). 2 = weak/indirect (general metabolism, adhesion, bone/cartilage/beak development, circadian, learning/memory, apoptosis, growth-factor pathways). 1 = no specific link.
